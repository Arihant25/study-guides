0.2 Notation

The guide uses the professor's notation, which is Kay's notation. Where Poor's book uses another symbol, the table says so.

Symbol Meaning
\(\bx = [x[0],\dots,x[N-1]]^T\) The data record. \(N\) samples, stacked as a column vector.
\(\Hz,\ \Ho\) The two hypotheses. In radar, \(\Hz\) is "noise only" and \(\Ho\) is "signal present".
\(p(\bx;\Hz),\ p(\bx;\Ho)\) The PDF (or PMF) of the data under each hypothesis. The slides also write \(f_0(x), f_1(x)\). In the Bayesian setting we write \(p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i)\), because the hypothesis is then a random event.
\(L(\bx)\) The likelihood ratio \(p(\bx;\Ho)/p(\bx;\Hz)\). Always \(\Ho\) on top.
\(\gamma\) The threshold on \(L(\bx)\). In the NP theorem the slides call it \(\eta\).
\(T(\bx),\ \gamma'\) The simplified test statistic and its threshold.
\(R_0,\ R_1\) Decision regions. \(\bx\in R_1\) means "decide \(\Ho\)". \(R_1\) is the critical region.
\(\delta(\bx)\) A decision rule, written as the probability of deciding \(\Ho\) given \(\bx\). For a non-randomised rule \(\delta(\bx)\in\{0,1\}\).
\(\PFA,\ \PD,\ P_M\) False-alarm, detection and miss probabilities. \(P_M = 1-\PD\).
\(\alpha,\ \beta\) Statisticians' names: \(\alpha = \PFA\) (size, significance level, Type I error) and \(\beta = P_M\) (Type II error). \(1-\beta = \PD\) is the power. Careful: in Poor's sequential chapter, \(\beta\) means \(\PD\).
\(P_e\) Average probability of error, \(P(\Hz)\PFA + P(\Ho)P_M\).
\(C_{ij}\) Cost of deciding \(\mathcal{H}_i\) when \(\mathcal{H}_j\) is true. First index = decision, second index = truth.
\(\mathcal{R}\) Bayes risk, the average cost.
\(d^2\) Deflection coefficient, \((E[T;\Ho]-E[T;\Hz])^2/\mathrm{var}(T;\Hz)\).
\(\E\) Signal energy, \(\sum_n s^2[n] = \bs^T\bs\).
\(Q(x)\) Right-tail probability of \(\Nc(0,1)\).
\(Q_{\chi^2_N}(x)\) Right-tail probability of a chi-squared variable with \(N\) degrees of freedom.

0.3 The one recipe

Lecture 3 ends with a flow chart. The professor said every derivation from then on would follow it. It is worth learning as a fixed sequence, because almost every exam question is an instance of it.

  1. Write \(p(\bx;\Hz)\) and \(p(\bx;\Ho)\). For i.i.d. samples this is a product of \(N\) one-sample densities.
  2. Form \(L(\bx) = p(\bx;\Ho)/p(\bx;\Hz)\) and take the logarithm.
  3. Cancel every term that does not depend on \(\bx\). Move constants into the threshold.
  4. Isolate the part that depends on the data. That is \(T(\bx)\). The test is \(T(\bx)\dec\gamma'\). If you divide by a negative number, flip the inequality.
  5. Find the distribution of \(T\) under \(\Hz\) and under \(\Ho\).
  6. Compute \(\PFA\) and \(\PD\) (or \(P_e\)). For NP, set \(\gamma'\) from \(\PFA=\alpha\) using \(\Hz\) alone, then substitute into \(\PD\) to get the ROC.
Lec 3 (Bayesian-2) p.10

Chapter 1

Mathematical toolkit

Every performance calculation in this course reduces to one question: what is the distribution of the test statistic under each hypothesis? For Gaussian data the answer is almost always a Gaussian or a chi-squared variable. This chapter collects the facts you need for both, and then introduces the sufficient statistic, which explains why a whole data vector can be replaced by one number.

1.1 The Gaussian PDF

Definition

A scalar \(x\sim\Nc(\mu,\sigma^2)\) has the PDF

\[ p(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]. \]

A vector \(\bx\in\mathbb{R}^N\) with \(\bx\sim\Nc(\bmu,\bC)\), where \(\bC\) is symmetric and positive definite, has the PDF

\[ p(\bx)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\det^{1/2}(\bC)}\exp\!\left[-\tfrac12(\bx-\bmu)^T\bC^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)\right], \]

with mean \(\bmu=E[\bx]\) and covariance \(\bC=E[(\bx-\bmu)(\bx-\bmu)^T]\).

For white Gaussian noise (WGN), \(\bC=\sigma^2\mathbf{I}\). Then \(\det\bC=\sigma^{2N}\) and \(\bC^{-1}=\mathbf{I}/\sigma^2\), so the PDF splits into a product of \(N\) scalar PDFs:

\[ p(\bx)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x[n]-\mu[n])^2\right]. \]

Most derivations in this course start from this line.

1.2 Five properties used again and again

  1. Complete specification. The mean and covariance determine the whole distribution.
  2. Uncorrelated means independent. If \(\bC\) is diagonal, the components are independent. This is false for general random variables. It is true for jointly Gaussian ones.
  3. Linear transformation. If \(\bx\sim\Nc(\bmu,\bC)\) and \(\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\bx+\mathbf{b}\), with \(\mathbf{A}\) of full row rank, then
\[ \mathbf{y}\sim\Nc\!\left(\mathbf{A}\bmu+\mathbf{b},\ \mathbf{A}\bC\mathbf{A}^T\right). \]
  1. Marginals are Gaussian. Any subvector of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian.
  2. Conditionals are Gaussian. Partition \(\bx=[\bx_1^T\ \bx_2^T]^T\). Then \(\bx_1\mid\bx_2\) is Gaussian with mean \(\bmu_1+\bC_{12}\bC_{22}^{-1}(\bx_2-\bmu_2)\) and covariance \(\bC_{11}-\bC_{12}\bC_{22}^{-1}\bC_{21}\). The covariance does not depend on \(\bx_2\). This is the source of the MMSE estimator used in chapter 10.
Preliminaries: Gaussian PDF p.3–4

1.3 Linear statistics, and the deflection coefficient

Property 3 makes linear detectors easy to analyse. Suppose the detector computes \(T(\bx)=\bh^T\bx\), and \(\bx\sim\Nc(\bmu_i,\bC)\) under \(\mathcal{H}_i\). Take \(\mathbf{A}=\bh^T\) (a single row) in property 3:

\[ T\mid\mathcal{H}_i\ \sim\ \Nc\!\left(\bh^T\bmu_i,\ \bh^T\bC\bh\right). \]

The two hypotheses give two Gaussians with the same variance and different means. That is the whole story for a linear-Gaussian detector.

Two quantities are needed below. Chapter 2 defines them properly; for now read them like this. The detector compares \(T\) with a threshold \(\gamma'\) and says "signal" (decides \(\Ho\)) when \(T>\gamma'\). \(\PFA\), the false-alarm probability, is the chance it says "signal" when there is none: \(\Pr\{T>\gamma'\}\) under \(\Hz\). \(\PD\), the detection probability, is the chance it says "signal" when there is one: \(\Pr\{T>\gamma'\}\) under \(\Ho\). A good detector has small \(\PFA\) and large \(\PD\).

Derivation

The universal ROC of a linear-Gaussian detector. Let the test be \(T>\gamma'\). Write \(\sigma_T^2=\bh^T\bC\bh\). Using \(\Pr\{\Nc(\mu,\sigma^2)>\gamma'\}=Q((\gamma'-\mu)/\sigma)\) (section 1.4):

\[ \PFA=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-\bh^T\bmu_0}{\sigma_T}\right),\qquad \PD=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-\bh^T\bmu_1}{\sigma_T}\right). \]

Invert the first equation: \(\gamma'=\bh^T\bmu_0+\sigma_T\,Q^{-1}(\PFA)\). Substitute into the second:

\[ \PD=Q\!\left(\frac{\bh^T\bmu_0+\sigma_T Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\bh^T\bmu_1}{\sigma_T}\right) =Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\frac{\bh^T(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)}{\sigma_T}\right). \]

Define the deflection coefficient

\[ d^2=\frac{\left(E[T;\Ho]-E[T;\Hz]\right)^2}{\mathrm{var}(T;\Hz)}=\frac{\left(\bh^T(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)\right)^2}{\bh^T\bC\bh}. \]

Its square root is \(\sqrt{d^2}=\dfrac{|\bh^T(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)|}{\sigma_T}\): the square has thrown away the sign. So we may replace the fraction in \(\PD\) by \(\sqrt{d^2}\) only when that fraction is positive, that is, when \(\bh^T(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)>0\) (the statistic is larger under \(\Ho\)). Then

\[ \boxed{\ \PD=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\sqrt{d^2}\right)\ } \]
Preliminaries: Gaussian PDF p.5
Note

What \(d^2\) measures. Under the two hypotheses, \(T\) has the same bell-shaped PDF, once centred at \(\bh^T\bmu_0\) and once at \(\bh^T\bmu_1\). The signal deflects (shifts) the statistic. \(\sqrt{d^2}\) is the size of that shift measured in standard deviations of \(T\): it is the signal-to-noise ratio of the statistic itself. The shape of the two bells is fixed, so their overlap, and with it every error probability, depends only on how many standard deviations apart they are.

  • \(d=0\): the bells sit on top of each other, and \(\PD=\PFA\). The detector is guessing.
  • \(d=3\): the bells are 3 standard deviations apart. At \(\PFA=0.01\) the threshold sits \(Q^{-1}(0.01)=2.33\) standard deviations above the \(\Hz\) mean, which is 0.67 below the \(\Ho\) mean, so \(\PD=Q(2.33-3)=Q(-0.67)=0.75\).
  • Large \(d\): the bells barely overlap, and \(\PD\to1\) at any fixed \(\PFA\).

So \(d^2\) is the single figure of merit for these detectors. To make a better detector, raise \(d^2\): the matched filter, the generalised matched filter and signal design (chapters 7–8) all do exactly that. It is defined as a square so that it does not depend on the sign or the scale of \(\bh\): replacing \(\bh\) by \(c\,\bh\) multiplies the top by \(c^2\) and the bottom by \(c^2\).

Why the condition \(\bh^T(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)>0\)? It says the test points the right way: "large \(T\) means signal". If the fraction were negative, \(T\) would be smaller under \(\Ho\), and the derivation gives \(\PD=Q\big(Q^{-1}(\PFA)+\sqrt{d^2}\big)\lt\PFA\): worse than guessing. The cure is to flip the test (decide \(\Ho\) when \(T\lt\gamma'\)), or use \(-\bh\). A statistic that comes out of a likelihood ratio, done with correct signs, always satisfies the condition. For example \(\bh=\bC^{-1}(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)\) gives \(\bh^T(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)=(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)^T\bC^{-1}(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)>0\), because \(\bC^{-1}\) is positive definite. So in practice you substitute directly; the condition is there to catch a test written the wrong way round.

Note

For a linear statistic with Gaussian data, \(d^2\) is the only number that matters. Two detectors with the same \(d^2\) have the same ROC. You will see this formula in chapter 3 (DC level), chapter 7 (matched filter, \(d^2=\E/\sigma^2\)), chapter 8 (generalised matched filter, \(d^2=\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\)) and chapter 11 (UMP tests). When you derive a linear detector, you can jump straight to this result once you have \(d^2\).

1.4 The Q function

Definition \[ Q(x)=\int_x^\infty\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\Pr\{\Nc(0,1)>x\}. \]

Properties:

  • \(Q(0)=\tfrac12\), \(Q(-x)=1-Q(x)\), and \(Q\) is strictly decreasing, so \(Q^{-1}\) exists and is also decreasing.
  • If \(x\sim\Nc(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \(\Pr\{x>\gamma\}=Q\!\left(\dfrac{\gamma-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\). Proof: \((x-\mu)/\sigma\sim\Nc(0,1)\), and \(x>\gamma\) is the same event as \((x-\mu)/\sigma>(\gamma-\mu)/\sigma\).
  • Similarly \(\Pr\{x\lt \gamma\}=1-Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=Q\!\left(\frac{\mu-\gamma}{\sigma}\right)\).
  • \(\Pr\{|x|>\gamma\}=2Q(\gamma/\sigma)\) for \(x\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\). This appears in every two-sided test.
  • \(Q(x)=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(x/\sqrt2)\).
  • Bounds for \(x>0\): \(\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\dfrac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\lt Q(x)\lt \dfrac{e^{-x^2/2}}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}\), and the bound \(Q(x)\le\tfrac12e^{-x^2/2}\) (the slide calls this the Chernoff bound; the classical Chernoff bound is \(Q(x)\le e^{-x^2/2}\), and the \(\tfrac12\) version is a slightly tighter bound).
Preliminaries: Gaussian PDF p.6
Common mistake

It is easy to write \(P(X>\gamma)=Q\big(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)\). The argument must be the threshold, not the variable: \(Q\big(\frac{\gamma-\mu}{\sigma}\big)\).

1.5 Chi-squared distributions

When both hypotheses have zero mean and differ only in variance, the detector is quadratic. Its statistic is a sum of squared Gaussians. Those are chi-squared variables.

Definition

Central chi-squared. If \(x_1,\dots,x_N\) are i.i.d. \(\Nc(0,1)\), then \(Y=\sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2\sim\chi^2_N\), with \(N\) degrees of freedom. Its PDF is

\[ p(y)=\frac{1}{2^{N/2}\Gamma(N/2)}\,y^{N/2-1}e^{-y/2},\qquad y>0, \]

with \(E[Y]=N\) and \(\mathrm{var}(Y)=2N\). Its right-tail probability is written \(Q_{\chi^2_N}(y)=\Pr\{Y>y\}\).

Non-central chi-squared. If the \(x_i\sim\Nc(\mu_i,1)\) are independent, then \(Y=\sum x_i^2\sim\chi'^2_N(\lambda)\) with non-centrality \(\lambda=\sum_i\mu_i^2\), \(E[Y]=N+\lambda\) and \(\mathrm{var}(Y)=2N+4\lambda\).

Preliminaries: Gaussian PDF p.7 · Lec 5 p.12–13
Derivation

Moments. For \(x\sim\Nc(\mu,1)\): \(E[x^2]=1+\mu^2\) and \(E[x^4]=\mu^4+6\mu^2+3\), so \(\mathrm{var}(x^2)=E[x^4]-(E[x^2])^2=2+4\mu^2\). Summing \(N\) independent terms gives \(E[Y]=N+\lambda\) and \(\mathrm{var}(Y)=2N+4\lambda\). Put \(\lambda=0\) for the central case.

\(N=2\) is exponential. With \(N=2\) the PDF above is \(p(y)=\tfrac12e^{-y/2}\), an exponential with mean 2. So

\[ \boxed{\ Q_{\chi^2_2}(y)=e^{-y/2}\ } \]

This is the simplest closed form, and exam questions use it whenever they want a closed-form ROC. More generally, every even \(N\) has a finite-sum tail (the Erlang form), \(Q_{\chi^2_N}(y)=e^{-y/2}\sum_{k=0}^{N/2-1}\frac{(y/2)^k}{k!}\), and \(N=1\) gives \(Q_{\chi^2_1}(y)=2Q(\sqrt y)\).

Two scaling facts turn real problems into these standard forms:

  • If \(x[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\) i.i.d., then \(\sum x^2[n]/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_N\). So \(\Pr\{\sum x^2[n]>\gamma'\}=Q_{\chi^2_N}(\gamma'/\sigma^2)\).
  • If \(x[n]\) are i.i.d. exponential with rate \(\lambda\) (mean \(1/\lambda\)), then \(2\lambda x[n]\sim\chi^2_2\), and \(2\lambda\sum_n x[n]\sim\chi^2_{2N}\). If \(x[n]\) is Rayleigh with parameter \(\sigma^2\), then \(x^2[n]/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_2\). Both facts come from the \(N=2\) case and are used in chapter 5 and in Assignment 4.

For large \(N\) (say \(N\gtrsim30\)), the central limit theorem gives \(Q_{\chi^2_N}(y)\approx Q\!\left(\frac{y-N}{\sqrt{2N}}\right)\). This is good in the body of the distribution and poor in the far tail, so be careful when \(\PFA\) is very small.

1.6 Sufficient statistics

The DC-level detector of chapter 3 uses the sample mean \(\bar x\) instead of all \(N\) samples. Why is no information lost? The sufficient statistic answers this.

Definition

Let \(\{f_\theta(\bx)\}\) be a family of distributions indexed by \(\theta\). A function \(T(\bx)\) is a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\) if the conditional distribution of \(\bx\) given \(T(\bx)\) does not depend on \(\theta\).

Preliminaries: Sufficient statistic p.3

In words: once you know \(T(\bx)\), the rest of the data is pure noise as far as \(\theta\) is concerned. Whatever you can do with \(\bx\), you can do with \(T(\bx)\).

Proof: in class

Claim. Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be i.i.d. coin tosses, \(X_i\in\{0,1\}\), with \(\theta=\Pr\{X_i=1\}\). Then \(T=\sum_i X_i\) is sufficient for \(\theta\).

Proof. Fix a sequence \(\bx\) with \(k\) ones. Its probability is \(\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\). The number of ones has a binomial distribution: \(\Pr\{T=k\}=\binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\). So

\[ \Pr\{\mathbf{X}=\bx\mid T=k\}=\frac{\Pr\{\mathbf{X}=\bx,\ T=k\}}{\Pr\{T=k\}} =\frac{\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}}{\binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}}=\frac{1}{\binom nk}. \]

If \(\bx\) does not have \(k\) ones, the conditional probability is 0. Neither value involves \(\theta\). Given the count, every arrangement of the ones is equally likely, whatever the coin. ∎

Preliminaries: Sufficient statistic p.4

Checking the definition directly is tedious for continuous data. The factorisation theorem gives a test that only needs the PDF.

Theorem (Neyman–Fisher factorisation)

\(T(\bx)\) is sufficient for \(\theta\) if and only if the PDF can be written as

\[ p(\bx;\theta)=g\big(T(\bx),\theta\big)\,h(\bx), \]

where \(g\) depends on the data only through \(T(\bx)\), and \(h\) does not depend on \(\theta\).

Proof (discrete case). Suppose the factorisation holds. For any \(\bx\) with \(T(\bx)=t\),

\[ \Pr\{\mathbf{X}=\bx\mid T=t\}=\frac{g(t,\theta)h(\bx)}{\sum_{\bx':T(\bx')=t}g(t,\theta)h(\bx')} =\frac{h(\bx)}{\sum_{\bx':T(\bx')=t}h(\bx')}. \]

The factor \(g(t,\theta)\) cancels because it is the same for every \(\bx'\) in the sum. The result is free of \(\theta\), so \(T\) is sufficient. Conversely, if \(T\) is sufficient, write \(p(\bx;\theta)=\Pr\{T=t;\theta\}\cdot\Pr\{\mathbf{X}=\bx\mid T=t\}\). The first factor is a function \(g(t,\theta)\); the second does not depend on \(\theta\) and is \(h(\bx)\). ∎

Preliminaries: Sufficient statistic p.5
Common mistake

It is easy to call this the "Neyman–Pearson factorisation". It is the Neyman–Fisher factorisation. The Neyman–Pearson theorem is the optimal detector of chapter 4. Mixing the names in an exam answer costs marks.

Example

DC level in WGN, \(\theta=A\) unknown, \(\sigma^2\) known. Expand the square:

\[ p(\bx;A)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left(NA^2-2A\sum x[n]\right)\right]\cdot \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n]\right]. \]

The first factor depends on the data only through \(\sum x[n]\); the second does not involve \(A\). So \(T=\sum x[n]\), or equivalently \(\bar x\), is sufficient. The same algebra with \(\sigma^2=1\) proves the slide's second claim: for i.i.d. \(\Nc(\theta,1)\) data, \(\bar x\) is sufficient.

Zero-mean data, \(\sigma^2\) unknown.

\[ p(\bx;\sigma^2)=\underbrace{\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n]\right]}_{g(T,\sigma^2)}\cdot\underbrace{1}_{h(\bx)}. \]

So \(T=\sum x^2[n]\) is sufficient. This is the energy detector of chapter 5.

Preliminaries: Sufficient statistic p.4–5

Sufficiency in detection

In detection, the "parameter" is the hypothesis itself, \(\theta\in\{\Hz,\Ho\}\). If

\[ p(\bx;\mathcal{H}_i)=g_i\big(T(\bx)\big)\,h(\bx),\qquad i=0,1, \]

then \(h\) cancels in the likelihood ratio:

\[ L(\bx)=\frac{g_1(T(\bx))\,h(\bx)}{g_0(T(\bx))\,h(\bx)}=\frac{g_1(T(\bx))}{g_0(T(\bx))}. \]

The optimal test depends on the data only through \(T\). This has a large practical consequence: we compute \(\PFA\) and \(\PD\) from the scalar PDF of \(T\), never from an \(N\)-dimensional integral over a decision region.

Preliminaries: Sufficient statistic p.6
Note

The converse also holds. The likelihood ratio \(L(\bx)\) itself is always a sufficient statistic for deciding between two simple hypotheses. Factor \(p(\bx;\Ho)=L(\bx)\,p(\bx;\Hz)\) and \(p(\bx;\Hz)=1\cdot p(\bx;\Hz)\): this is the factorisation with \(T=L\), \(g_1(t)=t\), \(g_0(t)=1\), \(h=p(\bx;\Hz)\). That is why the LRT never throws away useful information.

Chapter 2

The detection problem

Estimation theory, before the mid-semester exam, produced a number: an estimate \(\hat\theta\) of an unknown parameter. Detection theory produces a decision. The question changes from "how big is it?" to "is it there or not?". Performance is measured by probabilities of error, not by variance.

2.1 Two examples

Radar. A transmitter sends a short pulse. If an aircraft is present, a weak, noisy echo of the pulse comes back. If not, the receiver sees noise only. After sampling,

\[ \Hz:\ x[n]=w[n],\qquad \Ho:\ x[n]=s[n]+w[n],\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1, \]

with \(w[n]\) white Gaussian noise. The receiver must decide which line generated the data.

Binary communication (BPSK). A transmitter sends a positive pulse for bit 1 and a negative pulse for bit 0. The receiver sees the pulse plus noise and must decide which bit was sent.

The two examples differ in one important way. In communication the receiver knows how often each bit is sent (usually half the time each). In radar nobody knows the "probability that an enemy aircraft is present". This difference decides which optimality criterion we use.

Lec 1 p.9, p.14

2.2 Binary hypothesis testing

Definition

A simple binary hypothesis test chooses between

\[ \Hz:\ \bx\sim p(\bx;\Hz),\qquad \Ho:\ \bx\sim p(\bx;\Ho), \]

where both PDFs (or PMFs) are completely known. "Simple" means no unknown parameters. If a hypothesis contains an unknown parameter, it is composite (chapter 11). With more than two hypotheses the test is M-ary (chapter 6).

Definition

A decision rule (detector) is a map \(\delta:\mathbb{R}^N\to\{\Hz,\Ho\}\). It is the same thing as a partition of the observation space into two decision regions:

\[ \mathbb{R}^N=R_0\cup R_1,\qquad R_0\cap R_1=\emptyset,\qquad \bx\in R_i\Rightarrow\text{decide }\mathcal{H}_i. \]

\(R_1\) is called the critical region.

Lec 1 p.10–16

So "design an optimal detector" means "choose \(R_1\) optimally". Different criteria of optimality give different \(R_1\). The rest of the course is about which \(R_1\) is best, and how good it is.

A useful first question, asked in class (Lec 1): with \(\Hz:\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\) and \(\Ho:\Nc(A,\sigma^2)\), does \(N=1\) or \(N=100\) work better? Clearly \(N=100\). Averaging 100 samples shrinks the noise variance by 100, so the two hypotheses become easy to tell apart. Chapter 3 makes this exact: the performance depends on \(NA^2/\sigma^2\).

2.3 The four outcomes

\(\Hz\) true \(\Ho\) true
decide \(\Hz\) correct rejection, \(1-\PFA\) miss, \(P_M=\beta\) (Type II error)
decide \(\Ho\) false alarm, \(\PFA=\alpha\) (Type I error) detection, \(\PD=1-\beta\) (power)
Definition

For a decision region \(R_1\),

\[ \PFA=\Pr\{\text{decide }\Ho;\Hz\}=\int_{R_1}p(\bx;\Hz)\,d\bx,\qquad \PD=\Pr\{\text{decide }\Ho;\Ho\}=\int_{R_1}p(\bx;\Ho)\,d\bx, \] \[ P_M=\int_{R_0}p(\bx;\Ho)\,d\bx=1-\PD. \]

Statisticians call \(\alpha=\PFA\) the size or significance level, and \(1-\beta=\PD\) the power of the test.

Lec 1 p.16–17

Each probability integrates the PDF of the hypothesis that is true over the region of the decision that was made. A good reminder from class (Lec 1): "true hypothesis, hence its PDF".

2.4 There is no single best detector

Two trivial detectors show the problem:

  • Always decide \(\Hz\): \(R_1=\emptyset\), so \(\PFA=0\) but \(\PD=0\).
  • Always decide \(\Ho\): \(R_1=\mathbb{R}^N\), so \(\PD=1\) but \(\PFA=1\).

Making \(R_1\) bigger raises \(\PD\) and \(\PFA\) together. Making it smaller lowers both. There is an inherent trade-off between the two error types. "Optimal" only has a meaning after we fix one scalar figure of merit. The course uses two.

Bayesian (chapter 3)

Priors \(P(\Hz),P(\Ho)\) are known. Minimise the average cost, or the average probability of error \(P_e=P(\Hz)\PFA+P(\Ho)P_M\).

Natural for communication: symbol probabilities are known by design, and the two errors cost the same.

Neyman–Pearson (chapter 4)

No priors. Fix \(\PFA\le\alpha\) and maximise \(\PD\).

Natural for radar: the errors are wildly asymmetric (a false alarm wastes a missile, a miss loses a city) and priors do not exist.

Lec 1 p.11–13, p.21
Note

Every problem has three ingredients (Lec 1 p.12). A data model: the PDF of \(\bx\) under each hypothesis. A criterion: minimum \(P_e\) (or Bayes risk), or maximum \(\PD\) at fixed \(\PFA\). A performance analysis: closed-form \(\PFA\), \(\PD\), \(P_e\), and the ROC. Exam questions follow the same three steps.

Chapter 3

Bayesian detection

In the Bayesian view the true hypothesis is itself random. \(\Hz\) occurs with prior probability \(P(\Hz)\) and \(\Ho\) with \(P(\Ho)=1-P(\Hz)\). We also attach a cost to each kind of decision. Then every detector has one number, its average cost, and we can ask for the detector that makes this number smallest. This chapter derives that detector. It turns out to be a likelihood ratio test, and so does every other optimal detector in this course.

3.1 Costs and the Bayes risk

Definition

\(C_{ij}\) is the cost of deciding \(\mathcal{H}_i\) when \(\mathcal{H}_j\) is true. The Bayes risk is the average cost,

\[ \mathcal{R}=E[C]=\sum_{i=0}^{1}\sum_{j=0}^{1}C_{ij}\,\Pr\{\text{decide }\mathcal{H}_i\mid\mathcal{H}_j\}\,P(\mathcal{H}_j). \]

We assume an error costs more than the correct decision: \(C_{10}>C_{00}\) and \(C_{01}>C_{11}\).

Lec 1 p.22 · Lec 6 (summary) p.8 · Kay §3.7

Written out, with \(P_0=P(\Hz)\) and \(P_1=P(\Ho)\):

\[ \mathcal{R}=C_{00}P_0(1-\PFA)+C_{10}P_0\PFA+C_{01}P_1P_M+C_{11}P_1\PD . \]

3.2 The minimum Bayes-risk detector

This is the most important derivation in the first half of the course. It was asked for 8 marks in the 2024 end-sem, and its special case (minimum \(P_e\), section 3.3) was part (a) of a 10-mark quiz question in 2025. Learn it until you can write it without thinking.

Theorem (minimum Bayes risk)

The decision rule that minimises \(\mathcal{R}\) is the likelihood ratio test

\[ \boxed{\ L(\bx)=\frac{p(\bx\mid\Ho)}{p(\bx\mid\Hz)}\ \dec\ \gamma=\frac{(C_{10}-C_{00})\,P(\Hz)}{(C_{01}-C_{11})\,P(\Ho)}\ } \]

Proof. Write each conditional probability as an integral over a decision region. Deciding \(\mathcal{H}_i\) means \(\bx\in R_i\), so \(\Pr\{\text{decide }\mathcal{H}_i\mid\mathcal{H}_j\}=\int_{R_i}p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_j)\,d\bx\). Then

\[ \mathcal{R}=\int_{R_0}\!\big[C_{00}P_0\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)+C_{01}P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Ho)\big]d\bx +\int_{R_1}\!\big[C_{10}P_0\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)+C_{11}P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Ho)\big]d\bx . \]

Each point \(\bx\) belongs to exactly one region. So we can minimise \(\mathcal{R}\) one point at a time: put \(\bx\) in the region whose integrand is smaller at \(\bx\). To see this cleanly, use \(\int_{R_0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}-\int_{R_1}\) and \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^N}p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_j)\,d\bx=1\):

\[ \mathcal{R}=C_{00}P_0+C_{01}P_1+\int_{R_1}\Big[(C_{10}-C_{00})P_0\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)-(C_{01}-C_{11})P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Ho)\Big]d\bx . \]

The first two terms do not depend on the detector. The integral is smallest when \(R_1\) contains exactly the points where the integrand is negative. Including a point with a positive integrand would add to \(\mathcal{R}\); leaving out a point with a negative integrand would fail to subtract from it. So decide \(\Ho\) when

\[ (C_{01}-C_{11})P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Ho)>(C_{10}-C_{00})P_0\,p(\bx\mid\Hz). \]

Both cost differences are positive, so we may divide by \((C_{01}-C_{11})P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)\) without flipping the inequality. That gives \(L(\bx)>\gamma\) with the \(\gamma\) above. Points where the integrand is exactly zero contribute nothing, so they may go in either region. ∎

Kay Appendix 3B

Look at what the result says. The statistic \(L(\bx)\) depends only on the data model. The costs and the priors only move the threshold. That is the first appearance of the course's central idea: all optimal detectors compute the same \(L(\bx)\) and differ only in \(\gamma\).

Note

The "posterior cost" form. Divide the integrands by \(p(\bx)\) and use Bayes' rule, \(P(\mathcal{H}_j\mid\bx)=p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_j)P(\mathcal{H}_j)/p(\bx)\). The expected cost of deciding \(\mathcal{H}_i\), given the data, is

\[ \mathcal{C}_i(\bx)=\sum_j C_{ij}\,P(\mathcal{H}_j\mid\bx), \]

and the Bayes rule decides the hypothesis with the smaller \(\mathcal{C}_i(\bx)\). This form extends to \(M\) hypotheses with no change (chapter 6). Lec 6 (summary) p.8–9 prints it with the priors \(P(\mathcal{H}_j)\) instead of the posteriors \(P(\mathcal{H}_j\mid\bx)\). With priors only, the rule would ignore the data. The posteriors are correct.

3.3 Minimum probability of error: MAP and ML

Take \(C_{00}=C_{11}=0\) and \(C_{01}=C_{10}=1\). Then the Bayes risk is exactly the probability of error, \(\mathcal{R}=P_0\PFA+P_1P_M=P_e\). Past papers ask for this case separately, so here is a self-contained derivation.

Theorem (minimum \(P_e\))

The detector that minimises \(P_e\) decides \(\Ho\) when

\[ \frac{p(\bx\mid\Ho)}{p(\bx\mid\Hz)}>\frac{P(\Hz)}{P(\Ho)} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(\Ho\mid\bx)>P(\Hz\mid\bx). \]

Proof. An error happens when we decide \(\Ho\) under \(\Hz\), or \(\Hz\) under \(\Ho\):

\[ P_e=P_0\int_{R_1}p(\bx\mid\Hz)\,d\bx+P_1\int_{R_0}p(\bx\mid\Ho)\,d\bx =P_1+\int_{R_1}\big[P_0\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)-P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Ho)\big]d\bx, \]

using \(\int_{R_0}p(\bx\mid\Ho)\,d\bx=1-\int_{R_1}p(\bx\mid\Ho)\,d\bx\). \(P_e\) is smallest when \(R_1\) holds exactly the points where the bracket is negative, that is, where \(P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Ho)>P_0\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)\). Dividing by \(P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)\) gives the likelihood ratio form. Dividing instead by \(p(\bx)\) and using Bayes' rule gives \(P(\Ho\mid\bx)>P(\Hz\mid\bx)\). ∎

Lec 1 p.22 · Kay §3.6
MAP detector

Minimum \(P_e\) means: choose the hypothesis with the larger a posteriori probability, \(\max_i P(\mathcal{H}_i\mid\bx)\). Threshold \(\gamma=P_0/P_1\).

ML detector

If also \(P_0=P_1=\tfrac12\), then \(\gamma=1\): choose the hypothesis under which the data are more likely, \(\max_i p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i)\).

3.4 Monotone transformations

If \(g\) is strictly increasing, then \(L(\bx)>\gamma\) and \(g(L(\bx))>g(\gamma)\) are the same event. So we may take logarithms (products become sums, Gaussian exponents become quadratics). We may add constants to both sides, and multiply both sides by a positive constant. The threshold absorbs all of it.

Common mistake

Multiplying or dividing by a negative number flips the inequality. Dividing by a quantity of unknown sign (an unknown amplitude \(A\), say) is not allowed at all, because you do not know which way the test points. This matters for composite tests in chapter 11. In the binomial example it is easy to divide by \(\ln(p_0/p_1)-\ln\frac{1-p_0}{1-p_1}\), which is negative, without flipping. See section 5.5 for the correct version.

3.5 Example: DC level in WGN

This example runs through the whole course. Learn every step.

\[ \Hz:\ x[n]=w[n],\qquad \Ho:\ x[n]=A+w[n],\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1, \]

with \(A>0\) known and \(w[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\) i.i.d. So \(x[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\) under \(\Hz\) and \(x[n]\sim\Nc(A,\sigma^2)\) under \(\Ho\).

Derivation

Step 1: the likelihood ratio. The normalising constants are the same and cancel:

\[ L(\bx)=\frac{\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x[n]-A)^2\right]} {\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n]\right]}. \]

Step 2: take the log. Expand \((x-A)^2=x^2-2Ax+A^2\); the \(x^2\) terms cancel:

\[ \ln L(\bx)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(A^2-2Ax[n]\right) =\frac{A}{\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]-\frac{NA^2}{2\sigma^2}\ \dec\ \ln\gamma . \]

Step 3: isolate the data. Since \(NA/\sigma^2>0\), divide by it without flipping:

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\bar x=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\ \dec\ \gamma'=\frac{\sigma^2\ln\gamma}{NA}+\frac A2\ } \]

Step 4: distribution of \(T\). \(T=\bh^T\bx\) with \(\bh=\frac1N[1,\dots,1]^T\). By section 1.3, \(T\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2/N)\) under \(\Hz\) and \(T\sim\Nc(A,\sigma^2/N)\) under \(\Ho\).

Step 5: error probabilities.

\[ \PFA=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right),\qquad P_M=1-Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-A}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right)=Q\!\left(\frac{A-\gamma'}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right). \]
Lec 2 p.6–7

What is special about \(T(\bx)\)? The slides ask this. Three answers. It is a scalar: \(N\) numbers became one. It is a sufficient statistic (section 1.6), so nothing was lost. And it is the MVU estimator of \(A\) from the first half of the course: the detector estimates the DC level and compares the estimate with a threshold. Finding its distribution is useful because it turns every error probability into a one-dimensional Gaussian tail.

Derivation

Equal priors, minimum \(P_e\). \(\gamma=P_0/P_1=1\), so \(\ln\gamma=0\) and \(\gamma'=A/2\). Then both errors are the same:

\[ \PFA=P_M=Q\!\left(\frac{A/2}{\sigma/\sqrt N}\right)=Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{NA^2}{4\sigma^2}}\right), \qquad \boxed{\ P_e=\tfrac12\PFA+\tfrac12P_M=Q\!\left(\tfrac12\sqrt{d^2}\right),\quad d^2=\frac{NA^2}{\sigma^2}\ } \]

\(P_e\) falls as \(N\) grows. If you need \(P_e=0.05\) and the SNR \(A^2/\sigma^2\) is fixed by the hardware, the only thing you control is \(N\). From \(\tfrac12\sqrt{NA^2/\sigma^2}=Q^{-1}(0.05)=1.645\), you need \(N=10.8\,\sigma^2/A^2\).

Homework (set in class): "find the minimisation"

Show directly, without the general theorem, that the threshold minimising \(P_e\) for this example is where the weighted PDFs cross.

Solution. Write \(P_e\) as a function of the threshold:

\[ P_e(\gamma')=P_0\int_{\gamma'}^{\infty}p(t\mid\Hz)\,dt+P_1\int_{-\infty}^{\gamma'}p(t\mid\Ho)\,dt . \]

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(\dfrac{dP_e}{d\gamma'}=-P_0\,p(\gamma'\mid\Hz)+P_1\,p(\gamma'\mid\Ho)\). Setting this to zero gives

\[ \frac{p(\gamma'\mid\Ho)}{p(\gamma'\mid\Hz)}=\frac{P_0}{P_1}: \]

the optimal threshold is where \(P_1\,p(t\mid\Ho)\) and \(P_0\,p(t\mid\Hz)\) cross. Insert the two Gaussians \(\Nc(A,\sigma^2/N)\) and \(\Nc(0,\sigma^2/N)\) and take logs:

\[ \frac{\gamma'^2-(\gamma'-A)^2}{2\sigma^2/N}=\ln\frac{P_0}{P_1} \ \Longrightarrow\ \gamma'=\frac A2+\frac{\sigma^2}{NA}\ln\frac{P_0}{P_1}. \]

This is the LRT threshold with \(\gamma=P_0/P_1\), as the theorem promised. To check it is a minimum: below \(\gamma'\) the derivative is negative and above it positive, because the Gaussian likelihood ratio is increasing in \(t\). With equal priors, \(\gamma'=A/2\). ∎

Homework (Lec 2 p.7): unequal priors and unequal costs

Find the effect of unequal priors and costs on the DC-level detector and its performance.

Solution. The statistic is still \(\bar x\). Only the threshold moves:

\[ \gamma'=\frac A2+\frac{\sigma^2}{NA}\ln\gamma,\qquad \gamma=\frac{(C_{10}-C_{00})P_0}{(C_{01}-C_{11})P_1}. \]

\(\gamma>1\) exactly when \((C_{10}-C_{00})P_0>(C_{01}-C_{11})P_1\): for example \(P_0>P_1\) with equal cost differences, or a costlier false alarm with equal priors. Then the threshold moves right: we need stronger evidence before saying "signal". When the inequality is reversed, it moves left. Priors and costs act together; neither decides on its own.

For the performance, write \(d=\sqrt{NA^2/\sigma^2}\) and normalise the threshold by the standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt N\):

\[ \frac{\gamma'}{\sigma/\sqrt N}=\frac{\sqrt N A}{2\sigma}+\frac{\sigma\ln\gamma}{\sqrt N A}=\frac d2+\frac{\ln\gamma}{d}, \qquad \frac{A-\gamma'}{\sigma/\sqrt N}=\frac d2-\frac{\ln\gamma}{d}. \]

So

\[ \PFA=Q\!\left(\frac d2+\frac{\ln\gamma}{d}\right),\qquad P_M=Q\!\left(\frac d2-\frac{\ln\gamma}{d}\right), \] \[ \mathcal{R}=C_{00}P_0+C_{01}P_1+(C_{10}-C_{00})P_0\,\PFA-(C_{01}-C_{11})P_1\,(1-P_M). \]

For 0–1 costs, \(\gamma=P_0/P_1\) and

\[ P_e=P_0\,Q\!\left(\frac d2+\frac{\ln(P_0/P_1)}{d}\right)+P_1\,Q\!\left(\frac d2-\frac{\ln(P_0/P_1)}{d}\right). \]

This is exactly the formula on Lec 8 p.6 for binary signalling with unequal priors; chapter 9 uses it again.

Numbers. Take \(d=2\), \(P_0=0.8\), \(P_1=0.2\). Then \(\ln 4=1.386\), \(\PFA=Q(1.693)=0.045\), \(P_M=Q(0.307)=0.380\), and \(P_e=0.8(0.045)+0.2(0.380)=0.112\). If you kept the equal-prior threshold \(A/2\), both errors would be \(Q(1)=0.159\) and \(P_e=0.159\). The prior-aware threshold accepts more misses (which are now rare events) in exchange for far fewer false alarms.

3.6 Example: two multivariate Gaussians with the same covariance

\[ \Hz:\ \bx\sim\Nc(\bmu_0,\bC),\qquad \Ho:\ \bx\sim\Nc(\bmu_1,\bC). \]
Derivation

The determinants are equal and cancel. The log-likelihood ratio is

\[ \ln L(\bx)=-\tfrac12\Big[(\bx-\bmu_1)^T\bC^{-1}(\bx-\bmu_1)-(\bx-\bmu_0)^T\bC^{-1}(\bx-\bmu_0)\Big]. \]

Expand both quadratic forms. The \(\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bx\) terms cancel. Because \(\bC^{-1}\) is symmetric, \(\bmu^T\bC^{-1}\bx=\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bmu\), so the cross terms combine:

\[ \ln L(\bx)=(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)^T\bC^{-1}\bx-\tfrac12\left(\bmu_1^T\bC^{-1}\bmu_1-\bmu_0^T\bC^{-1}\bmu_0\right)\ \dec\ \ln\gamma . \] \[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=(\bmu_1-\bmu_0)^T\bC^{-1}\bx\ \dec\ \gamma'=\ln\gamma+\tfrac12\left(\bmu_1^T\bC^{-1}\bmu_1-\bmu_0^T\bC^{-1}\bmu_0\right)\ } \]

Performance. \(T=\bh^T\bx\) with \(\bh=\bC^{-1}\Delta\), where \(\Delta=\bmu_1-\bmu_0\). Then \(E[T;\Ho]-E[T;\Hz]=\bh^T\Delta=\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\Delta\) and \(\mathrm{var}(T)=\bh^T\bC\bh=\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\bC\bC^{-1}\Delta=\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\Delta\). So

\[ d^2=\frac{(\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\Delta)^2}{\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\Delta}=\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\Delta,\qquad \PFA=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-\bh^T\bmu_0}{\sqrt{d^2}}\right),\quad \PD=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-\bh^T\bmu_1}{\sqrt{d^2}}\right). \]

For NP, \(\PD=Q(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\sqrt{d^2})\). For equal priors (\(\gamma=1\)) the threshold sits halfway between the two means of \(T\), and \(P_e=Q(\tfrac12\sqrt{d^2})\).

The quantity \(\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\Delta\) is the squared Mahalanobis distance between the two means. It measures their separation in units of the noise, direction by direction. With white noise it becomes \(\|\Delta\|^2/\sigma^2\). This example is the general form of chapters 7, 8 and 9: a known signal in noise is the case \(\bmu_0=\mathbf{0}\), \(\bmu_1=\bs\).

3.7 Example: DC level in Laplacian noise

Classwork (Lec 3 Bayesian-2 p.8)

Let \(x[n]=A+w[n]\) under \(\Ho\) and \(x[n]=w[n]\) under \(\Hz\), with \(A>0\) known and \(w[n]\) i.i.d. Laplacian with zero mean, \(p(w)=\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|w|/b}\). Derive the LRT. Is the sample mean still sufficient? Find the performance.

Derivation

The detector. For i.i.d. samples,

\[ p(\bx;\Hz)=\prod_n\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x[n]|/b},\qquad p(\bx;\Ho)=\prod_n\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x[n]-A|/b}. \] \[ \ln L(\bx)=\frac1b\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\big(|x[n]|-|x[n]-A|\big)\ \dec\ \ln\gamma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}g(x[n])\ \dec\ b\ln\gamma,\qquad g(x)=|x|-|x-A|\ } \]

Work out \(g\) piece by piece:

\[ g(x)=\begin{cases}-A, & x\le 0\\ 2x-A, & 0\lt x\lt A\\ A, & x\ge A.\end{cases} \]

So \(g\) is a limiter. It is linear in the middle and clips at \(\pm A\).

Why a limiter? Laplacian noise has heavier tails than Gaussian noise. A very large sample is more likely to be a noise spike than evidence for the signal. The optimal detector caps the influence of any one sample. The sample mean has no such protection; one outlier can drag it across the threshold.

Is the sample mean still sufficient? No. The likelihood ratio depends on the data through \(\sum g(x[n])\), and two data sets with the same mean can give different values. Take \(N=2\): \(\bx=(A,A)\) gives \(\bar x=A\) and \(T=2A\), while \(\bx=(0,2A)\) also gives \(\bar x=A\) but \(T=-A+A=0\). The same \(\bar x\) leads to different \(L(\bx)\), so \(\bar x\) cannot carry all the information. (No scalar statistic simpler than \(\sum g(x[n])\) works here; Gaussian noise is special in giving the sample mean.)

Derivation

Performance for \(N=1\). For a threshold \(t\) with \(-A\lt t\lt A\), the event \(g(x)>t\) is \(2x-A>t\), that is, \(x>c\) with \(c=(t+A)/2\in(0,A)\). The Laplacian tail is \(\Pr\{w>c\}=\tfrac12e^{-c/b}\) for \(c\ge0\). So

\[ \PFA=\Pr\{w>c\}=\tfrac12e^{-c/b},\qquad \PD=\Pr\{w>c-A\}=1-\tfrac12e^{-(A-c)/b}. \]

Eliminate \(c\): \(e^{c/b}=1/(2\PFA)\), so

\[ \PD=1-\frac{e^{-A/b}}{4\,\PFA},\qquad \tfrac12e^{-A/b}\lt \PFA\lt \tfrac12 . \]

With equal priors, \(\gamma=1\), so \(t=0\) and \(c=A/2\). Both errors equal \(\tfrac12e^{-A/(2b)}\), and \(P_e=\tfrac12e^{-A/(2b)}\).

For \(N>1\), \(T\) is a sum of clipped Laplacian variables. Its distribution has no simple closed form. For large \(N\) use the central limit theorem, or simulate.

Note

\(g(x)\) takes the values \(\pm A\) with positive probability (whenever \(x\le0\) or \(x\ge A\)). So even though the data are continuous, \(T\) has point masses. Thresholds placed exactly at \(\pm A\) would need the randomised rules of chapter 4 to reach every \(\PFA\). This is why the formula above only covers part of the ROC.

Common mistake

It is easy to write \(L(\bx)\) with \(p(\bx;\Hz)\) on top and end with \(\sum(|x[n]-A|-|x[n]|)\dec\gamma'\). That is the wrong way round: it would decide "signal" when the samples are near zero. The correct statistic is \(\sum(|x[n]|-|x[n]-A|)\), large when the samples are near \(A\). A quick sanity check catches this kind of slip: the statistic should be large when the data look like \(\Ho\).

3.8 Randomisation never helps a Bayesian

For discrete data we will sometimes need randomised rules, where \(\delta(\bx)\in[0,1]\) is the probability of deciding \(\Ho\). Does randomising help reduce the Bayes risk? No. Repeat the proof of section 3.2 with \(\delta\) in place of the indicator of \(R_1\):

\[ \mathcal{R}(\delta)=C_{00}P_0+C_{01}P_1+\sum_{\bx}\delta(\bx)\Big[(C_{10}-C_{00})P_0\,p(\bx\mid\Hz)-(C_{01}-C_{11})P_1\,p(\bx\mid\Ho)\Big]. \]

This is linear in each \(\delta(\bx)\). A linear function on \([0,1]\) is minimised at an endpoint: take \(\delta(\bx)=1\) where the bracket is negative and \(0\) where it is positive. Where the bracket is zero, any \(\delta(\bx)\) gives the same risk. So a deterministic LRT always achieves the minimum Bayes risk. Randomisation is a Neyman–Pearson tool, needed to hit an exact \(\PFA\). Assignment 4, Q6 asks exactly this; it is solved in chapter 13.

Chapter 4

Neyman–Pearson detection

Chapter 3 needed priors and costs. In radar, sonar and most sensing problems they do not exist in any usable form. Neyman and Pearson (1933) proposed a different criterion: do not invent priors. Fix the false-alarm rate at a level the system can tolerate, and then detect as well as possible. This chapter states and proves their theorem. The answer is again a likelihood ratio test. Only the rule for the threshold changes.

4.1 The criterion

Definition

The Neyman–Pearson problem. Among all decision rules with

\[ \PFA=\int_{R_1}p(\bx;\Hz)\,d\bx\ \le\ \alpha, \]

find the one that maximises \(\PD=\int_{R_1}p(\bx;\Ho)\,d\bx\). The number \(\alpha\) is the size or significance level of the test. A rule that solves this problem is an \(\alpha\)-level Neyman–Pearson rule, or a most powerful test of size \(\alpha\).

Lec 3 (NP-1) p.5

The criterion is asymmetric on purpose. \(\PFA\) is controlled; \(\PD\) is optimised. No priors and no costs appear. In radar \(\alpha\) is set by how many false tracks the system can handle, typically \(10^{-6}\) or smaller.

4.2 Randomised decision rules

With continuous data, we can usually choose a threshold that makes \(\PFA\) exactly \(\alpha\). With discrete data we often cannot. A binomial count can only exceed 8 or exceed 9; there may be no threshold in between that gives \(\PFA=0.05\). The fix is to randomise on the boundary.

Definition

A randomised decision rule is a function \(\delta:\mathcal{X}^N\to[0,1]\). \(\delta(\bx)\) is the probability of deciding \(\Ho\) when \(\bx\) is observed. For example

\[ \delta(\bx)=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if } p(\bx;\Ho)>\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\\ q & \text{if } p(\bx;\Ho)=\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\\ 0 & \text{if } p(\bx;\Ho)\lt\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz),\end{cases} \]

which means: on the boundary, toss a coin that shows "\(\Ho\)" with probability \(q\). Its error probabilities are averages of \(\delta\):

\[ \PFA(\delta)=\int\delta(\bx)\,p(\bx;\Hz)\,d\bx=E_0[\delta(\bx)],\qquad \PD(\delta)=\int\delta(\bx)\,p(\bx;\Ho)\,d\bx=E_1[\delta(\bx)]. \]
Lec 3 (NP-1) p.6 · Lec 4 p.7

A non-randomised rule is the special case \(\delta(\bx)=\mathbb{1}\{\bx\in R_1\}\). Then the integrals above reduce to the integrals over \(R_1\) from chapter 2. Think of \(\delta\) as an indicator of \(R_1\) that is allowed to take fractional values.

4.3 The Neyman–Pearson theorem

Theorem (Neyman–Pearson)

Let \(\alpha>0\). Consider rules of the form

\[ \delta(\bx)=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if } p(\bx;\Ho)>\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\\ c(\bx) & \text{if } p(\bx;\Ho)=\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\\ 0 & \text{if } p(\bx;\Ho)\lt\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz),\end{cases}\qquad \eta\ge0,\ \ 0\le c(\bx)\le1. \tag{NP} \]
  1. Optimality. If \(\delta\) has the form (NP) and \(\PFA(\delta)=\alpha\), then \(\PD(\delta)\ge\PD(\delta')\) for every rule \(\delta'\) with \(\PFA(\delta')\le\alpha\).
  2. Existence. For every \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) there is a rule of the form (NP), with a constant \(c(\bx)=c_0\), whose \(\PFA\) is exactly \(\alpha\).
  3. Uniqueness. Every \(\alpha\)-level NP rule has the form (NP), except possibly on a set of zero probability under both hypotheses.
Lec 3 (NP-1) p.7 · Lec 4 p.8 · Lec 5 p.5 · Poor Prop. II.D.1

The three parts answer three questions. Is the LRT the best? (1) Can we always build one with the right size? (2) Is there anything else that is equally good? (3) The proofs are short. The professor did all three in class. Here they are in full.

Proof of part 1 (optimality)

Let \(\delta\) have the form (NP) with \(\PFA(\delta)=\alpha\), and let \(\delta'\) be any rule with \(\PFA(\delta')\le\alpha\). We claim that for every \(\bx\),

\[ \big[\delta(\bx)-\delta'(\bx)\big]\big[p(\bx;\Ho)-\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\big]\ \ge\ 0 . \]

Check the three cases. If \(p(\bx;\Ho)>\eta p(\bx;\Hz)\), the second factor is positive and \(\delta(\bx)=1\ge\delta'(\bx)\), so the first factor is \(\ge0\). If \(p(\bx;\Ho)\lt\eta p(\bx;\Hz)\), the second factor is negative and \(\delta(\bx)=0\le\delta'(\bx)\), so the first is \(\le0\). If they are equal, the second factor is zero. In every case the product is non-negative.

Integrate over all \(\bx\) and split the integral into four pieces:

\[ \underbrace{\int\delta\,p(\bx;\Ho)}_{\PD(\delta)}-\underbrace{\int\delta'\,p(\bx;\Ho)}_{\PD(\delta')} -\eta\Big[\underbrace{\int\delta\,p(\bx;\Hz)}_{\PFA(\delta)}-\underbrace{\int\delta'\,p(\bx;\Hz)}_{\PFA(\delta')}\Big]\ \ge\ 0 . \]

Rearrange:

\[ \PD(\delta)-\PD(\delta')\ \ge\ \eta\big[\PFA(\delta)-\PFA(\delta')\big]=\eta\big[\alpha-\PFA(\delta')\big]\ \ge\ 0, \]

because \(\eta\ge0\) and \(\PFA(\delta')\le\alpha\). So \(\PD(\delta)\ge\PD(\delta')\). ∎

Poor (II.D.6)–(II.D.8)

The key inequality, \(\PD(\delta)-\PD(\delta')\ge\eta[\PFA(\delta)-\PFA(\delta')]\), is worth remembering on its own. It holds for any rule \(\delta'\), whatever its size. We use it again to prove uniqueness here, and to prove the ROC properties in chapter 5.

Proof of part 2 (existence)

Define the function \(\phi(\eta)=\Pr_0\{p(\bx;\Ho)>\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\}=\Pr_0\{L(\bx)>\eta\}\), the probability under \(\Hz\) that the likelihood ratio exceeds \(\eta\). As \(\eta\) grows, the event shrinks, so \(\phi\) is non-increasing. It goes from (at most) 1 near \(\eta=0\) to 0 as \(\eta\to\infty\). It is right-continuous, like any tail probability, but it may jump down where \(L(\bx)\) has a point mass.

Let \(\eta_0\) be the smallest \(\eta\) with \(\phi(\eta)\le\alpha\). (It exists because \(\phi\) is right-continuous.) Just to the left of \(\eta_0\), \(\phi\) is above \(\alpha\), so

\[ \Pr_0\{L>\eta_0\}\ \le\ \alpha\ \le\ \Pr_0\{L\ge\eta_0\}=\Pr_0\{L>\eta_0\}+\Pr_0\{L=\eta_0\}. \]

Two cases.

  • If \(\Pr_0\{L>\eta_0\}\lt\alpha\), then \(\Pr_0\{L=\eta_0\}>0\) (there is a jump at \(\eta_0\)). Choose \[ c_0=\frac{\alpha-\Pr_0\{L>\eta_0\}}{\Pr_0\{L=\eta_0\}}. \] By the double inequality above, \(0\le c_0\le1\), so this is a valid probability.
  • If \(\Pr_0\{L>\eta_0\}=\alpha\) exactly, there is nothing to make up. Any \(c_0\in[0,1]\) works when \(\Pr_0\{L=\eta_0\}=0\); otherwise take \(c_0=0\).

In both cases the rule (NP) with \(\eta=\eta_0\) and \(c(\bx)=c_0\) has

\[ \PFA=\Pr_0\{L>\eta_0\}+c_0\,\Pr_0\{L=\eta_0\}=\alpha .\qquad\blacksquare \]

Common mistake

Case 2 is easy to get wrong. When \(\Pr_0\{L>\eta_0\}=\alpha\), the plain non-randomised test already has size \(\alpha\), so the boundary must contribute nothing: \(c_0=0\) is correct (and if the boundary has zero probability, any \(c_0\) is fine). Also note the existence proof needs \(\eta_0\) to be the smallest threshold with \(\phi\le\alpha\); that is what guarantees \(c_0\le1\).

Proof of part 3 (uniqueness)

Let \(\delta\) be the NP rule of form (NP) and \(\delta''\) any other \(\alpha\)-level NP rule. Both are optimal, so \(\PD(\delta'')=\PD(\delta)\). Apply the key inequality with \(\delta'=\delta''\):

\[ 0=\PD(\delta)-\PD(\delta'')\ \ge\ \eta\big[\alpha-\PFA(\delta'')\big]\ \ge\ 0 . \]

So \(\PFA(\delta'')=\alpha\) as well (for \(\eta>0\)). Now both error probabilities match, so every step of the optimality proof holds with equality:

\[ \int\big[\delta(\bx)-\delta''(\bx)\big]\big[p(\bx;\Ho)-\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\big]\,d\bx=0 . \]

The integrand is non-negative everywhere (shown in part 1). A non-negative function with zero integral is zero except on a set of probability zero. Wherever \(p(\bx;\Ho)\ne\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\), the second factor is non-zero, so the first must vanish: \(\delta''(\bx)=\delta(\bx)\), which is 1 or 0 as (NP) requires. The two rules can differ only on the boundary set \(\{p(\bx;\Ho)=\eta\,p(\bx;\Hz)\}\), where (NP) allows any \(c(\bx)\). So \(\delta''\) also has the form (NP). ∎

4.4 The continuous case, and the Lagrangian view

For continuous data, \(\Pr_0\{L(\bx)=\eta\}=0\) in almost every problem we meet. The boundary does not matter, randomisation is unnecessary, and the theorem becomes the form on the slides.

Theorem (NP, continuous data)

To maximise \(\PD\) subject to \(\PFA=\alpha\), decide \(\Ho\) if

\[ L(\bx)=\frac{p(\bx;\Ho)}{p(\bx;\Hz)}>\gamma,\qquad\text{where }\ \int_{\{\bx:L(\bx)>\gamma\}}p(\bx;\Hz)\,d\bx=\alpha . \]
Lec 3 (NP-1) p.8 · Lec 4 p.9 · Kay Theorem 3.1
Derivation

Where the LRT comes from (Kay, Appendix 3A). Maximise \(\PD\) subject to \(\PFA=\alpha\) with a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\):

\[ F=\PD+\lambda(\PFA-\alpha)=\int_{R_1}\big[p(\bx;\Ho)+\lambda\,p(\bx;\Hz)\big]d\bx-\lambda\alpha . \]

As in the Bayes proof, \(F\) is largest when \(R_1\) contains exactly the points with a positive integrand: \(p(\bx;\Ho)+\lambda p(\bx;\Hz)>0\), that is, \(L(\bx)>-\lambda\). Write \(\gamma=-\lambda\). If \(\lambda\) were positive, every point would satisfy the inequality, \(R_1\) would be everything, and \(\PFA=1\). So for \(\alpha\lt1\) we need \(\lambda\lt0\), i.e. \(\gamma>0\), chosen to meet the constraint.

Note

The threshold comes from \(\Hz\) alone. \(\gamma'\) is fixed by \(\Pr\{T>\gamma';\Hz\}=\alpha\). You never need the \(\Ho\) model to set the threshold, only to compute \(\PD\). This has a practical consequence that exam questions like to use. If the \(\Ho\) model turns out to be wrong (say the true amplitude is \(\beta A\) instead of \(A\)), \(\PFA\) is still exactly \(\alpha\); only \(\PD\) changes. It is also the reason UMP tests exist (chapter 11).

4.5 Example: DC level in WGN

Same model as section 3.5: \(\Hz:x[n]=w[n]\), \(\Ho:x[n]=A+w[n]\), \(A>0\) known, WGN with variance \(\sigma^2\).

Derivation

The likelihood ratio is the same as in section 3.5, so the test is again

\[ T(\bx)=\bar x\ \dec\ \gamma' . \]

Only the way we set \(\gamma'\) differs. Under \(\Hz\), \(\bar x\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2/N)\), so

\[ \PFA=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right)=\alpha \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \gamma'=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{N}}\,Q^{-1}(\PFA). \]

Under \(\Ho\), \(\bar x\sim\Nc(A,\sigma^2/N)\):

\[ \PD=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-A}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right)=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\sqrt{\frac{NA^2}{\sigma^2}}\right). \] \[ \boxed{\ \PD=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\sqrt{d^2}\right),\qquad d^2=\frac{NA^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{\E}{\sigma^2}\ } \]

Check \(d^2\) against its definition: \(E[T;\Ho]-E[T;\Hz]=A\) and \(\mathrm{var}(T;\Hz)=\sigma^2/N\), so \(d^2=A^2/(\sigma^2/N)=NA^2/\sigma^2\). Here \(\E=NA^2\) is the total signal energy and \(\sigma^2\) the noise power.

  • Only the energy-to-noise ratio matters, not how the energy is spread in time. A large \(A\) with small \(N\) and a small \(A\) with large \(N\) perform the same if \(NA^2\) is the same.
  • \(d^2\) is usually quoted in dB, \(10\log_{10}d^2\). Doubling \(N\) adds 3 dB.
  • For fixed \(\PFA\), \(\PD\) increases with \(d^2\). For fixed \(d^2\), a smaller \(\PFA\) (larger \(Q^{-1}(\PFA)\)) gives a smaller \(\PD\).
Example (design question, Lec 4 p.12)

Solution. Invert the ROC: \(\sqrt{d^2}=Q^{-1}(\PFA)-Q^{-1}(\PD)\). With \(Q^{-1}(10^{-6})=4.753\) and \(Q^{-1}(0.99)=-2.326\),

\[ \sqrt{d^2}=4.753+2.326=7.080,\qquad d^2=50.1\ (17.0\text{ dB}),\qquad N\ge\frac{d^2}{A^2/\sigma^2}=\frac{50.12}{0.1}=501.2\quad\Rightarrow\quad N=502 . \]

The ROC, and three examples

An NP detector has one free choice: the false-alarm level. Each level gives one pair \((\PFA,\PD)\). The set of all such pairs is a curve, the receiver operating characteristic. It summarises everything about a detector's performance for a fixed data model. This chapter proves its properties, all of which exam questions have asked, and then works through the three examples of Lec 5: a change in variance, binomial data and exponential data.

5.1 Definition

Definition

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) is the curve traced by \((\PFA,\PD)\) as the threshold \(\gamma\) of the LRT sweeps from \(+\infty\) to \(0\) (or the threshold \(\gamma'\) on \(T\) sweeps from \(+\infty\) to \(-\infty\)), for a fixed data model.

Lec 4 p.15 · Lec 5 p.8

5.2 Properties of the ROC

Lec 4 p.17 lists six properties without proof. The slope property can be proved two ways, and Assignment 4, Q4 asked for concavity. Here are all six with proofs. Throughout, the ROC is that of the likelihood ratio test (the NP detector), with randomisation allowed where needed.

Properties 1, 2 and 5
  1. It passes through \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). As \(\gamma\to\infty\), the region \(R_1=\{L(\bx)>\gamma\}\) shrinks to nothing and \(\PFA=\PD=0\). As \(\gamma\to0\), \(R_1\) grows to the whole space and \(\PFA=\PD=1\).
  2. It is non-decreasing. If \(\gamma_1>\gamma_2\), then \(\{L>\gamma_1\}\subset\{L>\gamma_2\}\). A larger region has a larger probability under both hypotheses. So lowering the threshold moves the point up and right, never up and left.
  3. Perfect detection is the corner \((0,1)\). \(\PFA=0\) with \(\PD=1\). It is reached only when the two PDFs do not overlap.
Property 3: the ROC lies on or above the diagonal

For every \(\alpha\), the NP detector of size \(\alpha\) has \(\PD\ge\alpha\).

Proof. Consider the rule that ignores the data and decides \(\Ho\) with probability \(\alpha\): \(\delta'(\bx)=\alpha\) for all \(\bx\). Then

\[ \PFA(\delta')=\int\alpha\,p(\bx;\Hz)\,d\bx=\alpha,\qquad \PD(\delta')=\int\alpha\,p(\bx;\Ho)\,d\bx=\alpha . \]

This guessing rule has size \(\alpha\). By NP optimality, the NP rule does at least as well: \(\PD^{NP}(\alpha)\ge\PD(\delta')=\alpha\). ∎

So the diagonal \(\PD=\PFA\) is the performance of a coin toss, and no optimal detector can do worse. A detector whose curve dips below the diagonal is not using its data well: flipping its decisions would beat it.

Property 4: the ROC is concave (Assignment 4, Q4)

For any two false-alarm levels \(p_1,p_2\) and any \(\lambda\in[0,1]\),

\[ \PD\big(\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2\big)\ \ge\ \lambda\,\PD(p_1)+(1-\lambda)\,\PD(p_2), \]

where \(\PD(p)\) is the detection probability of the NP detector of size \(p\).

Proof. Let \(\delta_1\) and \(\delta_2\) be the NP rules of sizes \(p_1\) and \(p_2\). Build a randomised rule: toss a coin with \(\Pr\{\text{heads}\}=\lambda\); on heads use \(\delta_1\), on tails use \(\delta_2\). Its decision function is \(\delta(\bx)=\lambda\delta_1(\bx)+(1-\lambda)\delta_2(\bx)\). Since \(\PFA\) and \(\PD\) are linear in \(\delta\),

\[ \PFA(\delta)=\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2,\qquad \PD(\delta)=\lambda\PD(p_1)+(1-\lambda)\PD(p_2). \]

So the mixed rule sits on the straight chord between the two ROC points. It is a valid rule of size \(\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2\). The NP rule of that size is at least as good. Hence the ROC at \(\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2\) lies on or above the chord. A function that lies above all its chords is concave. ∎

Assignment 4 Q4 (= Kay Problem 3.13) · Lec 4 p.17
Property 6: the slope of the ROC equals the threshold

At the point produced by the LRT \(L(\bx)>\gamma\),

\[ \frac{d\PD}{d\PFA}=\gamma . \]

Proof. Treat \(\ell=L(\bx)\) as a random variable, with PDF \(p_L(\ell;\Hz)\) under \(\Hz\) and \(p_L(\ell;\Ho)\) under \(\Ho\).

Step 1: a lemma, \(p_L(\ell;\Ho)=\ell\,p_L(\ell;\Hz)\). For any set \(S\) of values,

\[ \Pr\{L\in S;\Ho\}=\int_{\{\bx:L(\bx)\in S\}}p(\bx;\Ho)\,d\bx=\int_{\{\bx:L(\bx)\in S\}}L(\bx)\,p(\bx;\Hz)\,d\bx =\int_S \ell\,p_L(\ell;\Hz)\,d\ell . \]

The middle step uses \(p(\bx;\Ho)=L(\bx)p(\bx;\Hz)\), the definition of \(L\). The last step is the change of variables \(\ell=L(\bx)\): averaging \(L\) over \(\bx\) under \(\Hz\) is the same as averaging \(\ell\) under its own \(\Hz\) distribution. Since \(\Pr\{L\in S;\Ho\}=\int_S p_L(\ell;\Ho)\,d\ell\) for every \(S\), the two densities agree.

Step 2: differentiate.

\[ \PFA(\gamma)=\int_\gamma^\infty p_L(\ell;\Hz)\,d\ell,\qquad \PD(\gamma)=\int_\gamma^\infty p_L(\ell;\Ho)\,d\ell=\int_\gamma^\infty\ell\,p_L(\ell;\Hz)\,d\ell . \]

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(\dfrac{d\PFA}{d\gamma}=-p_L(\gamma;\Hz)\) and \(\dfrac{d\PD}{d\gamma}=-\gamma\,p_L(\gamma;\Hz)\). By the chain rule,

\[ \frac{d\PD}{d\PFA}=\frac{d\PD/d\gamma}{d\PFA/d\gamma}=\frac{-\gamma\,p_L(\gamma;\Hz)}{-p_L(\gamma;\Hz)}=\gamma . \qquad\blacksquare \]
Lec 4 p.17 · Lec 5 p.9
Note

A second proof, without densities. The key inequality of the NP proof (section 4.3) says that for the NP rule \(\delta\) at threshold \(\gamma\) and any other rule \(\delta'\),

\[ \PD(\delta')\ \le\ \PD(\delta)+\gamma\big[\PFA(\delta')-\PFA(\delta)\big]. \]

So every achievable point lies on or below the straight line of slope \(\gamma\) through the NP point. That line touches the ROC at the NP point and stays above it everywhere else. It is the tangent, so the slope at that point is \(\gamma\) (where the ROC is smooth). The same argument proves concavity again: a curve that has a supporting line at every point is concave.

Example

Check the slope for the DC level. With \(z=Q^{-1}(\PFA)\) and \(d=\sqrt{d^2}\), the ROC is \(\PD=Q(z-d)\). Since \(dQ(z)/dz=-\phi(z)\), with \(\phi\) the standard normal PDF,

\[ \frac{d\PD}{d\PFA}=\frac{\phi(z-d)}{\phi(z)}=\exp\!\left(zd-\tfrac{d^2}{2}\right). \]

On the other hand, the threshold on \(\bar x\) is \(\gamma'=z\sigma/\sqrt N\), and from section 3.5, \(\ln\gamma=\frac{NA}{\sigma^2}\gamma'-\frac{NA^2}{2\sigma^2}=zd-\frac{d^2}{2}\). The two agree.

One consequence is useful. The minimum-\(P_e\) operating point is where the ROC has slope \(P_0/P_1\). With equal priors it is where the slope is 1.

5.3 Using the ROC

To design a detector from its ROC (Lec 4 p.16):

  1. The system specification fixes \(\alpha\), for example \(10^{-6}\).
  2. The required \(\PD\) fixes the operating point.
  3. Read off the required \(d^2\), then convert it to an SNR or a number of samples.

Two cautions. Comparing two detectors at different \(\PFA\) values means nothing; compare them at the same \(\PFA\), or compare whole curves. And the area under the ROC averages over operating points you may never use. In radar only the region \(\PFA\le10^{-4}\) matters.

5.4 Example: change in the variance

Both hypotheses have zero mean. Only the noise power changes:

\[ \Hz:\ x[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma_0^2),\qquad \Ho:\ x[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma_1^2),\qquad \sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2,\ \text{i.i.d.} \]
Derivation

The detector.

\[ L(\bx)=\frac{(2\pi\sigma_1^2)^{-N/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}\sum x^2[n]\right)}{(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-N/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}\sum x^2[n]\right)} =\left(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}\right)^{N/2}\exp\!\left[\frac12\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}-\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}\right)\sum x^2[n]\right]. \] \[ \ln L(\bx)=\frac N2\ln\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}+\frac12\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}-\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n]\ \dec\ \ln\gamma . \]

Since \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\), the bracket \(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}\) is positive. Divide by it without flipping:

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n]\ \dec\ \gamma'=\frac{2\ln\gamma-N\ln(\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2)}{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}-\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}}\ } \]

This is an energy detector. Under \(\mathcal{H}_i\), \(x[n]/\sigma_i\sim\Nc(0,1)\), so \(T/\sigma_i^2\sim\chi^2_N\). Therefore

\[ \PFA=\Pr\left\{\frac{T}{\sigma_0^2}>\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_0^2}\right\}=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_0^2}\right), \qquad \PD=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_1^2}\right). \]

Eliminate \(\gamma'\) using \(\gamma'=\sigma_0^2Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(\PFA)\):

\[ \boxed{\ \PD=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}\,Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(\PFA)\right)\ } \]

\(N=2\): a closed form. \(Q_{\chi^2_2}(y)=e^{-y/2}\), so \(\PFA=e^{-\gamma'/(2\sigma_0^2)}\), hence \(\gamma'=-2\sigma_0^2\ln\PFA\), and

\[ \PD=e^{-\gamma'/(2\sigma_1^2)}=e^{(\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2)\ln\PFA}=\PFA^{\ \sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2}. \]

Example

\(N=1\): a two-sided test. With one sample, \(T=x^2[0]>\gamma'\) means \(|x[0]|>\sqrt{\gamma'}\). The decision region \(R_1\) is the two tails of the real line, outside \([-\sqrt{\gamma'},\sqrt{\gamma'}]\). Then

\[ \PFA=2Q\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma'}}{\sigma_0}\right),\qquad \PD=2Q\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma'}}{\sigma_1}\right)=2Q\!\left(\frac{\sigma_0}{\sigma_1}\,Q^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\PFA}{2}\right)\right). \]

For \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_0^2=4\) and \(\PFA=0.1\): \(\PD=2Q(0.5\times1.645)=0.41\). With \(N=2\) the region is the outside of a circle of radius \(\sqrt{\gamma'}\) in the \((x[0],x[1])\) plane. This "unequal variances gives a two-sided region" pattern appeared in both earlier end-sems.

Note

The statistic \(\sum x^2[n]\) and the threshold \(\gamma'=\sigma_0^2Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(\PFA)\) do not involve \(\sigma_1^2\). Only the assumption \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\) was used. So the same detector is optimal for every \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\). That makes it a uniformly most powerful test, the idea of chapter 11 (and the homework on Lec 10 p.12).

(a) The detector, any \(n,p_0,p_1\). The binomial coefficients cancel:

\[ L(x)=\frac{\binom nx p_1^x(1-p_1)^{n-x}}{\binom nx p_0^x(1-p_0)^{n-x}} =\left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right)^{x}\left(\frac{1-p_1}{1-p_0}\right)^{n-x}. \] \[ \ln L(x)=x\,\underbrace{\ln\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}}_{\kappa}+n\ln\frac{1-p_1}{1-p_0}\ \dec\ \ln\gamma . \]

\[ \boxed{\ T(x)=x\ \dec\ \gamma'=\frac{\ln\gamma-n\ln\frac{1-p_1}{1-p_0}}{\kappa}\ } \]

Chapter 6

Irrelevant data and many hypotheses

Lecture 6 closed the first module with two extensions and a summary. The first extension asks when extra data can be thrown away. The second allows more than two hypotheses. Both reuse the tools of chapters 3 and 4 directly.

6.1 Irrelevant data

Suppose we observe \(\bx_1\), and someone offers extra measurements \(\bx_2\). When does \(\bx_2\) help? Factor the joint PDF as \(p(\bx_1,\bx_2)=p(\bx_2\mid\bx_1)\,p(\bx_1)\) under each hypothesis:

\[ L(\bx_1,\bx_2)=\frac{p(\bx_1,\bx_2;\Ho)}{p(\bx_1,\bx_2;\Hz)} =\frac{p(\bx_2\mid\bx_1;\Ho)}{p(\bx_2\mid\bx_1;\Hz)}\cdot\frac{p(\bx_1;\Ho)}{p(\bx_1;\Hz)} . \]
Condition for irrelevance

If

\[ p(\bx_2\mid\bx_1;\Ho)=p(\bx_2\mid\bx_1;\Hz)\quad\text{for all }\bx_1,\bx_2, \]

then the first factor is 1, \(L(\bx_1,\bx_2)=L(\bx_1)\), and \(\bx_2\) is irrelevant: the optimal detector ignores it. In words: once \(\bx_1\) is known, \(\bx_2\) says nothing more about which hypothesis holds.

Lec 6 (summary) p.5 · Kay §3.5, eq. (3.11)

A common special case: \(\bx_1\) and \(\bx_2\) are independent under both hypotheses, and \(p(\bx_2)\) is the same under both. Then \(p(\bx_2\mid\bx_1;\mathcal{H}_i)=p(\bx_2)\), and the condition holds.

Example

Samples outside the signal interval. Observe \(2N\) samples. \(\Hz: x[n]=w[n]\) for all \(n\). \(\Ho: x[n]=A+w[n]\) for \(n\lt N\), and \(x[n]=w[n]\) for \(n\ge N\). The noise is white. The second half is pure noise under both hypotheses and is independent of the first half. So it is irrelevant. In the likelihood ratio its factors appear identically on top and bottom and cancel.

Assignment 4 Q1 turns this around. There, the first half is \(A+w[n]\) under both hypotheses and only the second half differs (\(0\) vs \(2A\)). The first half is now irrelevant, and the detector sums only the second half. It is solved in chapter 13.

Common mistake

"\(\bx_2\) has the same distribution under both hypotheses" is not the test. The test is about the conditional distribution given \(\bx_1\). The next example has \(p(\bx_2;\Ho)=p(\bx_2;\Hz)\), yet \(\bx_2\) is very useful.

Example (Lec 6 summary p.6)

A correlated reference channel. A second sensor sees only noise, but its noise is correlated with the main sensor's noise:

\[ \Hz:\ x_1=N_1,\ x_2=N_2;\qquad \Ho:\ x_1=A+N_1,\ x_2=N_2;\qquad N_2=\rho N_1+W, \]

with \(N_1\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\) and \(W\sim\Nc(0,\sigma_W^2)\) independent. \(x_2=N_2\) has the same distribution under both hypotheses. But given \(x_1\),

\[ x_2\mid x_1;\Hz\ \sim\ \Nc(\rho x_1,\sigma_W^2),\qquad x_2\mid x_1;\Ho\ \sim\ \Nc(\rho(x_1-A),\sigma_W^2), \]

which differ. So \(x_2\) is relevant (if \(\rho\ne0\)).

How much does it help? \([x_1,x_2]^T\) is Gaussian with mean \([0,0]^T\) or \([A,0]^T\) and the same covariance under both hypotheses:

\[ \bC=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma^2 & \rho\sigma^2\\ \rho\sigma^2 & \rho^2\sigma^2+\sigma_W^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det\bC=\sigma^2\sigma_W^2 . \]

This is the multivariate example of section 3.6 with \(\Delta=[A,0]^T\). Its deflection is

\[ d^2=\Delta^T\bC^{-1}\Delta=A^2\,[\bC^{-1}]_{11}=A^2\,\frac{\rho^2\sigma^2+\sigma_W^2}{\sigma^2\sigma_W^2} =\frac{A^2}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\rho^2A^2}{\sigma_W^2}. \]

Without \(x_2\) we would have \(d^2=A^2/\sigma^2\). The second term is the gain from the reference channel. The statistic is \(T\propto x_1-\frac{\rho\sigma^2}{\rho^2\sigma^2+\sigma_W^2}x_2\): subtract the best estimate of \(N_1\) formed from \(x_2\). It is a noise canceller. With \(\rho=0.9\), \(\sigma^2=1\) and \(\sigma_W^2=0.19\) (so \(N_2\) also has unit variance), \(d^2=A^2(1+0.81/0.19)=5.26A^2\), a gain of 7.2 dB. If \(\sigma_W^2\to0\), the noise cancels exactly and detection becomes perfect.

6.2 Multiple hypotheses

Now there are \(M\) hypotheses \(\mathcal{H}_0,\dots,\mathcal{H}_{M-1}\), with priors \(P(\mathcal{H}_j)\) and costs \(C_{ij}\) (decide \(i\) when \(j\) is true). The detector partitions the data space into \(M\) regions \(R_0,\dots,R_{M-1}\). The Bayes risk is

\[ \mathcal{R}=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}C_{ij}\,\Pr\{\text{decide }\mathcal{H}_i\mid\mathcal{H}_j\}\,P(\mathcal{H}_j). \]
Homework (Lec 6 summary p.9)

Derive the \(M\)-ary minimum Bayes-risk rule. Then derive the \(M\)-ary MAP and ML detectors.

Theorem (\(M\)-ary minimum Bayes risk)

Define the posterior cost of deciding \(\mathcal{H}_i\),

\[ \mathcal{C}_i(\bx)=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}C_{ij}\,P(\mathcal{H}_j\mid\bx). \]

The rule that minimises \(\mathcal{R}\) decides \(\mathcal{H}_k\) with \(k=\arg\min_i\mathcal{C}_i(\bx)\).

Proof. Write each conditional probability as an integral: \(\Pr\{\text{decide }\mathcal{H}_i\mid\mathcal{H}_j\}=\int_{R_i}p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_j)\,d\bx\). Then

\[ \mathcal{R}=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\int_{R_i}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}C_{ij}\,P(\mathcal{H}_j)\,p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_j)\,d\bx =\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\int_{R_i}\mathcal{C}_i(\bx)\,p(\bx)\,d\bx, \]

using Bayes' rule \(P(\mathcal{H}_j)\,p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_j)=P(\mathcal{H}_j\mid\bx)\,p(\bx)\). Every \(\bx\) lies in exactly one region, and \(p(\bx)\ge0\). The sum is therefore smallest when each \(\bx\) is placed in the region whose \(\mathcal{C}_i(\bx)\) is smallest. Any other assignment replaces \(\mathcal{C}_k(\bx)\) by a larger value on some set of positive probability. ∎

Derivation

\(M\)-ary MAP. Take 0–1 costs, \(C_{ii}=0\) and \(C_{ij}=1\) for \(i\ne j\). Then \(\mathcal{R}=P_e\), and

\[ \mathcal{C}_i(\bx)=\sum_{j\ne i}P(\mathcal{H}_j\mid\bx)=1-P(\mathcal{H}_i\mid\bx). \]

Minimising \(\mathcal{C}_i\) means maximising the posterior:

\[ \hat k=\arg\max_i P(\mathcal{H}_i\mid\bx)=\arg\max_i\ p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i)P(\mathcal{H}_i) =\arg\max_i\big[\ln p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i)+\ln P(\mathcal{H}_i)\big]. \]

(\(p(\bx)\) does not depend on \(i\), so it can be dropped.)

\(M\)-ary ML. With equal priors \(P(\mathcal{H}_i)=1/M\), the prior term is the same for every \(i\):

\[ \hat k=\arg\max_i\ p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i). \]

For \(M\) hypotheses it is easier to compute the probability of a correct decision, \(P_c=\sum_iP(\mathcal{H}_i)\int_{R_i}p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i)\,d\bx\), and then \(P_e=1-P_c\). There are \(M(M-1)\) error types but only \(M\) correct ones.

6.3 Example: three DC levels

Homework / Tutorial (Lec 6 summary p.10)

\(\mathcal{H}_0:x[n]=-A+w[n]\), \(\mathcal{H}_1:x[n]=w[n]\), \(\mathcal{H}_2:x[n]=A+w[n]\), \(A>0\), WGN with variance \(\sigma^2\). Derive the Bayesian detector and its performance, in two ways: using the likelihoods, and using a sufficient statistic.

Derivation

Approach 1: the likelihoods. With \(A_0=-A\), \(A_1=0\), \(A_2=A\),

\[ p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{D_i^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],\qquad D_i^2=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x[n]-A_i)^2 . \]

For equal priors (ML), maximising \(p(\bx\mid\mathcal{H}_i)\) means minimising \(D_i^2\). Add and subtract \(\bar x\):

\[ D_i^2=\sum(x[n]-\bar x)^2+2(\bar x-A_i)\underbrace{\sum(x[n]-\bar x)}_{=0}+N(\bar x-A_i)^2 . \]

The first term is the same for every \(i\). So choose the level \(A_i\) closest to \(\bar x\):

\[ \text{decide }\mathcal{H}_0\text{ if }\bar x\lt-\tfrac A2,\qquad \mathcal{H}_1\text{ if }-\tfrac A2\lt\bar x\lt\tfrac A2,\qquad \mathcal{H}_2\text{ if }\bar x>\tfrac A2 . \]

This is a minimum-distance receiver (chapter 9), here for 3-level pulse amplitude modulation.

Approach 2: the sufficient statistic. By section 1.6, \(\bar x\) is sufficient for the mean, so the problem reduces to three hypotheses on one scalar, \(\bar x\mid\mathcal{H}_i\sim\Nc(A_i,v)\) with \(v=\sigma^2/N\). The ML rule on \(\bar x\) picks the closest mean, which gives the same thresholds \(\pm A/2\) with far less algebra.

Chapter 7

Known signal in white noise

Module 2 starts here. The receiver knows the exact waveform it is looking for: its shape, its amplitude and its timing. This is coherent detection. Examples are a radar with a known transmitted pulse, a communication receiver locked to the carrier, and a synchronised spread-spectrum receiver. The optimal detector correlates the data with a stored copy of the signal. It can be built as a filter, the matched filter.

7.1 The problem

\[ \Hz:\ x[n]=w[n],\qquad \Ho:\ x[n]=s[n]+w[n],\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1, \]

with \(s[n]\) completely known and \(w[n]\) WGN with variance \(\sigma^2\). The signal energy is \(\E=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}s^2[n]=\bs^T\bs\). In vector form,

\[ \Hz:\ \bx\sim\Nc(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\bI),\qquad \Ho:\ \bx\sim\Nc(\bs,\sigma^2\bI). \]

The slide asks: have we studied this before? Yes. It is the two-Gaussian problem of section 3.6, with \(\bmu_0=\mathbf{0}\), \(\bmu_1=\bs\) and \(\bC=\sigma^2\bI\).

7.2 The replica-correlator

Derivation

The normalising constants cancel in the likelihood ratio:

\[ \ln L(\bx)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[(\bx-\bs)^T(\bx-\bs)-\bx^T\bx\Big] =-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[-2\bx^T\bs+\bs^T\bs\Big] =\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\bx^T\bs-\frac{\E}{2\sigma^2}\ \dec\ \ln\gamma . \]

Multiply by \(\sigma^2>0\):

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,s[n]=\bx^T\bs\ \dec\ \gamma'=\sigma^2\ln\gamma+\frac{\E}{2}\ } \]

The detector multiplies the data by a stored replica of the signal and adds up the products. That is why it is called the replica-correlator or correlation detector.

Distribution of \(T\). \(T=\bs^T\bx\) is linear in Gaussian data. With \(\bh=\bs\) in section 1.3:

\[ T\mid\Hz\sim\Nc(0,\ \sigma^2\bs^T\bs)=\Nc(0,\sigma^2\E),\qquad T\mid\Ho\sim\Nc(\bs^T\bs,\ \sigma^2\E)=\Nc(\E,\sigma^2\E). \]

NP performance. \(\PFA=Q(\gamma'/\sqrt{\sigma^2\E})\), so \(\gamma'=\sqrt{\sigma^2\E}\,Q^{-1}(\PFA)\), and

\[ \boxed{\ \PD=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\sqrt{\frac{\E}{\sigma^2}}\right),\qquad d^2=\frac{\E}{\sigma^2}=\frac{\text{signal energy}}{\text{noise power}}\ } \]

Bayes, equal priors. \(\gamma=1\) gives \(\gamma'=\E/2\), halfway between the two means, and \(P_e=Q\big(\tfrac12\sqrt{\E/\sigma^2}\big)\).

Common mistake

It is easy to put the \(\Hz\) exponent in the numerator of \(L(\bx)\). Then the middle lines have the wrong sign (\(\E-2\bs^T\bx\) instead of \(2\bs^T\bx-\E\)), and the final result is right only if the sign is quietly fixed at the end. Keep \(p(\bx;\Ho)\) on top.

7.3 The central result of coherent detection

The performance depends on the signal only through its energy. The shape, the bandwidth and the waveform do not matter. A chirp, a sinusoid and a DC level with the same energy are detected equally well in white Gaussian noise. So in WGN the waveform is chosen for other reasons (range resolution, spectrum use), not for detection. This changes completely when the noise is coloured (chapter 8).

An earlier end-sem asked, true or false: "When the noise is white Gaussian, deterministic signal detector performance only depends on signal energy and not on the signal shape." True, for the reason above.

Note

Processing gain. For a DC level, the input SNR per sample is \(A^2/\sigma^2\) and the output deflection is \(d^2=NA^2/\sigma^2\). The detector gains a factor \(N\), or \(10\log_{10}N\) dB, by adding \(N\) samples coherently: the signal adds in amplitude while independent noise adds only in power.

7.4 The matched filter

The correlator can also be built as a filter. Pass \(x[n]\) through an FIR filter with impulse response \(h[n]\) and sample the output at one time. The output is

\[ y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}h[n-k]\,x[k]. \]
Derivation

When does the filter equal the correlator? Choose the time-reversed signal as the impulse response,

\[ h[k]=\begin{cases}s[N-1-k], & k=0,1,\dots,N-1\\ 0, & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \]

and sample at \(n=N-1\). Then \(h[N-1-k]=s[N-1-(N-1-k)]=s[k]\), so

\[ y[N-1]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h[N-1-k]\,x[k]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}s[k]\,x[k]=T(\bx). \]

This filter is the matched filter. Its impulse response is "matched" to the signal: it is the signal played backwards.

Lec 6 (known signal-1) p.6–7
Homework / Tutorial (Lec 6 known signal-1 p.7)

Show that the signal output of the matched filter is largest at \(n=N-1\), and that the largest value is \(\E\).

Proof. With input \(s[n]\) (zero outside \(0\le n\le N-1\)),

\[ y_s[n]=\sum_k s[k]\,h[n-k]=\sum_k s[k]\,s[N-1-n+k]=r_{ss}[m],\qquad m=N-1-n, \]

where \(r_{ss}[m]=\sum_k s[k]s[k+m]\) is the (deterministic) autocorrelation of the signal. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\[ |r_{ss}[m]|=\Big|\sum_k s[k]\,s[k+m]\Big|\le\sqrt{\sum_k s^2[k]}\sqrt{\sum_k s^2[k+m]}\le\sqrt{\E}\sqrt{\E}=\E . \]

(The second sum is over a shifted copy of the signal, so it is at most \(\E\).) At \(m=0\), \(r_{ss}[0]=\sum_k s^2[k]=\E\), so the bound is attained. \(m=0\) means \(n=N-1\). So the output is largest when sampled at \(n=N-1\), where it equals the signal energy. ∎

Proof: in class (Lec 6 known signal-1 p.7)

Property. Among all FIR filters sampled at \(n=N-1\), the matched filter maximises the output signal-to-noise ratio.

Proof. Under \(\Ho\), the output at \(n=N-1\) splits into a signal part and a noise part:

\[ y[N-1]=\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h[N-1-k]\,s[k]}_{y_s}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h[N-1-k]\,w[k]}_{y_w}. \]

Define the output SNR as the signal power over the noise power, \(\eta=y_s^2/E[y_w^2]\). For WGN, \(E[w[k]w[l]]=\sigma^2\delta[k-l]\), so the cross terms vanish:

\[ E[y_w^2]=\sum_k\sum_l h[N-1-k]\,h[N-1-l]\,E[w[k]w[l]]=\sigma^2\sum_{k}h^2[N-1-k]. \]

Write \(g[k]=h[N-1-k]\), the reversed filter. Then

\[ \eta=\frac{\left(\sum_k g[k]\,s[k]\right)^2}{\sigma^2\sum_k g^2[k]} \le\frac{\left(\sum_k g^2[k]\right)\left(\sum_k s^2[k]\right)}{\sigma^2\sum_k g^2[k]}=\frac{\E}{\sigma^2}, \]

by Cauchy–Schwarz. Equality holds if and only if \(g[k]=c\,s[k]\) for some constant \(c\ne0\), that is, \(h[k]=c\,s[N-1-k]\). That is the matched filter (up to a gain, which changes neither the SNR nor the detector once the threshold is scaled with it). The maximum SNR is \(\E/\sigma^2=d^2\). ∎

Common mistake

It is easy to conclude that the constant \(c\) has to be 1. It does not. Any non-zero \(c\) gives equality in Cauchy–Schwarz, because the SNR does not change when \(h\) is scaled. \(c=1\) is just the convenient choice that makes \(y[N-1]=\bx^T\bs\) exactly.

Note

Frequency-domain view (Kay §4.3). The matched filter's frequency response is \(H(f)=S^*(f)e^{-j2\pi f(N-1)}\). It has the same magnitude as the signal spectrum: it passes the frequencies where the signal is strong and suppresses the others, which is exactly what maximises SNR in white noise. The phase term lines up all the frequency components at \(n=N-1\).

Caution. All of this assumes the signal starts at \(n=0\). If the arrival time is unknown, the receiver runs the matched filter continuously and looks for the peak; that is a composite problem (chapter 11).

7.5 Example: an exponential signal (Assignment 4, Q3)

Example

Detect \(s[n]=Ar^n\), \(n=0,\dots,N-1\), in WGN with variance \(\sigma^2\). Find the NP detector, its performance, and what happens as \(N\to\infty\) for \(0\lt r\lt1\), \(r=1\) and \(r>1\).

Detector. The replica-correlator: \(T=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,Ar^n>\gamma'\), with \(\gamma'=\sqrt{\sigma^2\E}\,Q^{-1}(\alpha)\). (For \(A>0\), it is enough to compute \(\sum x[n]r^n\) and compare it with \(\gamma'/A\); if \(A\lt0\), the inequality flips.)

Energy. A geometric series:

\[ \E=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}A^2r^{2n}=\begin{cases}A^2\dfrac{1-r^{2N}}{1-r^2}, & r\ne1\\ NA^2, & r=1.\end{cases} \qquad \PD=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\alpha)-\sqrt{\E/\sigma^2}\right). \]

Limits.

  • \(0\lt r\lt1\): the signal dies out. \(\E\to A^2/(1-r^2)\), a finite limit, so \(\PD\) saturates at \(Q\big(Q^{-1}(\alpha)-\sqrt{A^2/(\sigma^2(1-r^2))}\big)\lt1\). Collecting more samples eventually adds only noise; those late samples are nearly irrelevant.
  • \(r=1\): a DC level. \(\E=NA^2\) grows linearly, so \(\PD\to1\).
  • \(r>1\): the signal grows. \(\E\) grows exponentially, and \(\PD\to1\) very fast.

Chapter 8

Known signal in coloured noise

Real noise is often not white. Its samples are correlated, or some samples are noisier than others. The detector then has to weight the data before correlating, and the signal shape starts to matter. This chapter derives the generalised matched filter, interprets it as "whiten, then match", and shows how to design the best signal for a given noise.

8.1 The generalised matched filter

\[ \Hz:\ \bx=\bw,\qquad \Ho:\ \bx=\bs+\bw,\qquad \bw\sim\Nc(\mathbf{0},\bC), \]

with \(\bC\) known, symmetric, positive definite, and not necessarily diagonal.

Derivation

This is section 3.6 with \(\bmu_0=\mathbf{0}\) and \(\bmu_1=\bs\). The log-likelihood ratio is

\[ \ln L(\bx)=-\tfrac12\Big[(\bx-\bs)^T\bC^{-1}(\bx-\bs)-\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bx\Big] =\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bs-\tfrac12\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\ \dec\ \ln\gamma, \]

using \(\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bx=\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bs\) (symmetry). So

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bs\ \dec\ \gamma'=\ln\gamma+\tfrac12\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\ } \]

Performance. \(T=\bh^T\bx\) with \(\bh=\bC^{-1}\bs\). Its variance is \(\bh^T\bC\bh=\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bC\bC^{-1}\bs=\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\), under both hypotheses. Its mean is \(0\) under \(\Hz\) and \(\bh^T\bs=\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\) under \(\Ho\). By section 1.3,

\[ \boxed{\ \PD=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\sqrt{d^2}\right),\qquad d^2=\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\ } \]

For white noise, \(\bC=\sigma^2\bI\) and \(d^2=\E/\sigma^2\), the result of chapter 7.

Lec 7 p.3 · Kay §4.4

8.2 Example: independent samples with unequal variances

Let \(\bC=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_0^2,\dots,\sigma_{N-1}^2)\). Then \(\bC^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_n^2)\) and

\[ T(\bx)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{x[n]\,s[n]}{\sigma_n^2}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\underbrace{\frac{x[n]}{\sigma_n}}_{x'[n]}\,\underbrace{\frac{s[n]}{\sigma_n}}_{s'[n]}, \qquad d^2=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{s^2[n]}{\sigma_n^2}. \]

Noisy samples get small weights. Dividing each sample by its noise standard deviation makes the noise white with unit variance; this is the "equalisation of noise samples" on the slide. After that, the detector is an ordinary correlator with the scaled signal \(s'[n]\).

8.3 Prewhitening

The diagonal example generalises. Any coloured Gaussian noise can be turned into white noise by a linear transformation. Then the white-noise detector applies.

Derivation

\(\bC^{-1}\) is symmetric positive definite, so it can be factored as \(\bC^{-1}=\bD^T\bD\) with \(\bD\) invertible. Two standard choices:

  • Eigendecomposition: \(\bC=\bV\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bV^T\) with \(\bV\) orthogonal. Then \(\bC^{-1}=\bV\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\bV^T\), and \(\bD=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\bV^T\) works: \(\bD^T\bD=\bV\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\bV^T=\bC^{-1}\).
  • Cholesky factorisation of \(\bC\) itself: \(\bC=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\) with \(\mathbf{L}\) lower triangular, and \(\bD=\mathbf{L}^{-1}\), also lower triangular. Then \(\bD^T\bD=(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T)^{-1}=\bC^{-1}\), and each whitened sample uses only present and past data: a causal filter (the AR(1) filter of section 8.6 is of this kind). Factoring \(\bC^{-1}\) directly by Cholesky would instead give an upper-triangular, anti-causal \(\bD\).

Define \(\bx'=\bD\bx\), \(\bs'=\bD\bs\), \(\bw'=\bD\bw\). The transformed noise is white:

\[ E[\bw'\bw'^T]=\bD\,E[\bw\bw^T]\,\bD^T=\bD\bC\bD^T=\bI, \]

because \(\bD\bC\bD^T=\bD(\bD^T\bD)^{-1}\bD^T=\bD\bD^{-1}\bD^{-T}\bD^T=\bI\). And the statistic is a plain correlation in the new coordinates:

\[ T(\bx)=\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bs=\bx^T\bD^T\bD\bs=(\bD\bx)^T(\bD\bs)=\bx'^T\bs',\qquad d^2=\bs'^T\bs'=\|\bD\bs\|^2 . \]
Lec 7 p.5 · Kay §4.4

So the generalised matched filter has a two-stage structure: a prewhitener \(\bD\), followed by a replica-correlator matched to the prewhitened signal \(\bs'\). This is the structure used in practice. The performance is set by the energy of the whitened signal, which is why the signal shape now matters.

8.4 Signal design in coloured noise

We can choose \(\bs\) subject to an energy budget \(\bs^T\bs=\E\). To maximise \(\PD\), maximise \(d^2=\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\).

Derivation

Diagonal \(\bC\). Maximise \(\sum s^2[n]/\sigma_n^2\) subject to \(\sum s^2[n]=\E\). With a Lagrange multiplier \(\mu\) (called \(\mu\) here so it is not confused with the eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) below), \(\mathcal{L}=\sum s^2[n]/\sigma_n^2-\mu(\sum s^2[n]-\E)\), and

\[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial s[k]}=2s[k]\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_k^2}-\mu\right)=0 . \]

For each \(k\), either \(s[k]=0\) or \(\mu=1/\sigma_k^2\). Write \(d^2=\E\sum_n\frac{s^2[n]}{\E}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_n^2}\): it is \(\E\) times a weighted average of the \(1/\sigma_n^2\) with weights \(s^2[n]/\E\), so it is largest when all the weight sits on the largest \(1/\sigma_n^2\). The optimal signal is a single spike, \(s[k^*]=\pm\sqrt\E\) at \(k^*=\arg\min_n\sigma_n^2\), and zero elsewhere, with \(d^2=\E/\sigma^2_{\min}\).

General \(\bC\). Setting the gradient of \(\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs-\mu(\bs^T\bs-\E)\) to zero gives \(\bC^{-1}\bs=\mu\bs\): \(\bs\) must be an eigenvector. To see which one, expand \(\bs=\sum_i a_i\mathbf{v}_i\) in the orthonormal eigenvectors of \(\bC\) (eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\)). Then \(\bs^T\bs=\sum a_i^2=\E\) and

\[ d^2=\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs=\sum_i\frac{a_i^2}{\lambda_i}\ \le\ \frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}}\sum_ia_i^2=\frac{\E}{\lambda_{\min}}, \]

with equality when all the energy is on the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue:

\[ \boxed{\ \bs_{\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt{\E}\,\mathbf{v}_{\min},\qquad d^2_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{\E}{\lambda_{\min}(\bC)}\ } \]
Lec 7 p.7 · Kay §4.4

The engineering rule on the slide: put all the signal energy where the noise is weakest. "Where" means the sample, the direction in \(\mathbb{R}^N\), or (for stationary noise) the frequency band with the least noise power.

Common mistake

Lec 7 p.7 writes the diagonal example as \(\bC=\mathrm{diag}\{1/\sigma_0^2,\dots\}\). That is \(\bC^{-1}\); \(\bC\) itself is \(\mathrm{diag}\{\sigma_0^2,\dots\}\) (as on p.4). And note the eigenvector is for the smallest eigenvalue of \(\bC\), which is the largest eigenvalue of \(\bC^{-1}\).

8.5 Example: two correlated noise samples

Homework / Tutorial (Lec 7 p.8)

\(N=2\), \(\bC=\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(|\rho|\lt1\). Design the optimal signal shape by equalising the noise samples. What happens as \(\rho\to1\)?

Derivation

Eigenstructure. \(\det(\bC-\lambda\bI)=(1-\lambda)^2-\rho^2=0\) gives \(\lambda=1\pm\rho\). The eigenvectors are

\[ \mathbf{v}_+=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}\ (\lambda=1+\rho),\qquad \mathbf{v}_-=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\ (\lambda=1-\rho). \]

The "equalisation" view: rotate the data onto these axes, \(u_\pm=(x[0]\pm x[1])/\sqrt2\). In those coordinates the noise components are independent with variances \(1\pm\rho\). Dividing each by its standard deviation whitens the noise (\(\bD=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\bV^T\)).

The detector for any signal. \(\bC^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\rho\\-\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\), so

\[ T(\bx)=\bx^T\bC^{-1}\bs=\frac{x[0]s[0]+x[1]s[1]-\rho\big(x[0]s[1]+x[1]s[0]\big)}{1-\rho^2}, \qquad d^2=\frac{s^2[0]+s^2[1]-2\rho\,s[0]s[1]}{1-\rho^2}. \]

The optimal signal. Put the energy on the eigenvector with the smaller eigenvalue \(1-|\rho|\):

  • \(0\lt\rho\lt1\): \(\bs_{\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt{\E/2}\,[1,\,-1]^T\), \(d^2_{\mathrm{opt}}=\E/(1-\rho)\).
  • \(-1\lt\rho\lt0\): \(\bs_{\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt{\E/2}\,[1,\,1]^T\), \(d^2_{\mathrm{opt}}=\E/(1+\rho)\).

As \(\rho\to1\). \(d^2_{\mathrm{opt}}\to\infty\). The two noise samples become identical, \(w[1]=w[0]\). With \(\bs\propto[1,-1]^T\) the detector forms \(x[0]-x[1]\): the noise cancels exactly and only the signal difference remains. Detection becomes perfect. A signal that puts its energy on \([1,1]^T\) (the direction where the noise is strongest) gets \(d^2=\E/(1+\rho)\to\E/2\) instead.

8.6 Example: AR(1) noise

Homework / Tutorial (Lec 7 p.9)

\(w[n]=a\,w[n-1]+u[n]\), \(u[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma_u^2)\) white, \(|a|\lt1\). Design the whitening filter. What happens as \(a\to1\)?

Derivation

The whitening filter. Rearrange the model: \(u[n]=w[n]-a\,w[n-1]\). The first-order FIR filter

\[ \mathcal{D}(z)=1-a\,z^{-1},\qquad x'[n]=x[n]-a\,x[n-1], \]

turns the AR(1) noise back into its white driving noise \(u[n]\). Apply it to data and signal alike: \(s'[n]=s[n]-a\,s[n-1]\). Ignoring the first sample (edge effects), the detector and its deflection are

\[ T(\bx)=\frac{1}{\sigma_u^2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}x'[n]\,s'[n],\qquad d^2=\frac{1}{\sigma_u^2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\big(s[n]-a\,s[n-1]\big)^2 . \]

This matches the tridiagonal \(\bC^{-1}\) on the slide. If \(\bD\) is the matrix with 1 on the diagonal and \(-a\) just below it, scaled by \(1/\sigma_u\), then \(\bD^T\bD\) has \(1+a^2\) on the diagonal and \(-a\) beside it, which is \(\bC^{-1}\) up to the corner (edge) entries.

As \(a\to1\). The noise PSD is

\[ P_w(f)=\frac{\sigma_u^2}{|1-a\,e^{-j2\pi f}|^2}=\frac{\sigma_u^2}{1-2a\cos2\pi f+a^2}. \]

As \(a\to1\), this becomes huge near \(f=0\) (it equals \(\sigma_u^2/(1-a)^2\) there) and stays moderate near \(f=\pm\tfrac12\) (where it equals \(\sigma_u^2/(1+a)^2\)). The noise becomes a slowly wandering, almost-DC process. The whitening filter becomes a differencer, \(x[n]-x[n-1]\), which removes slow components. A signal with its energy near DC is then wiped out together with the noise. For a DC level, \(s'[n]=A(1-a)\) and \(d^2=(N-1)A^2(1-a)^2/\sigma_u^2\to0\). A signal that alternates quickly (energy near \(f=\tfrac12\)) survives the differencer and is detected easily. This is the frequency version of "put the energy where the noise is weakest".

Binary and M-ary signals

In communication there is always a signal. The question is which one. A binary system sends \(\bs_0\) or \(\bs_1\); an \(M\)-ary system sends one of \(M\) known waveforms. Priors are known and the errors cost the same, so the Bayesian minimum-\(P_e\) criterion is the natural one. The result is the minimum-distance receiver.

9.1 Which of two known signals was sent?

\[ \Hz:\ x[n]=s_0[n]+w[n],\qquad \Ho:\ x[n]=s_1[n]+w[n],\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1, \]

with known signals of energies \(\E_0\) and \(\E_1\), and WGN with variance \(\sigma^2\).

Derivation

\(p(\bx;\mathcal{H}_i)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}\exp\!\big[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|\bx-\bs_i\|^2\big]\). So

\[ \ln L(\bx)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[\|\bx-\bs_1\|^2-\|\bx-\bs_0\|^2\Big] =-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[-2\bx^T(\bs_1-\bs_0)+\E_1-\E_0\Big]\ \dec\ \ln\gamma, \]

because \(\|\bx-\bs_i\|^2=\bx^T\bx-2\bx^T\bs_i+\E_i\) and the \(\bx^T\bx\) terms cancel. Hence

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\bx^T(\bs_1-\bs_0)\ \dec\ \gamma'=\sigma^2\ln\gamma+\frac{\E_1-\E_0}{2}\ } \]

For equal priors (\(\gamma=1\)), go back one line: the test is \(\|\bx-\bs_0\|^2-\|\bx-\bs_1\|^2\dec0\), that is,

\[ \text{decide }\Ho\ \text{ if }\ \|\bx-\bs_1\|^2\lt\|\bx-\bs_0\|^2 . \]

Choose the signal closest to the data. This is the minimum-distance receiver.

Lec 8 p.3 · Kay §4.5

9.2 Geometry

  • The boundary \(\|\bx-\bs_1\|=\|\bx-\bs_0\|\) is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the two signals.
  • Unequal priors or costs change \(\gamma'\) only. The boundary \(\bx^T(\bs_1-\bs_0)=\gamma'\) stays perpendicular to \(\bs_1-\bs_0\); it translates along that direction and never rotates.
  • The detector projects \(\bx\) onto the difference \(\bs_1-\bs_0\). Every component of \(\bx\) orthogonal to that direction is irrelevant data (chapter 6). So the \(N\)-dimensional problem is really one-dimensional, whatever \(N\) is.
Lec 8 p.4

9.3 Probability of error

Derivation

Let \(\Delta=\bs_1-\bs_0\), so \(T=\Delta^T\bx\). By section 1.3, with \(\bC=\sigma^2\bI\),

\[ T\mid\mathcal{H}_i\sim\Nc\!\left(\Delta^T\bs_i,\ \sigma^2\|\Delta\|^2\right). \]

With equal priors, \(\gamma'=(\E_1-\E_0)/2\). Notice that

\[ \frac{\Delta^T\bs_0+\Delta^T\bs_1}{2}=\frac{(\bs_1-\bs_0)^T(\bs_1+\bs_0)}{2}=\frac{\E_1-\E_0}{2}=\gamma' . \]

The threshold sits exactly halfway between the two means. The distance from either mean to the threshold is \(\Delta^T(\bs_1-\bs_0)/2=\|\Delta\|^2/2\), and the standard deviation is \(\sigma\|\Delta\|\). So both conditional errors equal \(Q\big(\frac{\|\Delta\|^2/2}{\sigma\|\Delta\|}\big)\), and

\[ \boxed{\ P_e=Q\!\left(\frac{\|\bs_1-\bs_0\|}{2\sigma}\right)=Q\!\left(\tfrac12\sqrt{d^2}\right),\qquad d^2=\frac{\|\bs_1-\bs_0\|^2}{\sigma^2}\ } \]

This holds for any energies, not only equal ones. (The slide takes equal energies, where \(\gamma'=0\) and the two means are \(\pm(\E-\bs_1^T\bs_0)\).)

Lec 8 p.5

The design rule follows at once. \(P_e\) depends only on the distance between the two signals, not on where they are. To signal well, make the two waveforms as different as possible for the energy you can spend.

Common mistake

It is easy to sketch the two PDFs of \(T\) with \(T\mid\Ho\) on the left and \(T\mid\Hz\) on the right. It is the other way round: \(T=\Delta^T\bx\) is larger under \(\Ho\), so \(T\mid\Ho\) is the right-hand curve, with the larger mean \(\Delta^T\bs_1\).

Which \(\rho\) gives the smallest \(P_e\)? \(Q\) is decreasing, so we want the largest \(1-\rho\), that is, \(\rho=-1\). Antipodal signalling is optimal. 9.6 M-ary signals and the union bound

With \(M\) equally likely known signals \(\bs_0,\dots,\bs_{M-1}\) in WGN, the ML rule (section 6.2) picks the largest likelihood, which is the nearest signal:

\[ \hat k=\arg\min_i\|\bx-\bs_i\|^2=\arg\max_i\left[\bx^T\bs_i-\frac{\E_i}{2}\right], \]

since \(\|\bx-\bs_i\|^2=\bx^T\bx-2(\bx^T\bs_i-\E_i/2)\) and \(\bx^T\bx\) is common to all \(i\). The receiver is a bank of \(M\) correlators, each corrected by half its signal energy, followed by "pick the largest".

Lec 8 p.11

The decision regions are the Voronoi cells of the constellation: each cell is the set of points closer to one signal than to any other. These are polytopes, and the exact \(P_e\) requires integrating a Gaussian over a polytope. There is no closed form in general, so we bound it.

Theorem (union bound)

With \(d_{ij}=\|\bs_i-\bs_j\|\) and \(d_{\min}=\min_{i\ne j}d_{ij}\),

\[ P_e\ \le\ \frac1M\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\sum_{j\ne i}Q\!\left(\frac{d_{ij}}{2\sigma}\right)\ \le\ (M-1)\,Q\!\left(\frac{d_{\min}}{2\sigma}\right). \]

Proof. Suppose \(\bs_i\) was sent. An error means \(\bx\) is closer to some other signal: the error event is \(\bigcup_{j\ne i}E_{ij}\), where \(E_{ij}=\{\|\bx-\bs_j\|\lt\|\bx-\bs_i\|\}\). The probability of a union is at most the sum of the probabilities (the union bound). Each \(E_{ij}\) is exactly the error event of a binary minimum-distance test between \(\bs_i\) and \(\bs_j\), so by section 9.3, \(\Pr(E_{ij})=Q(d_{ij}/2\sigma)\). Hence

\[ \Pr\{\text{error}\mid\bs_i\}\le\sum_{j\ne i}Q\!\left(\frac{d_{ij}}{2\sigma}\right). \]

Average over the \(M\) equally likely signals to get the first inequality. For the second, every \(d_{ij}\ge d_{\min}\) and \(Q\) is decreasing, so each term is at most \(Q(d_{\min}/2\sigma)\), and each inner sum has \(M-1\) terms. ∎

Lec 8 p.12

At high SNR the bound is tight, and the smallest distances dominate: \(P_e\approx N_{\min}Q(d_{\min}/2\sigma)\), where \(N_{\min}\) is the average number of nearest neighbours. Constellation design means maximising the minimum distance for a given average energy.

Example

Two checks. (1) \(M\) orthogonal signals of energy \(\E\): every pair has \(d_{ij}^2=2\E\), so \(P_e\le(M-1)Q\big(\sqrt{\E/2\sigma^2}\big)\). For \(M=2\) this is the exact BFSK result. (2) The 3-level PAM of section 6.3, on the statistic \(\bar x\) with standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt N\): adjacent levels are \(A\) apart and the outer pair \(2A\) apart. Write \(a=\sqrt{NA^2/4\sigma^2}\). The level \(-A\) contributes \(Q(a)+Q(2a)\), the level \(0\) contributes \(2Q(a)\), and the level \(+A\) contributes \(Q(a)+Q(2a)\). So

\[ P_e\le\tfrac13\big[4Q(a)+2Q(2a)\big]=\tfrac43Q(a)+\tfrac23Q(2a). \]

The exact answer from chapter 6 is \(\frac43Q(a)\). The bound is off only by the tiny far-pair term \(\frac23Q(2a)\).

Chapter 10

Random signals

This chapter covers Lecture 9 (12 November). It is built from the slides (Lec 9), the board work in class, and Kay chapter 5.

Until now the signal was known exactly. Many signals are not. A fading radio channel, a fluctuating radar target, speech, a seismic tremor or an EEG trace is better modelled as a realisation of a random process than as a fixed waveform. We know its statistics (its covariance), not its values. The detector must decide whether such a process is present in the noise.

10.1 The model

\[ \Hz:\ \bx=\bw,\qquad \Ho:\ \bx=\bs+\bw,\qquad \bs\sim\Nc(\mathbf{0},\bC_s),\quad \bw\sim\Nc(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\bI), \]

with \(\bs\) and \(\bw\) independent. Under \(\Ho\) the data are a sum of two independent zero-mean Gaussians, so \(\bx\sim\Nc(\mathbf{0},\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)\). The professor split this into two setups:

  1. i.i.d. signal + i.i.d. noise: \(\bC_s=\sigma_s^2\bI\). Then \(\Hz:\Nc(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\bI)\) vs \(\Ho:\Nc(\mathbf{0},(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)\bI)\).
  2. non-i.i.d. signal + i.i.d. noise: general \(\bC_s\). Then \(\Hz:\Nc(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\bI)\) vs \(\Ho:\Nc(\mathbf{0},\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)\).
Note

The key structural change. Both hypotheses now have zero mean. All the information is in the covariance. The log-likelihood ratio is \(\tfrac12\bx^T\big[\bC_0^{-1}-\bC_1^{-1}\big]\bx+\text{const}\), with \(\bC_0=\sigma^2\bI\) and \(\bC_1=\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI\): a quadratic form, so the optimal detector is quadratic in the data. A linear statistic \(\bh^T\bx\) has zero mean under both hypotheses; it differs only in variance, which a one-sided threshold on it uses badly. Squaring (or a full quadratic form) captures the change in power in every direction at once.

10.2 Setup 1: a white signal and the energy detector

Derivation (board, 12 Nov) \[ p(\bx;\Hz)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n]},\qquad p(\bx;\Ho)=\frac{1}{(2\pi(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2))^{N/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)}\sum x^2[n]} . \] \[ \ln L(\bx)=\frac N2\ln\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+\sigma_s^2}+\frac12\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}-\frac{1}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}\right)\sum x^2[n] =\frac N2\ln\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+\sigma_s^2}+\frac{\sigma_s^2}{2\sigma^2(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)}\,T(\bx), \]

Derivation (board, 12 Nov) \[ p(\bx;\Ho)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\det^{1/2}(\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)}\exp\!\left[-\tfrac12\bx^T(\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}\bx\right],\quad p(\bx;\Hz)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\bx^T\bx\right]. \]

10.4 The estimator–correlator

The slide asks: why rewrite \(T\) like this, and why call the vector \(\hat\bs\)? Because it is the best estimate of the signal.

Theorem (estimator–correlator)

The NP detector for a zero-mean Gaussian signal in WGN is

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\bx^T\hat\bs=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,\hat s[n]\ \dec\ \gamma',\qquad \hat\bs=\bC_s(\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}\bx\ } \]

where \(\hat\bs=E[\bs\mid\bx]\) is the MMSE estimate of the signal under \(\Ho\) (a Wiener filter).

Why \(\hat\bs\) is the MMSE estimate. Under \(\Ho\), \(\bs\) and \(\bx=\bs+\bw\) are jointly Gaussian with zero means, \(\bC_{xx}=\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI\), and cross-covariance \(\bC_{sx}=E[\bs(\bs+\bw)^T]=\bC_s\) (since \(\bs\) and \(\bw\) are independent). By the conditional Gaussian property (section 1.2, property 5), \(E[\bs\mid\bx]=\bC_{sx}\bC_{xx}^{-1}\bx=\bC_s(\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}\bx\). From estimation theory, the conditional mean is the MMSE estimator. ∎

Lec 9 p.9 · Kay Theorem 5.1, (5.5)–(5.6)

This is a beautiful and intuitive result. In chapter 7 we correlated the data with the known signal. Now the signal is unknown, so we first estimate it in the MMSE sense and then correlate the data with the estimate, using it as the replica. The block diagram: \(\bx\) goes into a Wiener filter that outputs \(\hat\bs\); \(\bx\) and \(\hat\bs\) are multiplied sample by sample; the products are summed and compared with \(\gamma'\).

Example

White signal, \(\bC_s=\sigma_s^2\bI\).

\[ \hat s[n]=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}\,x[n],\qquad T(\bx)=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n] . \]

The Wiener filter just scales each sample: close to 1 when the SNR is high (trust the data), close to 0 when it is low. The positive constant goes into the threshold, and we recover the energy detector of section 10.2. A white signal has no structure to exploit: no waveform, no preferred frequency, no phase. The only feature left is total power.

10.5 The canonical form

Derivation (board, 12 Nov)

Diagonalise the signal covariance: \(\bC_s=\bV\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s\bV^T\), with \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{N-1})\) its eigenvalues and \(\bV\) orthonormal (\(\bV^T\bV=\bI\)). Adding \(\sigma^2\bI\) does not change the eigenvectors; it adds \(\sigma^2\) to each eigenvalue:

\[ \bC_s+\sigma^2\bI=\bV(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s+\sigma^2\bI)\bV^T,\qquad (\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}=\bV(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}\bV^T . \]

So

\[ T(\bx)=\bx^T\bV\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s\underbrace{\bV^T\bV}_{\bI}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}\bV^T\bx . \]

Let \(\by=\bV^T\bx\), the data expressed in the eigenvector coordinates. Then

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n+\sigma^2}\,y^2[n]\ } \]

The weights \(\lambda_n/(\lambda_n+\sigma^2)\) are Wiener filter weights, one per eigen-direction.

Lec 9 p.13

Distribution of \(\by\). \(\by=\bV^T\bx\) is a linear transformation, so \(\mathrm{cov}(\by)=\bV^T\mathrm{cov}(\bx)\bV\). Under \(\Hz\) this is \(\sigma^2\bV^T\bV=\sigma^2\bI\); under \(\Ho\) it is \(\bV^T(\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)\bV=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s+\sigma^2\bI\). Both are diagonal, so the \(y[n]\) are independent:

\[ \Hz:\ y[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2),\qquad \Ho:\ y[n]\sim\Nc(0,\lambda_n+\sigma^2),\qquad\text{independent.} \]

Why is this useful? The detector becomes a weighted energy detector: decorrelate, square each component, weight it by how much signal that direction carries, and add. Directions where the signal is strong (\(\lambda_n\gg\sigma^2\)) get weight near 1; directions with no signal get weight near 0 and are effectively discarded. \(T\) is a weighted sum of independent \(\chi^2_1\) variables. Its CDF has no closed form in general (it can be computed numerically); the professor said we would not study it in detail. Two cases do have closed forms: equal eigenvalues (a scaled \(\chi^2\), section 10.2) and eigenvalues that come in equal pairs (Kay Example 5.4).

10.6 Example: two correlated signal samples

Homework / Tutorial (Lec 9 p.15) = Kay Example 5.3

\(N=2\), \(\bC_s=\sigma_s^2\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(|\rho|\le1\), white noise \(\sigma^2\bI\). Find \(T(\bx)\), \(\hat\bs\) and the related terms, and the canonical estimator–correlator.

Derivation

Eigenstructure. As in section 8.5, the eigenvectors are \(\mathbf{v}_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}[1,1]^T\) and \(\mathbf{v}_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}[1,-1]^T\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_0=\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)\) and \(\lambda_1=\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)\). So

\[ y[0]=\frac{x[0]+x[1]}{\sqrt2},\qquad y[1]=\frac{x[0]-x[1]}{\sqrt2}. \]

Canonical detector.

\[ T(\bx)=\frac{\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)}{\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)+\sigma^2}\,y^2[0]+\frac{\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)}{\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)+\sigma^2}\,y^2[1]. \]

The signal estimate. \(\hat\bs=\bV\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}\bV^T\bx =\sum_n\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n+\sigma^2}\,y[n]\,\mathbf{v}_n\): project the data on each eigenvector, shrink each projection by its Wiener weight, and rebuild.

Special cases.

  • \(\rho=0\): both weights equal \(\sigma_s^2/(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)\), and since \(y^2[0]+y^2[1]=x^2[0]+x^2[1]\), \(T\) is the energy detector.
  • \(\rho\to1\): the signal samples are identical, \(s[0]=s[1]\). Then \(\lambda_1\to0\), the second weight vanishes, and \(T\propto y^2[0]\propto(x[0]+x[1])^2\). The detector adds the samples coherently and then squares. The direction \([1,-1]^T\) contains noise only, and is discarded as irrelevant.
  • \(\rho\to-1\): \(s[1]=-s[0]\), and by the same argument \(T\propto(x[0]-x[1])^2\).

In every case the components are independent under \(\Hz\) with variance \(\sigma^2\), so \(T\) is a weighted sum of two \(\sigma^2\chi^2_1\) variables (a single scaled \(\chi^2_1\) when one weight is zero, and a scaled \(\chi^2_2\) when the weights are equal).

Also \(\bC_s=\mathbf{A}^{-T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\), so \(\bC_s+\bC_w=\mathbf{A}^{-T}(\mathbf{B}+\bI)\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) and \((\bC_s+\bC_w)^{-1}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}+\bI)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T\). Then

\[ \bC_w^{-1}\bC_s(\bC_s+\bC_w)^{-1}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}^{-T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{B}+\bI)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T =\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}+\bI)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T . \]

So \(T=\bx^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}+\bI)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^T\bx\). Diagonalise \(\mathbf{B}\) as in section 10.5 to finish. ∎

In words: whiten the noise first; in the whitened coordinates the noise variance is 1, and the canonical form of section 10.5 applies with \(\sigma^2=1\).

Derivation (Kay §5.6): the general Gaussian detector

Now let the signal have a mean as well: \(\bs\sim\Nc(\bmu_s,\bC_s)\), noise \(\Nc(\mathbf{0},\bC_w)\). Under \(\Ho\), \(\bx\sim\Nc(\bmu_s,\bC_s+\bC_w)\). Keeping the data-dependent terms of \(2\ln L\):

\[ \bx^T\bC_w^{-1}\bx-(\bx-\bmu_s)^T(\bC_s+\bC_w)^{-1}(\bx-\bmu_s) =\bx^T\big[\bC_w^{-1}-(\bC_s+\bC_w)^{-1}\big]\bx+2\bx^T(\bC_s+\bC_w)^{-1}\bmu_s+\text{const}. \]

Halve it and use the identity above:

\[ \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\bx^T(\bC_s+\bC_w)^{-1}\bmu_s+\tfrac12\,\bx^T\bC_w^{-1}\bC_s(\bC_s+\bC_w)^{-1}\bx\ } \]

A linear term (a generalised matched filter for the mean) plus a quadratic term (an estimator–correlator for the random part). Two checks: with \(\bC_s=\mathbf{0}\) it is the generalised matched filter \(\bx^T\bC_w^{-1}\bmu_s\) of chapter 8; with \(\bmu_s=\mathbf{0}\) it is the estimator–correlator of Problem 5.10.

Chapter 11

Composite hypothesis testing

So far both PDFs were known completely. In practice the hypotheses usually contain unknown parameters: a DC level of unknown amplitude, a sinusoid of unknown phase, noise of unknown power. Such hypotheses are composite. The difficulty fits in one line: the LRT depends on the unknown parameter, so the "optimal" detector cannot be built without knowing what we are trying to detect. This chapter shows when the difficulty disappears (UMP tests) and what to do when it does not (the Bayesian approach and the GLRT).

11.1 Composite hypotheses and the power function

Definition

Let the data have PDF \(p(\bx;\btheta)\), \(\btheta\in\Theta=\Theta_0\cup\Theta_1\). A composite test is

\[ \Hz:\ \btheta\in\Theta_0,\qquad \Ho:\ \btheta\in\Theta_1, \]

where at least one of the sets has more than one point. The error probabilities become functions of \(\btheta\):

\[ \PFA(\btheta)=\Pr\{\text{decide }\Ho;\btheta\},\ \btheta\in\Theta_0,\qquad \PD(\btheta)=\Pr\{\text{decide }\Ho;\btheta\},\ \btheta\in\Theta_1 . \]

Together they form the power function \(\beta(\btheta)=\Pr\{\text{decide }\Ho;\btheta\}\) over all of \(\Theta\).

Lec 10 p.3–5 · Kay §6.3

Performance is now a function, not a number. The slide asks what an ideal power function looks like. Answer: \(\beta(\btheta)=0\) on \(\Theta_0\) (never a false alarm) and \(\beta(\btheta)=1\) on \(\Theta_1\) (always a detection). For \(\Theta_0=[-1,1]\) it is zero inside the interval and one outside. No real test achieves it, but a good test's power function is small on \(\Theta_0\) and rises quickly to 1 on \(\Theta_1\).

Definition

NP criterion for composite tests. Maximise \(\PD(\btheta)\) for every \(\btheta\in\Theta_1\), subject to \(\PFA(\btheta)\le\alpha\) for every \(\btheta\in\Theta_0\). A test that does this is uniformly most powerful (UMP). "Uniformly" means the same test, with the same threshold, is optimal at every point of \(\Theta_1\). A UMP test does not always exist.

Lec 10 p.6

11.2 The one-sided case: a UMP test exists

\[ \Hz:\ A=0,\qquad \Ho:\ A>0,\qquad x[n]=A+w[n],\ \ \sigma^2\text{ known.} \]
Derivation

Fix any \(A>0\) and write the NP test as if \(A\) were known. From section 3.5,

\[ \ln L(\bx)=\frac{NA}{\sigma^2}\bar x-\frac{NA^2}{2\sigma^2}\ \dec\ \ln\gamma . \]

Because \(A>0\) we may divide by \(NA/\sigma^2\) without flipping: \(\bar x\dec\gamma'\). The threshold seems to depend on \(A\), but that is an illusion: we set it from \(\PFA\), and under \(\Hz\) \(\bar x\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2/N)\) for every \(A\). So

\[ \boxed{\ \bar x\ \dec\ \gamma'=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{N}}\,Q^{-1}(\alpha)\ },\qquad \PD(A)=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\alpha)-\sqrt{\frac{NA^2}{\sigma^2}}\right),\quad A>0 . \]

Why it is UMP. For each fixed \(A>0\), this is the NP test for \(A=0\) vs that \(A\): it has the largest \(\PD\) among all tests of size \(\alpha\). And it is the same test for every \(A>0\), because neither the statistic nor the threshold involves \(A\). So no size-\(\alpha\) test beats it at any \(A>0\). The key fact is the one from chapter 4: the threshold is set by \(\Hz\) alone.

Lec 10 p.9 · Kay Example 6.1

11.3 The two-sided case: no UMP test

Now let \(\Ho:A\ne0\). For \(A>0\) the NP test is \(\bar x>\gamma'\). For \(A\lt0\), dividing by \(NA/\sigma^2\lt0\) flips the inequality, and the NP test is \(\bar x\lt-\gamma'\). These are two different tests. Each is best on its own half-line and very bad on the other. Since the sign of \(A\) is unknown, neither is best over all of \(\Theta_1=\{A\ne0\}\). No UMP test exists.

Definition

A clairvoyant detector is the NP detector designed as if the unknown parameter were known. It cannot be built, but its performance, here \(\PD(A)=Q\big(Q^{-1}(\alpha)-\sqrt{NA^2/\sigma^2}\big)\) for every \(A\ne0\), is an upper bound for any realisable test. It plays the role the CRLB played in estimation.

11.4 When do UMP tests exist?

They tend to exist when

the family \(p(\bx;\theta)\) has a monotone likelihood ratio in a scalar statistic \(T(\bx)\), and the alternative is one-sided (\(\theta>\theta_0\) or \(\theta\lt\theta_0\)).

They essentially never exist when

the alternative is two-sided; \(\btheta\) is a vector; or unknowns appear under both hypotheses (nuisance parameters, such as an unknown \(\sigma^2\)).

Lec 10 p.13
Theorem (monotone likelihood ratio, Karlin–Rubin)

Suppose that for every \(\theta_1>\theta_0\) the ratio \(p(\bx;\theta_1)/p(\bx;\theta_0)\) is an increasing function of a scalar statistic \(T(\bx)\). Then for \(\Hz:\theta=\theta_0\) vs \(\Ho:\theta>\theta_0\), the test "decide \(\Ho\) if \(T(\bx)>\gamma'\)", with \(\gamma'\) set by \(\Pr\{T>\gamma';\theta_0\}=\alpha\), is UMP.

Proof. Fix any \(\theta_1>\theta_0\). The NP test for \(\theta_0\) vs \(\theta_1\) is \(p(\bx;\theta_1)/p(\bx;\theta_0)>\gamma\). Since the ratio increases with \(T\), this is the same event as \(T(\bx)>\gamma'\) for a suitable \(\gamma'\). The threshold \(\gamma'\) is fixed by the size condition under \(\theta_0\), so it does not depend on \(\theta_1\). Hence one test is the NP test for every \(\theta_1>\theta_0\): it is UMP. ∎

A common case: the exponential family \(p(\bx;\theta)=h(\bx)\exp[c(\theta)T(\bx)-d(\theta)]\) with \(c\) increasing. Then \(\ln\frac{p(\bx;\theta_1)}{p(\bx;\theta_0)}=[c(\theta_1)-c(\theta_0)]T(\bx)-\dots\) increases with \(T\). Gaussian mean (\(T=\sum x[n]\)), Gaussian variance (\(T=\sum x^2[n]\)), exponential, Poisson and binomial data are all of this form. That is why every one-sided example in this guide has a UMP test.

Homework (Lec 10 p.12)

\(x[n]\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\) i.i.d. Example 1: \(\Hz:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2\) vs \(\Ho:\sigma^2>\sigma_0^2\). Example 2: \(\Hz:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2\) vs \(\Ho:\sigma^2\ne\sigma_0^2\). Derive the NP detector and the power function. When does a UMP test exist, and why?

Example 1 (one-sided). For any fixed \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\), section 5.4 gives the NP test \(T=\sum x^2[n]>\gamma'\). Its threshold comes from \(\Hz\) alone: \(\gamma'=\sigma_0^2\,Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(\alpha)\). Neither the statistic nor the threshold involves \(\sigma_1^2\), so the test is UMP. Its power function is

\[ \beta(\sigma^2)=\Pr\{T>\gamma';\sigma^2\}=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma^2}\,Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(\alpha)\right), \]

which equals \(\alpha\) at \(\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2\) and increases to 1 as \(\sigma^2\to\infty\). (The Gaussian variance family is an exponential family with \(T=\sum x^2[n]\) and \(c(\sigma^2)=-1/(2\sigma^2)\), increasing, so Karlin–Rubin applies.)

Example 2 (two-sided). For \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\) the NP test is "\(T\) large". For \(\sigma_1^2\lt\sigma_0^2\) the coefficient \(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}\) in \(\ln L\) is negative, so dividing flips the test: decide \(\Ho\) when \(T\) is small, \(T\lt\sigma_0^2\,Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(1-\alpha)\). Two different tests, so no UMP test exists. A sensible two-sided detector rejects when \(T\) is too small or too large: \(T\lt c_1\) or \(T>c_2\), with, say, \(c_1=\sigma_0^2Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(1-\alpha/2)\) and \(c_2=\sigma_0^2Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(\alpha/2)\). Its power function is

\[ \beta(\sigma^2)=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{c_2}{\sigma^2}\right)+1-Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{c_1}{\sigma^2}\right). \]

The GLRT gives the same shape. With \(\hat\sigma^2=T/N\) and \(u=\hat\sigma^2/\sigma_0^2\), \(2\ln L_G=N(u-1-\ln u)\), which is zero at \(u=1\) and grows on both sides. So it also rejects when the sample variance is far from \(\sigma_0^2\) in either direction.

The earlier end-sem question with a variance jump after sample \(n_0\) is Example 1 in disguise: the first \(n_0\) samples are irrelevant, and \(\sum_{n\ge n_0}x^2[n]>\gamma'\) is UMP for every \(\Delta>0\) (chapter 13).

11.5 When no UMP test exists: the Bayesian approach

Two ways out. The Bayesian approach treats \(\btheta\) as random with a prior and integrates it out. The GLRT replaces \(\btheta\) by its maximum likelihood estimate under each hypothesis. Integration versus maximisation is the only structural difference. The Bayesian approach needs a prior; the GLRT needs a computable MLE.

\[ L_B(\bx)=\frac{\int p(\bx\mid\btheta_1)\,p(\btheta_1)\,d\btheta_1}{\int p(\bx\mid\btheta_0)\,p(\btheta_0)\,d\btheta_0}\ >\ \gamma, \qquad L_G(\bx)=\frac{p(\bx;\hat\btheta_1,\Ho)}{p(\bx;\hat\btheta_0,\Hz)}\ >\ \gamma . \]

After integration, both hypotheses are simple again, so the Bayesian detector is NP-optimal for that prior.

Derivation (Lec 10 p.17)

DC level with a Gaussian prior. \(\Hz:A=0\), \(\Ho:A\ne0\), with prior \(A\sim\Nc(0,\sigma_A^2)\) independent of the noise. Then

\[ p(\bx;\Ho)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x[n]-A)^2}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_A^2}}e^{-\frac{A^2}{2\sigma_A^2}}\,dA =\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{N/2}\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_A^2}}\int e^{-\frac12Q(A)}dA, \]

with \(Q(A)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(x[n]-A)^2+\frac{A^2}{\sigma_A^2}\). Expand and complete the square in \(A\). Define \(\frac{1}{v}=\frac{N}{\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_A^2}\):

\[ Q(A)=\frac{1}{v}A^2-\frac{2N\bar x}{\sigma^2}A+\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n] =\frac{1}{v}\Big(A-\frac{N\bar x v}{\sigma^2}\Big)^2-\frac{N^2\bar x^2v}{\sigma^4}+\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n]. \]

The Gaussian integral gives \(\int e^{-\frac{1}{2v}(A-\cdot)^2}dA=\sqrt{2\pi v}\). Divide by \(p(\bx;\Hz)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n]}\); the \(\sum x^2[n]\) terms cancel:

\[ L_B(\bx)=\sqrt{\frac{v}{\sigma_A^2}}\exp\!\left(\frac{N^2v}{2\sigma^4}\,\bar x^2\right)\ >\ \gamma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ T(\bx)=\bar x^2\ \dec\ \gamma'\ } \]

The prior is symmetric about zero, so the detector ignores the sign of \(\bar x\) and squares it. The threshold is set from \(\Hz\), where \(\bar x\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2/N)\): \(\PFA=\Pr\{|\bar x|>\sqrt{\gamma'}\}=2Q\big(\sqrt{N\gamma'/\sigma^2}\big)\). It does not need \(\sigma_A^2\). (With a non-zero prior mean \(\mu_A\) it would, and the test would no longer be symmetric.)

11.6 The generalised likelihood ratio test

Definition

The GLRT decides \(\Ho\) if

\[ L_G(\bx)=\frac{p(\bx;\hat\btheta_1,\Ho)}{p(\bx;\hat\btheta_0,\Hz)}=\frac{\max_{\btheta_1}p(\bx;\btheta_1,\Ho)}{\max_{\btheta_0}p(\bx;\btheta_0,\Hz)}\ >\ \gamma, \]

where \(\hat\btheta_i\) is the MLE of the unknowns assuming \(\mathcal{H}_i\) is true. If \(\Hz\) is completely known, this is just \(\max_{\btheta_1}L(\bx;\btheta_1)\): maximise the likelihood ratio over the unknown.

Lec 10 p.15 · Kay §6.4.2

The GLRT has no general optimality property, but it works well in practice, it is easy to apply, and for large \(N\) it is asymptotically optimal among a broad class of tests. It also delivers estimates of the unknowns as a by-product.

Derivation (Lec 10 p.19)

GLRT 1: unknown amplitude, \(\sigma^2\) known. \(\Hz:A=0\), \(\Ho:A\ne0\). The MLE of \(A\) (from estimation theory) is \(\hat A=\bar x\). So

\[ \ln L_G(\bx)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[\sum(x[n]-\bar x)^2-\sum x^2[n]\Big] =-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[-2\bar x\sum x[n]+N\bar x^2\Big]=\frac{N\bar x^2}{2\sigma^2}. \] \[ \boxed{\ \bar x^2\ \dec\ \gamma'=\frac{2\sigma^2\ln\gamma}{N}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|\bar x|\ \dec\ \gamma''\ } \]

It coincides with the Bayesian detector. That is not an accident: both are forced by the symmetry of the problem under \(A\to-A\).

Performance. Under \(\Hz\), \(\PFA=\Pr\{|\bar x|>\gamma''\}=2Q\big(\gamma''/\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}\big)\), so \(\gamma''=\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}\,Q^{-1}(\PFA/2)\). Under \(\Ho\), \(\bar x\sim\Nc(A,\sigma^2/N)\), and with \(d=\sqrt{NA^2/\sigma^2}\),

\[ \PD(A)=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{\PFA}{2}\right)-d\right)+Q\!\left(Q^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{\PFA}{2}\right)+d\right). \]

Figure 11.1 shows it is only slightly below the clairvoyant bound, and it works for both signs of \(A\).

since \(\ln(1+u)\) is increasing. Compared with GLRT 1, the statistic is normalised by the estimated noise power.

Then \(\bar x=\sigma\bar u\) and \(\hat\sigma_1^2=\sigma^2\hat\sigma_{1,u}^2\), so \(T=\bar u^2/\hat\sigma_{1,u}^2\): the \(\sigma^2\) cancels. The distribution of \(T\) under \(\Hz\) does not depend on the unknown \(\sigma^2\), so a single threshold gives the same \(\PFA\) whatever the noise level. Such a detector has a constant false-alarm rate (CFAR). GLRT 1 could not be used here: its threshold \(\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}Q^{-1}(\PFA/2)\) needs \(\sigma^2\).

For large \(N\), \(NT\approx N\bar x^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_1\), and the performance approaches that of GLRT 1.

Chapter 12

Sequential detection

Every detector so far used a fixed number of samples \(N\), chosen before looking at the data. Wald asked a different question in 1943. The US Statistical Research Group wanted to know how many artillery shells from a production lot must be tested before accepting or rejecting the lot, when each test destroys the shell. Fixing \(N\) in advance is wasteful: sometimes the first few shells already settle the matter. Wald's answer was to look at the samples one at a time and stop as soon as the evidence is decisive. The method was classified until 1945, when Wald published it; his book Sequential Analysis followed in 1947.

12.1 The three-way decision

Observations \(x_1,x_2,\dots\) arrive one at a time. They are i.i.d. with density \(p_0\) under \(\Hz\) and \(p_1\) under \(\Ho\). After each observation the detector chooses one of three actions: accept \(\Hz\), accept \(\Ho\), or take another observation. The number of samples \(N\) is now a random variable, and we want to make its average small.

Definition

A sequential test splits, for each \(n\), the space of \((x_1,\dots,x_n)\) into three disjoint regions: \(R_0^{(n)}\) (stop, accept \(\Hz\)), \(R_1^{(n)}\) (stop, accept \(\Ho\)) and \(R_c^{(n)}\) (continue). Sampling continues while the data stay in \(R_c^{(n)}\). \(N\) is the first \(n\) at which they do not. A test has strength \((\alpha,\beta)\) if

\[ \alpha=\Pr_0\{\text{accept }\Ho\}\ (\text{Type I, }\PFA),\qquad \beta=\Pr_1\{\text{accept }\Hz\}\ (\text{Type II, }P_M). \]

A sequential test has four performance numbers: \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(E_0\{N\}\) and \(E_1\{N\}\).

Lec 11 p.6–11
Common mistake

Notation clash. In this chapter (as in the lecture) \(\beta\) is the miss probability, and \(A\) is the upper threshold. Poor's book uses \(\beta\) for the detection probability and swaps the names \(A\) and \(B\). If you revise from Poor, translate first.

The slides ask two questions. Should we use the NP criterion for the shells? NP fixes \(N\) before sampling, so it would destroy \(N\) shells even when the first few already decide the matter. A sequential test spends fewer shells on average for the same error rates. If samples cost nothing, which is better, sequential or fixed-sample? Then the question disappears: we could take as many samples as we like and drive both errors towards zero with an ordinary NP test. Sequential testing pays off precisely when each sample has a cost: destroyed products, patients in a trial, sensing time in a cognitive radio, users in an A/B test.

12.2 Wald's sequential probability ratio test

Let \(p_{1n}=\prod_{i=1}^np_1(x_i)\), \(p_{0n}=\prod_{i=1}^np_0(x_i)\) and \(\lambda_n=p_{1n}/p_{0n}\), the likelihood ratio of the first \(n\) samples.

Definition (Wald's SPRT)

Choose two constants \(0\lt B\lt1\lt A\). At stage \(n\):

\[ \begin{cases}\lambda_n\ge A & \Rightarrow\ \text{stop, accept }\Ho,\\ \lambda_n\le B & \Rightarrow\ \text{stop, accept }\Hz,\\ B\lt\lambda_n\lt A & \Rightarrow\ \text{take }x_{n+1}.\end{cases} \qquad N=\min\{n\ge1:\ \lambda_n\ge A\ \text{or}\ \lambda_n\le B\}. \]

Logarithmic form. With \(z_i=\ln\dfrac{p_1(x_i)}{p_0(x_i)}\), \(S_n=\ln\lambda_n=\sum_{i=1}^nz_i\), \(a=\ln A>0\) and \(b=\ln B\lt0\): stop and accept \(\Ho\) if \(S_n\ge a\); stop and accept \(\Hz\) if \(S_n\le b\); otherwise continue.

Lec 11 p.12–13

The idea is inherited from Neyman–Pearson: for a fixed \(n\), the best test is a threshold on \(\lambda_n\). The SPRT uses two thresholds after every sample because there are now two ways to stop. Since the samples are i.i.d., \(S_n\) is a sum of i.i.d. increments: a random walk that starts at 0. The test stops when the walk first leaves the strip \((b,a)\). That is why the lecture digressed to random walks: \(N\) is the first-passage time of a random walk out of an interval, and which boundary it crosses decides the hypothesis.

Example

Gaussian mean shift (Poor Example III.D.1). \(\Hz:x_i\sim\Nc(0,\sigma^2)\), \(\Ho:x_i\sim\Nc(\theta,\sigma^2)\), \(\theta>0\). Then

\[ z_i=\ln\frac{p_1(x_i)}{p_0(x_i)}=\frac{\theta}{\sigma^2}\Big(x_i-\frac\theta2\Big),\qquad S_n=\frac{\theta}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(x_i-\frac\theta2\Big). \]

The SPRT accumulates the centred data and stops when the running sum leaves \((b,a)\).

12.3 The drift: Kullback–Leibler divergence

Definition

The Kullback–Leibler divergence of \(p_1\) from \(p_0\) is

\[ D(p_1\|p_0)=\int p_1(x)\ln\frac{p_1(x)}{p_0(x)}\,dx=E_{p_1}\!\left[\ln\frac{p_1(x)}{p_0(x)}\right]. \]

It behaves like a distance between densities, but it is not symmetric: \(D(p_0\|p_1)\ne D(p_1\|p_0)\) in general.

Lec 11 p.15
Theorem (the drift of the walk) \[ E_1\{z\}=D(p_1\|p_0)\ge0,\qquad E_0\{z\}=-D(p_0\|p_1)\le0, \]

with equality only if \(p_0=p_1\).

Proof. The first identity is the definition. For the second, \(E_0\{z\}=\int p_0\ln\frac{p_1}{p_0}=-\int p_0\ln\frac{p_0}{p_1}=-D(p_0\|p_1)\). For the signs, use \(\ln u\le u-1\), with equality only at \(u=1\):

\[ -D(p_0\|p_1)=\int p_0\ln\frac{p_1}{p_0}\,dx\le\int p_0\Big(\frac{p_1}{p_0}-1\Big)dx=\int p_1-\int p_0=0 . \]

The same argument with the roles swapped gives \(D(p_1\|p_0)\ge0\). ∎

So the walk drifts upward under \(\Ho\) and downward under \(\Hz\). The larger the divergence, the faster it drifts, and the sooner it hits the correct boundary. For the Gaussian shift, \(D(p_1\|p_0)=D(p_0\|p_1)=\theta^2/(2\sigma^2)\).

12.4 The SPRT terminates

Proof: in class (Lec 11 p.17)

Theorem. If \(\Pr\{z\ne0\}>0\) (that is, \(p_0\) and \(p_1\) are different densities), then \(\Pr\{N\lt\infty\}=1\) under either hypothesis.

Proof. The walk starts inside the strip \((b,a)\), whose width is \(a-b\). If at any time the next \(M\) increments add up to more than \(a-b\) in absolute value, the walk must have left the strip by the end of those \(M\) steps (if it had not already). So it is enough to show that such a block of steps eventually occurs.

Step 1. Since \(\Pr\{z\ne0\}>0\), there is an \(\epsilon>0\) with \(\Pr\{|z|>\epsilon\}>0\). So \(p=\Pr\{z>\epsilon\}>0\) or \(p'=\Pr\{z\lt-\epsilon\}>0\); take the first case (the other is symmetric). Choose an integer \(M>(a-b)/\epsilon\).

Step 2. For one block of \(M\) steps, the event "every step exceeds \(\epsilon\)" implies "the block sum exceeds \(M\epsilon>a-b\)". By independence,

\[ q=\Pr\Big\{\Big|\sum_{\text{block}}z_i\Big|>a-b\Big\}\ \ge\ \Pr\{z_1>\epsilon,\dots,z_M>\epsilon\}=p^M>0 . \]

Step 3. Split the sequence into consecutive blocks of \(M\) steps. The blocks are independent. If the test is still running after \(jM\) samples, then none of the first \(j\) blocks had a sum larger than \(a-b\) in absolute value. So

\[ \Pr\{N>jM\}\le(1-q)^j . \]

Step 4. \(N=\infty\) would mean \(N>jM\) for every \(j\). Hence \(\Pr\{N=\infty\}\le\lim_{j\to\infty}(1-q)^j=0\). ∎

The proof gives more: \(N\) has a geometric tail, so \(E\{N\}=\sum_{n\ge0}\Pr\{N>n\}\le M\sum_j(1-q)^j=M/q\lt\infty\). We need this in section 12.7.

12.5 The fundamental inequalities

Proof: in class (Lec 11 p.19)

Theorem. If the SPRT with thresholds \(A,B\) has strength \((\alpha,\beta)\), then

\[ \boxed{\ A\le\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha},\qquad B\ge\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\ } \]

Proof of the first. Let \(R_1\) be the set of sample sequences on which the test stops and accepts \(\Ho\). It is a disjoint union over \(n\) of the events \(\{N=n,\ \lambda_n\ge A\}\). On each such event, \(p_{1n}\ge A\,p_{0n}\), that is, \(p_{0n}\le\frac1A p_{1n}\). Integrate over \(R_1\) (summing over \(n\)):

\[ \alpha=\Pr_0\{R_1\}=\sum_n\int_{\{N=n,\lambda_n\ge A\}}p_{0n}\ \le\ \frac1A\sum_n\int_{\{N=n,\lambda_n\ge A\}}p_{1n}=\frac1A\Pr_1\{R_1\}=\frac{1-\beta}{A}. \]

So \(A\le(1-\beta)/\alpha\).

Proof of the second. On the events where the test accepts \(\Hz\), \(\lambda_n\le B\), so \(p_{1n}\le B\,p_{0n}\). Integrate over that region \(R_0\):

\[ \beta=\Pr_1\{R_0\}\le B\,\Pr_0\{R_0\}=B(1-\alpha), \]

so \(B\ge\beta/(1-\alpha)\). (Both steps use \(\Pr\{N\lt\infty\}=1\), so that \(R_0\) and \(R_1\) together have probability 1.) ∎

When are these equalities? Only if the test stops exactly on a boundary, \(\lambda_N=A\) or \(\lambda_N=B\), with no overshoot. With continuous data this has probability zero, so the inequalities are strict. It can happen with discrete data whose log-likelihood moves on a lattice that the boundaries sit on (the Bernoulli example below, if \(a\) is a multiple of the step). It holds approximately when each step \(z_i\) is small compared with \(a-b\), which is the case for weak signals and long tests.

Wald's approximations

Ignore the overshoot and treat the inequalities as equalities:

\[ \boxed{\ A\approx\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha},\qquad B\approx\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\ } \qquad\Big(a\approx\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha},\quad b\approx\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\Big). \]

These are very useful in practice: the thresholds depend only on the desired \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). We do not need the densities or any distribution of \(N\) to set them.

12.6 Wald's bound on the total error

Proof: in class (Lec 11 p.20)

Suppose we design with \(A=\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}\) and \(B=\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\), but the test actually achieves strength \((\alpha',\beta')\). Then

\[ \alpha'\le\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta},\qquad \beta'\le\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha},\qquad \boxed{\ \alpha'+\beta'\le\alpha+\beta\ } \]

Proof. The fundamental inequalities hold for the actual strength: \(A\le\frac{1-\beta'}{\alpha'}\) and \(B\ge\frac{\beta'}{1-\alpha'}\). Insert the design values of \(A\) and \(B\) and cross-multiply (all quantities are positive):

\[ \alpha'(1-\beta)\le\alpha(1-\beta'),\qquad \beta'(1-\alpha)\le\beta(1-\alpha'). \tag{*} \]

From the first, \(\alpha'\le\alpha\frac{1-\beta'}{1-\beta}\le\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}\), since \(1-\beta'\le1\). Likewise \(\beta'\le\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\). Now add the two inequalities (*):

\[ \alpha'-\alpha'\beta+\beta'-\alpha\beta'\ \le\ \alpha-\alpha\beta'+\beta-\alpha'\beta . \]

The terms \(-\alpha'\beta\) and \(-\alpha\beta'\) appear on both sides and cancel, leaving \(\alpha'+\beta'\le\alpha+\beta\). ∎

Interpretation: jointly, you can never be worse on both counts. If one error slips above its target (and only slightly, since \(\alpha/(1-\beta)\approx\alpha\) for small \(\beta\)), the other more than compensates. In practice the overshoot makes the test conservative, and both errors come out below target. The professor's simulation (Lec 11 p.21) shows this:

Nominal \((\alpha,\beta)\) \(\alpha'\) \(\beta'\) \(\alpha'+\beta'\) Bound \(\alpha+\beta\) \(E\{N\}\)
(0.05, 0.05) 0.0286 0.0299 0.0585 0.10 7.0
(0.01, 0.01) 0.0053 0.0061 0.0115 0.02 10.5

The realised errors are roughly half the nominal ones, and that is paid for with extra samples.

12.7 The average sample number

The lecture gives the formulas and skips the proof. The proof is short once we have Wald's equation.

Theorem (Wald's equation)

If \(z_1,z_2,\dots\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\), and \(N\) is a stopping time with \(E\{N\}\lt\infty\), then \(E\{S_N\}=\mu\,E\{N\}\).

Proof. Write \(S_N=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}z_i\,\mathbb{1}\{N\ge i\}\). Whether the test is still running at step \(i\) (\(N\ge i\)) is decided by \(z_1,\dots,z_{i-1}\) only, so \(\mathbb{1}\{N\ge i\}\) is independent of \(z_i\). Therefore

\[ E\{S_N\}=\sum_{i=1}^\infty E\{z_i\}\Pr\{N\ge i\}=\mu\sum_{i=1}^\infty\Pr\{N\ge i\}=\mu\,E\{N\}. \]

(Exchanging sum and expectation is allowed because \(E\{N\}\lt\infty\), shown in section 12.4.) ∎

Derivation

The average sample numbers. Ignore the overshoot, so that \(S_N\) equals \(a\) or \(b\). Under \(\Ho\), \(S_N=a\) with probability \(1-\beta\) (correct acceptance) and \(S_N=b\) with probability \(\beta\). With \(\mu_1=E_1\{z\}=D(p_1\|p_0)\), Wald's equation gives \(E_1\{N\}=E_1\{S_N\}/\mu_1\). Insert Wald's approximations for \(a\) and \(b\):

\[ \boxed{\ E_1\{N\}\approx\frac{(1-\beta)\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}+\beta\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}{D(p_1\|p_0)}\ } \]

Under \(\Hz\), \(S_N=a\) with probability \(\alpha\) and \(b\) with probability \(1-\alpha\), and \(\mu_0=-D(p_0\|p_1)\):

\[ \boxed{\ E_0\{N\}\approx\frac{\alpha\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}+(1-\alpha)\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}{-D(p_0\|p_1)}\ } \]

Both are positive: under \(\Hz\) the numerator is dominated by the negative \(b\) term. The formulas explain everything about the SPRT's speed. Smaller error targets widen the strip only logarithmically, and a larger divergence (hypotheses easier to tell apart) shortens the test in proportion.

12.8 Sequential versus fixed sample size

Example (Poor Example III.D.2)

Example (Poor Exercise III.25)

Bernoulli data. \(\Pr\{x_i=1\}=1/3\) under \(\Hz\) and \(2/3\) under \(\Ho\). Target \(\alpha=\beta=0.01\).

The increments are \(z_i=\ln2\) if \(x_i=1\) and \(-\ln2\) if \(x_i=0\). So \(S_n=k_n\ln2\), where \(k_n\) is (number of ones) − (number of zeros). Wald's thresholds are \(a=-b=\ln99=4.595=6.63\ln2\). Since \(k_n\) is an integer, the test is: keep sampling until the ones lead the zeros by 7 (accept \(\Ho\)) or the zeros lead by 7 (accept \(\Hz\)).

This walk moves \(\pm1\) and stops exactly at \(\pm7\), so it is the gambler's-ruin problem and can be solved exactly. Under \(\Hz\) a step is \(+1\) with probability \(\frac13\); the chance of reaching \(+7\) before \(-7\) is \(\frac{2^7-1}{2^{14}-1}=0.0078\). So the true \(\alpha=\beta=0.0078\), below the target 0.01: the overshoot (stopping at \(7\ln2\) instead of \(6.63\ln2\)) made the test conservative. The divergence is \(D=\frac23\ln2-\frac13\ln2=\frac13\ln2=0.231\). Wald's approximation gives \(E\{N\}\approx\frac{0.98\times4.595}{0.231}=19.5\). The exact value, from Wald's equation with the real boundary \(7\ln2\) and the exact \(\alpha\), is 20.7. A fixed-sample test with the same 1% error bound needs \(n=47\).

12.9 Optimality and limits

Theorem (Wald–Wolfowitz, stated)

Among all sequential tests (including all fixed-sample tests) whose error probabilities are no larger than those of a given SPRT, none has a smaller average sample number, under either hypothesis. In this sense the SPRT is optimal; it is the sequential analogue of the NP lemma. (The slide says "proved later"; the proof was not given, and Poor omits it too.)

Why not always use an SPRT? (Lec 11 p.23)

  • The sample size is finite with probability 1, but it is not bounded. Ambiguous data can make a test run very long. Figure 12.1's middle path shows it: when the truth is between the hypotheses, the drift is near zero. The practical fix is a truncated SPRT that forces a decision after a maximum number of samples.
  • It needs exact \(p_0\) and \(p_1\). The fixed-sample NP test for a one-sided shift is UMP and does not need \(\theta\); the SPRT's increments \(z_i\) do. If the true \(\theta\) were half the design value, the walk would hover around zero under \(\Ho\).
  • The theory relies on i.i.d. samples. Without that, little is known.

A4 Q1 · 15 marks

Observe \(x[n]\), \(n=0,\dots,2N-1\), in WGN with variance \(\sigma^2\). The signal is \(A\) for \(n\lt N\) under both hypotheses; for \(n\ge N\) it is \(0\) under \(\Hz\) and \(2A\) under \(\Ho\). With \(A>0\), find the NP detector and its performance. Explain its operation and whether it simplifies. Can it be reduced if \(A\) is unknown?

Solution Irrelevant first half; a DC detector on the second half

Split \(\bx=[\bx_1^T\ \bx_2^T]^T\) (first and second halves). The first half has the same distribution, \(\Nc(A,\sigma^2)\) i.i.d., under both hypotheses, and is independent of the second half. Its factors cancel in \(L(\bx)\): it is irrelevant (section 6.1). What remains is

\[ \ln L(\bx)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=N}^{2N-1}\big[(x[n]-2A)^2-x^2[n]\big]=\frac{2A}{\sigma^2}\sum_{n=N}^{2N-1}x[n]-\frac{2NA^2}{\sigma^2}. \]

Since \(A>0\): \(T(\bx)=\sum_{n=N}^{2N-1}x[n]\dec\gamma'\). Under \(\Hz\), \(T\sim\Nc(0,N\sigma^2)\); under \(\Ho\), \(T\sim\Nc(2NA,N\sigma^2)\). So \(\gamma'=\sqrt{N\sigma^2}\,Q^{-1}(\PFA)\) and

\[ \PD=Q\!\left(Q^{-1}(\PFA)-\sqrt{d^2}\right),\qquad d^2=\frac{(2NA)^2}{N\sigma^2}=\frac{4NA^2}{\sigma^2}. \]

Operation: discard the first half, average the second, compare with a threshold. It is a DC-level detector for amplitude \(2A\) on \(N\) samples; that is already its simplest form. \(A\) unknown (\(A>0\)): the statistic and the threshold do not involve \(A\) (the threshold comes from \(\Hz\), where the second half is pure noise), so the same detector works for every \(A>0\). It is UMP (chapter 11). Only \(\PD\) depends on \(A\).

\(N\) i.i.d. Rayleigh samples, \(p(x;\sigma^2)=\frac{x}{\sigma^2}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\), \(x\ge0\). \(\Hz:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2\), \(\Ho:\sigma^2=\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\). (a) Joint PDFs, likelihood ratio, sufficient statistic. (b) The most powerful test of size \(\alpha\).

Solution Energy statistic, chi-squared with 2N degrees of freedom

(a) \(p(\bx;\sigma_i^2)=\frac{\prod_nx[n]}{\sigma_i^{2N}}\exp\!\big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_i^2}\sum x^2[n]\big)\). The product \(\prod x[n]\) cancels in the ratio:

\[ \ln L=N\ln\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}+\frac12\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}-\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}\Big)\sum x^2[n]. \]

The sufficient statistic is \(T=\sum x^2[n]\), and \(L\) increases with \(T\) because \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\).

Statement of the Neyman–Fisher factorisation theorem.

If the PDF \(p(\mathbf x;\theta)\) can be factored as

\[p(\mathbf x;\theta)=g\big(T(\mathbf x),\theta\big)\,h(\mathbf x),\]

2026 End Q1(vi) · 2.5 marks

Precise statement of the Neyman–Pearson lemma for two simple hypotheses.

Solution

Test \(H_0:\mathbf x\sim p_0\) against \(H_1:\mathbf x\sim p_1\) with a (possibly randomised) rule δ(x) = probability of deciding H₁, and let \(L(\mathbf x)=p_1(\mathbf x)/p_0(\mathbf x)\).

  1. Optimality. Any rule of the form \[\delta(\mathbf x)=\begin{cases}1&L(\mathbf x)>\gamma\\ c_0&L(\mathbf x)=\gamma\\ 0&L(\mathbf x)\lt \gamma\end{cases}\] with \(E_0[\delta]=P_{FA}=\alpha\) has the largest \(P_D\) among all rules with \(P_{FA}\le\alpha\).
  2. Existence. For every α ∈ (0, 1) there are γ ≥ 0 and \(c_0\in[0,1]\) that make \(P_{FA}=\alpha\) exactly: \(c_0=\frac{\alpha-\Pr_0\{L>\gamma\}}{\Pr_0\{L=\gamma\}}\). For continuous data \(c_0\) isn't needed.
  3. Uniqueness. Every most powerful size-α rule has this form, except possibly on a set of zero probability under both hypotheses.

In words: to maximise \(P_D\) subject to \(P_{FA}=\alpha\), say H₁ when \(L(\mathbf x)>\gamma\), with γ fixed by \(P_{FA}=\int_{L>\gamma}p_0=\alpha\).

\(H_0:X\sim p_0\), \(H_1:X\sim p_1\), priors \(\pi_0,\pi_1\). Derive the detector that minimises the Bayes risk.

Solution

Short answer: decide H₁ if \(\dfrac{p_1(x)}{p_0(x)}>\dfrac{\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})}{\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})}\). With 0/1 costs this is MAP, threshold \(\pi_0/\pi_1\).

\(C_{ij}\) = cost of deciding \(H_i\) when \(H_j\) is true. A deterministic rule splits the data space into \(R_1\) (decide H₁) and \(R_0\) (its complement). The Bayes risk is the average cost:

\[\mathcal R=\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}\,\pi_j\Pr\{\text{decide }H_i|H_j\}=\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}\,\pi_j\int_{R_i}p_j(x)\,dx.\]

Use \(\int_{R_0}p_j=1-\int_{R_1}p_j\) to write everything over \(R_1\):

\[\mathcal R=C_{00}\pi_0+C_{01}\pi_1+\int_{R_1}\Big[\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})p_0(x)-\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})p_1(x)\Big]dx.\]

The first two terms don't depend on the rule. The integral is smallest if \(R_1\) contains exactly the points where the integrand is negative:

\[x\in R_1\iff\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})p_1(x)>\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})p_0(x).\]

Assuming errors cost more than correct decisions (\(C_{10}>C_{00}\), \(C_{01}>C_{11}\)), divide through to get the LRT

\[L(x)=\frac{p_1(x)}{p_0(x)}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma=\frac{\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})}{\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})}.\]

Points where the integrand is exactly 0 can go either way without changing \(\mathcal R\). With \(C_{00}=C_{11}=0\), \(C_{10}=C_{01}=1\), the risk is \(P_e\) and γ = \(\pi_0/\pi_1\): decide H₁ if \(\pi_1p_1(x)>\pi_0p_0(x)\), i.e. pick the larger posterior (MAP). Equal priors as well give ML.

The detector is \(x^2[0]+x^2[1]>2\sigma_0^2\ln(1/P_{FA})\): say H₁ when the point \((x[0],x[1])\) falls outside a circle of radius \(\sqrt{2\sigma_0^2\ln(1/P_{FA})}\). Since \(\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2\lt 1\), \(P_D>P_{FA}\), as it should be.

SPRT on iid \(X_i\), \(p_0\) vs \(p_1\), finite thresholds \(\tau_0,\tau_1\), stopping time N. Can N = ∞? Prove it or give a counterexample.

Solution

Short answer: no, as long as \(p_0\ne p_1\). N is finite with probability 1 (and even \(E\{N\}\lt \infty\)). The only exception is the degenerate case \(p_0=p_1\), where the test never stops.

Set-up. Let \(z_i=\ln\frac{p_1(X_i)}{p_0(X_i)}\) and \(S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nz_i\). The test continues while \(\tau_0\lt S_n\lt \tau_1\) (log thresholds, \(\tau_0\lt 0\lt \tau_1\)), and \(N=\min\{n:S_n\ge\tau_1\text{ or }S_n\le\tau_0\}\). Under either hypothesis the \(z_i\) are iid, so \(S_n\) is a random walk. Let \(D=\tau_1-\tau_0\lt \infty\) be the width of the band.

Claim. If \(p_0\ne p_1\) then \(\Pr\{z_i\ne0\}>0\), so for some ε > 0 either \(\Pr\{z_i>\varepsilon\}=q>0\) or \(\Pr\{z_i\lt -\varepsilon\}=q>0\). Take the first case (the second is the same with the sign flipped).

Proof. Let \(k=\lceil D/\varepsilon\rceil+1\). If k steps in a row all have \(z_i>\varepsilon\), the walk moves up by more than D, so it must leave the band during that block, wherever it started inside it. Split time into blocks of length k: \(\{1,\dots,k\},\{k+1,\dots,2k\},\dots\) Each block is "all big steps" with probability \(\delta=q^k>0\), independently of the other blocks. To still be running after m blocks, none of them can have been all big steps:

\[\Pr\{N>mk\}\le(1-\delta)^m\ \xrightarrow{m\to\infty}\ 0.\]

So \(\Pr\{N=\infty\}=\lim_m\Pr\{N>mk\}=0\). The tail is geometric, so \(E\{N\}=\sum_n\Pr\{N>n\}\le k\sum_m(1-\delta)^m=k/\delta\lt \infty\). This holds under H₀ and H₁ alike.

Counterexample when the condition fails. If \(p_0=p_1\), every \(z_i=0\), so \(S_n=0\) for all n. Since \(\tau_0\lt 0\lt \tau_1\), the walk never leaves the band and N = ∞ with probability 1.

Remark. Even with \(p_0\ne p_1\), single sample paths that never stop can exist. For instance, with Bernoulli data and steps \(+a,-a\), the path +, −, +, −, … stays inside forever. That set of paths has probability zero, so "N < ∞ almost surely" is the precise statement. The thresholds have to be finite for this: with \(\tau_1=\infty\), the test could stop under H₀, where the walk drifts down, but under H₁ it might never stop.

Design the optimal NP detector for \(H_0: X\sim[0.2\ \ 0.3\ \ 0.5]\), \(H_1: X\sim[0.6\ \ 0.1\ \ 0.3]\), with \(P_{FA}=0.05\).

Solution

Short answer: if X = x₁, say H₁ with probability 0.25; otherwise say H₀. \(P_D=0.15\).

X takes three values x₁, x₂, x₃. Compute the likelihood ratio at each one and rank them, because NP fills the "yes" region from the largest ratio down:

x\(p_0\)\(p_1\)\(L=p_1/p_0\)rank
x₁0.20.631
x₂0.30.11/33
x₃0.50.30.62

The ratio isn't monotone in the label, so the order is x₁, x₃, x₂. The possible non-random tests give \(P_{FA}\in\{0,\ 0.2,\ 0.7,\ 1\}\). Even putting only x₁ in the "yes" region gives \(P_{FA}=0.2>0.05\), so the threshold is γ = 3 (sitting on x₁) and we randomise there:

\[\Pr\{L>3;H_0\}=0,\qquad c_0=\frac{0.05-0}{\Pr\{X=x_1;H_0\}}=\frac{0.05}{0.2}=0.25.\] \[\delta(x)=\begin{cases}H_1\text{ w.p. }0.25,\ H_0\text{ w.p. }0.75 & x=x_1\ (L=\gamma=3)\ H_0 & x\in\{x_2,x_3\}\ (L\lt 3)\end{cases}\]

\(P_{FA}=0.25(0.2)=0.05\) and \(P_D=0.25(0.6)=0.15\). The ROC is the polygon through (0, 0), (0.2, 0.6), (0.7, 0.9), (1, 1), and this rule sits a quarter of the way along its first segment.

\(H_0:\mathbf x=\mathbf w\) vs \(H_1:\mathbf x=\mathbf s+\mathbf w\), \(\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\), \(\mathbf s\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,C_s)\), \(C_s=\sigma_s^2\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(|\rho|\le1\). Find \(T(\mathbf x)\), \(\hat{\mathbf s}\) and the canonical estimator-correlator.

Solution

Short answer: with \(y_0=\frac{x[0]+x[1]}{\sqrt2}\), \(y_1=\frac{x[0]-x[1]}{\sqrt2}\), \(\lambda_{0,1}=\sigma_s^2(1\pm\rho)\):

\[T(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^T\hat{\mathbf s}=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_0+\sigma^2}y_0^2+\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\sigma^2}y_1^2\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma'.\]

This is the Lec 9 homework in section 18 with more of the working shown.

1. The LRT. \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\) under H₀ and \(\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,C_s+\sigma^2I)\) under H₁. Dropping constants,

\[\ln L(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^T\Big[\tfrac1{\sigma^2}I-(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\Big]\mathbf x=\frac1{2\sigma^2}\mathbf x^TC_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf x,\]

using the matrix inversion lemma. Absorbing \(1/2\sigma^2\) into the threshold gives \(T=\mathbf x^T\hat{\mathbf s}\).

2. The signal estimate (Wiener / MMSE). \(\hat{\mathbf s}=C_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf x\). For this 2×2 case, with \(D=\det(C_s+\sigma^2I)=(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)^2-\rho^2\sigma_s^4=(\lambda_0+\sigma^2)(\lambda_1+\sigma^2)\),

\[\hat{\mathbf s}=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{D}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_s^2(1-\rho^2)+\sigma^2&\rho\sigma^2\ \rho\sigma^2&\sigma_s^2(1-\rho^2)+\sigma^2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x.\]

Check: ρ = 0 gives \(\hat{\mathbf s}=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}\mathbf x\), the scalar Wiener gain.

3. Canonical form. Diagonalise \(C_s=V\Lambda_sV^T\):

\[V=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \Lambda_s=\mathrm{diag}\big(\sigma_s^2(1+\rho),\ \sigma_s^2(1-\rho)\big).\]

V also diagonalises \(C_s+\sigma^2I\), so \(\hat{\mathbf s}=V\,\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\sigma^2}\big)V^T\mathbf x\). With \(\mathbf y=V^T\mathbf x\) the statistic is the weighted sum in the short answer. The structure: rotate (sum and difference channels), square, weight each channel by its Wiener gain \(\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\sigma^2)\), add.

4. Distributions. \(y_0,y_1\) are independent, \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) under H₀ and \(\mathcal N(0,\lambda_i+\sigma^2)\) under H₁. T is a weighted sum of two χ²₁ variables, so γ′ for a given \(P_{FA}\) is found numerically in general.

5. Special cases.

  • ρ = 0: equal weights \(w=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}\), \(T\propto x^2[0]+x^2[1]\), the energy detector. \(T/(w\sigma^2)\sim\chi^2_2\) under H₀, so \(P_{FA}=e^{-\gamma'/2w\sigma^2}\).
  • ρ → 1: \(\lambda_1=0\), only the sum channel is kept, \(T\propto(x[0]+x[1])^2\). The signal is the same in both samples, so average them.
  • ρ → −1: only the difference channel, \(T\propto(x[0]-x[1])^2\).

With \(h[k]=s[N-1-k]\) for \(k=0,\dots,N-1\) (0 otherwise) and \(y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h[k]x[n-k]\), show the output for \(x[n]=s[n]\) is maximised at \(n=N-1\) with maximum value \(\mathcal E=\sum s^2[n]\).

Solution

Short answer: the output is the signal's autocorrelation, \(y[n]=r_{ss}[N-1-n]\), and an autocorrelation peaks at lag 0 with value \(\mathcal E\).

Substitute x = s and h:

\[y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}s[N-1-k]\,s[n-k].\]

Let m = N − 1 − k (so k = N − 1 − m, and m also runs over 0, …, N − 1):

\[y[n]=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}s[m]\,s[m+n-(N-1)]=r_{ss}[\,n-(N-1)\,],\qquad r_{ss}[l]=\sum_m s[m]\,s[m+l],\]

with s = 0 outside 0, …, N − 1. (\(r_{ss}\) is even, so this equals \(r_{ss}[N-1-n]\).)

Bound. By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[|r_{ss}[l]|\le\sqrt{\sum_m s^2[m]}\sqrt{\sum_m s^2[m+l]}\le\sqrt{\mathcal E}\sqrt{\mathcal E}=\mathcal E,\]

since a shifted copy can't have more energy than the whole signal.

Attained at n = N − 1. There l = 0 and

\[y[N-1]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}s[N-1-k]\,s[N-1-k]=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}s^2[m]=\mathcal E.\]

So \(y[n]\le\mathcal E=y[N-1]\) for every n. For a general input the same substitution gives \(y[N-1]=\sum_n x[n]s[n]\), which is why the two structures (correlator, and matched filter sampled at N − 1) are equivalent.

14.4 Self-test

Cover the answers, say each one aloud, then check.

  • Why is the LRT optimal for both Bayes and NP?Both objectives are integrals over \(R_1\) of a combination \(p(\bx;\Ho)-c\,p(\bx;\Hz)\), maximised pointwise by putting \(\bx\) in \(R_1\) when the combination is positive. Only \(c\) (the threshold) differs.
  • What does a cost or a prior change in the detector?Only the threshold, never the statistic.
  • When do you need a randomised test?NP with discrete data, when no threshold gives \(\PFA=\alpha\) exactly. Never for Bayes.
  • Why can the NP threshold be set without the \(\Ho\) model?\(\PFA\) is an integral of \(p(\bx;\Hz)\) only.
  • What is \(d^2\) for a DC level, a known signal, coloured noise, two signals?\(NA^2/\sigma^2\); \(\E/\sigma^2\); \(\bs^T\bC^{-1}\bs\); \(\|\bs_1-\bs_0\|^2/\sigma^2\).
  • Why is the ROC concave?Mixing two NP tests gives any point on the chord; the NP test at that \(\PFA\) is at least as good.
  • What is the slope of the ROC?The LRT threshold \(\gamma\) that produced the point.
  • Is the sample mean sufficient for a DC level in Laplacian noise?No; the detector uses \(\sum(|x|-|x-A|)\), a sum of clipped samples.
  • When is extra data irrelevant?When its conditional PDF given the other data is the same under both hypotheses. Same marginal is not enough.
  • Why does the signal shape not matter in white noise?\(d^2=\E/\sigma^2\) depends only on energy.
  • How should you design a signal for coloured noise?Put all the energy on the eigenvector of \(\bC\) with the smallest eigenvalue: \(d^2=\E/\lambda_{\min}\).
  • Which binary signal set is best, and by how much?Antipodal; 3 dB better than orthogonal, 6 dB better than on–off at equal peak energy.
  • Why must a random-signal detector be quadratic?Both hypotheses have zero mean and differ only in covariance, so \(\ln L\) is a quadratic form in \(\bx\). A linear statistic has the same (zero) mean under both.
  • What is \(\hat\bs\) in the estimator–correlator?The MMSE (Wiener) estimate \(E[\bs\mid\bx]=\bC_s(\bC_s+\sigma^2\bI)^{-1}\bx\).
  • When does a UMP test exist?When one test, with a threshold free of the unknown, is NP-optimal for every \(\theta\in\Theta_1\): typically a one-sided problem with a monotone likelihood ratio.
  • GLRT vs Bayesian approach?Maximise over the unknown vs integrate it against a prior. For the two-sided DC level both give \(\bar x^2\).
  • What is CFAR and which detector has it?Constant false-alarm rate: the \(\Hz\) distribution of \(T\) does not depend on the nuisance parameter. \(\bar x^2/\hat\sigma_1^2\).
  • Why does the SPRT stop?A block of \(M\) large steps has positive probability \(q\); the chance of still running after \(j\) blocks is at most \((1-q)^j\to0\).
  • Wald's thresholds?\(A\approx(1-\beta)/\alpha\), \(B\approx\beta/(1-\alpha)\); guaranteed only \(\alpha'\le\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}\), \(\beta'\le\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\) and \(\alpha'+\beta'\le\alpha+\beta\); in practice the overshoot usually makes both errors smaller than the targets.
  • Why is the SPRT faster on average?It stops as soon as the evidence is decisive; \(E\{N\}=E\{S_N\}/E\{z\}\) grows like \(\ln(1/\alpha)/D\), about a quarter of the fixed-sample size for small errors.
