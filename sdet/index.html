01The big idea

What is a detection problem? Lec 1

You get some noisy measurements and must answer a yes/no question. A radar asks whether there's an aircraft or just noise. A Wi-Fi receiver asks whether a 0 or a 1 was sent. An ECG monitor asks whether a heartbeat is normal.

The two possible answers are the hypotheses: H₀ = "nothing there" and H₁ = "signal there". Formally, the data \(\mathbf x=[x[0],\dots,x[N-1]]^T\) comes from \(p(\mathbf x;H_0)\) or \(p(\mathbf x;H_1)\).

Every detector in this course works the same way: compute one number from the data, compare it to a threshold, and say yes if it's bigger. The topics differ only in which number you compute and where the threshold comes from.

The course goes from easy to hard:

  1. Both distributions fully known (Bayes, Neyman–Pearson)
  2. A known signal in noise (matched filter), then telling M known signals apart
  3. A random signal (estimate it, then match)
  4. A signal with unknown parts (UMP, Bayesian, GLRT)
  5. Data arriving one sample at a time (SPRT)

"Everything from here on is about computing \(L(\mathbf x)\) for richer and richer data models. Not about inventing new decision rules." (Lec 6)

02Two ways to be wrong

What can go wrong when you say yes or no? Lec 1

Truth: nothing there (H₀)Truth: signal there (H₁)
You say "no"✓ correct rejection✗ miss, \(P_M\) (Type II, β)
You say "yes"✗ false alarm, \(P_{FA}\) (Type I, α, "size")✓ detection, \(P_D=1-P_M\) ("power")

You can't make both errors small for free. Always saying "yes" gives zero misses, but every noise blip becomes a false alarm. You have to pick a trade-off, and Bayes and Neyman–Pearson (the next sections) are two ways of picking it.

Average error: \(P_e = P(H_0)P_{FA} + P(H_1)P_M\).

In depth: decision regions more

A decision rule splits the space of all possible data into two regions: \(R_1\) (say yes) and \(R_0\) (say no). Designing a detector means choosing \(R_1\).

\[P_{FA}=\int_{R_1}p(\mathbf x;H_0)\,d\mathbf x,\qquad P_D=\int_{R_1}p(\mathbf x;H_1)\,d\mathbf x.\]

Every problem has three ingredients: a data model (the PDF under each hypothesis), a criterion of optimality, and a performance analysis (\(P_{FA}\), \(P_D\), \(P_e\), ROC).

Radar: the errors are very asymmetric and there are no sensible priors, so use Neyman–Pearson. Communications: the errors are symmetric and the priors are known by design, so use Bayes / minimum \(P_e\).

03The one tool: the likelihood ratio

How do you turn data into a yes/no? Lec 1–3

Ask how much more likely this data is if the signal is there than if it isn't.

Likelihood ratio test (LRT)
\[L(\mathbf x)=\frac{p(\mathbf x;H_1)}{p(\mathbf x;H_0)}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma\]

Say yes (H₁) if \(L > \gamma\). γ is the threshold.

For Gaussian noise the ratio almost always simplifies to an average, a sum of squares or a correlation. The same recipe works every time:

\(L(\mathbf{x})\)→take \(\ln\)→drop terms without \(\mathbf{x}\)→\(T(\mathbf{x}) \gtrless \gamma'\)→distribution of \(T\) under H₀, H₁→\(P_{FA},\ P_D,\ P_e\)
Trap
  • Taking logs, adding constants and multiplying by positive numbers are all safe, because the threshold absorbs them.
  • Dividing by something whose sign you don't know (like an unknown amplitude A) flips the inequality and breaks the test. That's why two-sided problems have no UMP test.

Sufficient statistic

This is the one number \(T(\mathbf x)\) that holds all the useful information. For a DC level it's the average; for a change in power it's the sum of squares. Once you have it you can forget the raw data, and \(P_{FA}\), \(P_D\) come from the distribution of that single number.

In depth: Neyman–Fisher factorisation theory

\(T\) is sufficient for θ if the data is independent of θ once you know \(T\). The test for it:

\[p(\mathbf{x};\theta)=g\big(T(\mathbf{x}),\theta\big)\,h(\mathbf{x})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\text{ sufficient}.\]

In detection, if \(p(\mathbf{x};H_i)=g_i(T(\mathbf x))h(\mathbf x)\), then \(L=g_1(T)/g_0(T)\) depends on the data only through \(T\).

  • Coin tosses with \(\theta=P(X_i=1)\): \(T=\sum X_i\), since \(p=\theta^{T}(1-\theta)^{n-T}\).
  • DC level in WGN: \(T=\sum x[n]\) (see Tutorial Q2).
  • Unknown variance: \(T=\sum x^2[n]\).
  • Exponential family \(p(x;\theta)=\exp[A(\theta)B(x)+C(x)+D(\theta)]\) (Kay 3.11): \(T=\sum B(x[n])\). This is a quick way to guess \(T\) before deriving the LRT.

04Bayes: when you know the odds

Where does the threshold come from if you know how often each case happens? Lec 1–3, 6

In communications you know 0s and 1s are sent equally often, and you know what each mistake costs. Bayes uses that to minimise the average cost (the Bayes risk).

Bayes threshold
\[\gamma=\frac{(C_{10}-C_{00})\,P(H_0)}{(C_{01}-C_{11})\,P(H_1)}\]

\(C_{ij}\) = cost of deciding \(H_i\) when \(H_j\) is true. Read it as (chance of no signal × cost of a false alarm) over (chance of signal × cost of a miss).

If both mistakes cost the same, \(\gamma = P(H_0)/P(H_1)\). That's the MAP detector: pick the more probable answer. If the priors are also equal, \(\gamma = 1\): the ML detector, which picks the answer that best explains the data.

Priors and costs only move the threshold. The test statistic stays the same.
Intuition
If the signal is usually there (P(H₁) = ¾), the threshold drops and you say "yes" more easily. You accept a few more false alarms to cut misses.
In depth: why minimising Bayes risk gives the LRT derive
\[\mathcal{R}=\sum_{i=0}^{1}\sum_{j=0}^{1}C_{ij}\,P(H_j)\Pr\{\text{decide }H_i\mid H_j\}.\]
  1. Write \(\Pr\{\text{decide }H_0\mid H_j\} = 1-\int_{R_1}p(\mathbf{x}|H_j)d\mathbf{x}\) and collect terms: \[\mathcal{R}=C_{00}P(H_0)+C_{01}P(H_1)+\int_{R_1}\Big[(C_{10}-C_{00})P(H_0)p(\mathbf{x}|H_0)-(C_{01}-C_{11})P(H_1)p(\mathbf{x}|H_1)\Big]d\mathbf{x}.\]
  2. The first two terms are fixed. To minimise, put \(\mathbf{x}\) in \(R_1\) exactly where the integrand is negative.
  3. With \(C_{10}>C_{00}\) and \(C_{01}>C_{11}\), that is \(L(\mathbf x)>\gamma\) with the γ above.

Posterior-cost form (works for any number of hypotheses): decide the \(H_i\) with the smallest expected cost \(C_i(\mathbf x)=\sum_j C_{ij}P(H_j|\mathbf{x})\). With 0/1 costs, pick the largest \(p(\mathbf x|H_i)P(H_i)\) (M-ary MAP); with equal priors too, the largest \(p(\mathbf x|H_i)\) (M-ary ML). A worked 3-hypothesis example is in the homework section.

In depth: two Gaussians with the same covariance example

\(H_0:\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu_0,C)\) vs \(H_1:\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu_1,C)\). The quadratic terms cancel, leaving a linear detector:

\[T(\mathbf{x})=(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)^TC^{-1}\mathbf{x}\ \gtrless\ \ln\gamma+\tfrac12\big(\boldsymbol\mu_1^TC^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0^TC^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_0\big).\]

With \(\mathbf h = C^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)\), \(T\mid H_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf h^T\boldsymbol\mu_i,\ \mathbf h^TC\mathbf h)\), and \(d^2=(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)^TC^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)\) (the Mahalanobis distance).

In depth: DC level in Laplacian noise (classwork) example

Noise \(f(w)=\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|w|/b}\). Then

\[\ln L(\mathbf{x})=\frac1b\sum_{n}\Big(|x[n]|-|x[n]-A|\Big)\ \gtrless\ \ln\gamma.\]

Each term \(g(x)=|x|-|x-A|\) is a limiter: \(-A\) for \(x\le 0\), \(2x-A\) in between, \(+A\) for \(x\ge A\). The detector clips each sample, then sums. The sample mean is not sufficient here, because heavy-tailed noise makes big samples untrustworthy. For large N, use the CLT on the sum to get performance.

In depth: irrelevant data theory

Adding extra data \(\mathbf x_2\) gives \(L(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2)=\frac{p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_1)}{p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_0)}L(\mathbf x_1)\). So \(\mathbf x_2\) is irrelevant iff \(p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_1)=p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_0)\).

It is not enough for \(\mathbf x_2\) on its own to be independent of the hypothesis. A noise-only reference channel whose noise is correlated with the signal channel lets you cancel noise, which helps detection.

05Neyman–Pearson: when you don't know the odds

What if nobody can tell you how likely an enemy aircraft is? Lec 3–5

In radar there's no sensible prior, and a miss is far worse than a false alarm. So the rule changes: decide how many false alarms you can live with, then catch as many real signals as possible.

Neyman–Pearson theorem
\[\text{Maximise } P_D \text{ subject to } P_{FA}=\alpha \ \Rightarrow\ \text{say yes if } L(\mathbf x)>\gamma,\ \text{ with } \gamma \text{ chosen so that } P_{FA}=\alpha.\]

γ comes from the noise-only distribution \(p(T;H_0)\) alone.

Bayes and Neyman–Pearson give the same test. They pick the threshold differently.
In depth: proof sketch derive

Lagrangian: maximise \(F=P_D+\lambda(P_{FA}-\alpha)=\int_{R_1}\big[p(\mathbf x;H_1)+\lambda p(\mathbf x;H_0)\big]d\mathbf x-\lambda\alpha\). Include \(\mathbf x\) in \(R_1\) wherever the integrand is positive, i.e. \(p_1>-\lambda p_0\). Set \(\gamma=-\lambda>0\) and choose it to meet \(P_{FA}=\alpha\).

Optimality against any competitor \(\tilde\delta\) with \(P_{FA}(\tilde\delta)\le\alpha\): \(\int(\delta-\tilde\delta)(p_1-\gamma p_0)\,d\mathbf x\ge0\) pointwise, so \(P_D(\delta)-P_D(\tilde\delta)\ge\gamma\big(P_{FA}(\delta)-P_{FA}(\tilde\delta)\big)\ge0\).

The full theorem has three parts: optimality (any size-α rule of LRT form beats every rule with \(P_{FA}\le\alpha\)), existence (for every α ∈ (0,1) there's one with a constant randomisation \(c_0\)), and uniqueness (every NP rule has this form except on a set of zero probability).

Discrete data: flip a coin at the boundary

With counts (like binomial data) \(P_{FA}\) jumps in steps, so you may not hit α exactly. When the data lands exactly on the threshold, say "yes" with probability \(c_0\):

\[c_0=\frac{\alpha-\Pr\{L>\gamma;H_0\}}{\Pr\{L=\gamma;H_0\}}.\]
Example (classwork): binomial, n = 10, p₀ = 0.5, p₁ = 0.8, α = 0.05

The LRT reduces to counting heads, x. (Its coefficient \(\ln\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}\) is positive since \(p_1>p_0\).)

  • Saying yes at x ≥ 9 gives \(P_{FA}=11/1024=0.011\). Too small.
  • Saying yes at x ≥ 8 gives \(P_{FA}=56/1024=0.055\). Too big.
  • \(\Pr\{X=8;H_0\}=45/1024=0.0439\), so \(c_0=(0.05-0.0107)/0.0439=0.893\).
  • Rule: x ≥ 9 → yes; x = 8 → yes with probability 0.893; x ≤ 7 → no.
  • \(P_D=\Pr\{X\ge9;H_1\}+0.893\Pr\{X=8;H_1\}=0.376+0.893(0.302)\approx0.646\).

06The main example: a constant in noise

Most other topics build on this example. Lec 2–4

You get N samples. With no signal they're pure noise \(w[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\). With the signal, each sample has a known constant \(A>0\) added: \(x[n]=A+w[n]\).

The test: average the samples, and say yes if the average is big. Averaging N samples cuts the noise variance by N but keeps A, so more samples means better detection.
Performance (the formula you'll use most)
\[P_D=Q\Big(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-\sqrt{d^2}\Big),\qquad d^2=\frac{N A^2}{\sigma^2}\]

d² (the deflection coefficient) is the signal strength after processing: N × the per-sample SNR. Bigger is better, and doubling N adds 3 dB.

With equal priors (Bayes, minimum \(P_e\)), the threshold sits halfway at A/2 and \(P_e=Q\big(\tfrac12\sqrt{d^2}\big)\).

Design example from the slides

We want \(P_D = 0.99\) at \(P_{FA} = 10^{-6}\) with per-sample SNR \(A^2/\sigma^2 = 0.1\). How many samples?

\(\sqrt{d^2} = Q^{-1}(10^{-6}) - Q^{-1}(0.99) = 4.75 + 2.33 = 7.08\), so \(d^2 \approx 50.1\) (17 dB). Then N = 50.1 / 0.1 ≈ 501 samples.

In depth: deriving the detector, \(P_{FA}\), \(P_D\) and \(P_e\) derive
\[\ln L(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n\big[(x[n]-A)^2-x[n]^2\big]=\frac{A}{\sigma^2}\sum_n x[n]-\frac{NA^2}{2\sigma^2}\ \gtrless\ \ln\gamma\] \[\Rightarrow\quad T(\mathbf{x})=\bar{x}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma'=\frac{\sigma^2}{NA}\ln\gamma+\frac{A}{2}.\]

\(\bar x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2/N)\) under \(H_0\) and \(\mathcal{N}(A,\sigma^2/N)\) under \(H_1\), so

\[P_{FA}=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right),\qquad P_D=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-A}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right).\]

NP: set \(\gamma'=\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}\,Q^{-1}(P_{FA})\) and substitute into \(P_D\) to get the boxed formula.

Bayes, equal priors: γ = 1 gives γ′ = A/2, both errors equal \(Q\big(\sqrt{NA^2/4\sigma^2}\big)\), so \(P_e=Q(\tfrac12\sqrt{d^2})\). Moving γ′ right lowers \(P_{FA}\) and raises \(P_M\).

General definition: \(d^2=\frac{(E[T;H_1]-E[T;H_0])^2}{\mathrm{var}(T;H_0)}\). For any linear statistic \(T=\mathbf h^T\mathbf x\) of Gaussian data, \(d^2=\frac{(\mathbf h^T(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0))^2}{\mathbf h^TC\mathbf h}\), and the same \(P_D\) formula holds. Here \(d^2=NA^2/\sigma^2=\mathcal E/\sigma^2\) with \(\mathcal E=NA^2\): only the total energy matters.

07The ROC curve

How do you see the whole trade-off at once? Lec 4–5

Slide the threshold from very high to very low and plot \(P_D\) against \(P_{FA}\). That curve is the receiver operating characteristic (ROC). Try it:

Grey: d² = 1, 4, 9, 16. Blue: your d². Dashed diagonal: guessing. A stronger signal bends the curve toward the top-left corner (a perfect detector).
  • It always runs from (0,0) to (1,1), never goes down, and stays on or above the diagonal.
  • It's concave, and its slope at each point equals the threshold γ that produced it.
  • To design with it, take \(P_{FA}\) from the spec and the \(P_D\) you need, then read off the d² that gets you there and convert it to an SNR.
Trap
Only compare detectors at the same false-alarm rate, or compare whole curves. Area under the curve averages over operating points you may never use (in radar only \(P_{FA}\lesssim10^{-4}\) matters).
In depth: why the slope equals γ, and why the ROC is concave derive

With \(T=L\): \(\frac{dP_D}{dP_{FA}}=\frac{p_L(\gamma;H_1)}{p_L(\gamma;H_0)}=\gamma\), because \(p_L(l;H_1)=l\,p_L(l;H_0)\). As \(P_{FA}\) grows, γ shrinks, so the slope decreases and the curve is concave.

Concavity another way (Kay 3.13): flip a coin to choose between two detectors on the ROC. The mixed test lands on the chord between them. The NP detector at that \(P_{FA}\) does at least as well, so the ROC lies above every chord.

08The energy detector and other NP examples

What if the signal doesn't shift the average but makes things noisier? Lec 5

If the signal shows up only as extra power (variance goes from \(\sigma_0^2\) up to \(\sigma_1^2\)), the average stays at zero and tells you nothing.

The test: add up the squares of the samples (the total energy), and say yes if it's big.
  • A sum of squared Gaussians follows a chi-squared (χ²) distribution, which gives \(P_{FA}\) and \(P_D\). \(\chi^2_N\) has mean N and variance 2N.
  • For N = 2 there's an exact answer: \(P_D = P_{FA}^{\ \sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2}\).
  • For large N, χ² looks like a bell curve, so you can use Q.
In depth: variance change, exact performance derive
\[\ln L=-\frac N2\ln\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_0^2}+\frac12\Big(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}\Big)\sum x^2[n]\ \Rightarrow\ T(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n]\ \gtrless\ \gamma'.\]

\(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}>0\), so the direction is preserved. Under \(H_i\), \(T/\sigma_i^2\sim\chi^2_N\):

\[P_{FA}=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_0^2}\right),\qquad P_D=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(P_{FA})\right).\]

N = 2: \(Q_{\chi^2_2}(y)=e^{-y/2}\), so \(P_{FA}=e^{-\gamma'/2\sigma_0^2}\), \(P_D=e^{-\gamma'/2\sigma_1^2}\) and \(P_D=P_{FA}^{\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2}\) (this is Kay 3.9).

In depth: exponential data, exact ROC (classwork) derive

\(p(x;H_i)=\lambda_ie^{-\lambda_ix}\) with \(\lambda_1<\lambda_0\) (bigger mean under \(H_1\)).

\[\ln L=N\ln\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}+(\lambda_0-\lambda_1)\sum x[n]\ \Rightarrow\ T=\sum x[n]\gtrless\gamma'.\]

N = 1: \(P_{FA}=e^{-\lambda_0\gamma'}\), \(P_D=e^{-\lambda_1\gamma'}\), so \(P_D=P_{FA}^{\lambda_1/\lambda_0}\). General N: \(2\lambda_iT\sim\chi^2_{2N}\), so \(P_D=Q_{\chi^2_{2N}}\big(\tfrac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_{2N}}(P_{FA})\big)\).

09Toolkit: Q, Gaussians, χ²

The facts every derivation leans on. Lec 2 digressions

The Q function

\(Q(x)\) is the chance that a standard bell curve lands above x. \(Q^{-1}\) goes the other way. If \(T\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \(\Pr\{T>\gamma\}=Q\big(\frac{\gamma-\mu}{\sigma}\big)\). Remember \(Q(0)=0.5\) and \(Q(-x)=1-Q(x)\).

Why Gaussians are everywhere

Receiver noise is Gaussian (central limit theorem). Gaussians stay Gaussian under linear operations, so a linear detector's output is Gaussian and you can use Q. Log-likelihood ratios of Gaussians are quadratic, so χ² handles the rest.

Chi-squared

Sum of N squared standard Gaussians: \(\chi^2_N\), mean N, variance 2N. With nonzero means it's non-central, \(\chi'^2_N(\lambda)\), mean N + λ, variance 2N + 4λ, where λ is the sum of squared means.

In depth: Gaussian properties, Q bounds, χ² details reference
\[p(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\det^{1/2}(C)}\exp\!\Big[-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^TC^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\Big]\]
  1. Fully specified by \((\boldsymbol\mu,C)\).
  2. Uncorrelated ⇔ independent.
  3. Linear maps: \(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\sim\mathcal N(A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b,\ ACA^T)\); hence \(\mathbf h^T\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf h^T\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf h^TC\mathbf h)\).
  4. Marginals are Gaussian.
  5. Conditionals: \(\mathbf x_1|\mathbf x_2\sim\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_1+C_{12}C_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf x_2-\boldsymbol\mu_2),\ C_{11}-C_{12}C_{22}^{-1}C_{21}\big)\).

Q bounds (x > 0): \(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1+x^2)}e^{-x^2/2}

χ²: \(Q_{\chi^2_2}(y)=e^{-y/2}\) (χ²₂ is exponential with mean 2). For N ≳ 30, \(Q_{\chi^2_N}(y)\approx Q\big((y-N)/\sqrt{2N}\big)\), fine in the body but poor in the far tail.

More distributions (Kay Ch 2): if \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,C)\) then \(\mathbf x^TC^{-1}\mathbf x\sim\chi^2_N\). The envelope \(\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}\) of two iid zero-mean Gaussians is Rayleigh (Rician with nonzero means). A ratio of normalised χ² variables is F-distributed, which shows up in CFAR tests.

10Known signal: the matched filter

You know exactly what the signal looks like (e.g. your own radar pulse). How do you find it in noise? Lec 6

The test: multiply the data by a copy of the signal, sample by sample, and add it up (correlate). If the signal is there, the products line up and the sum gets big.
Matched filter / replica-correlator
\[T=\sum_n x[n]\,s[n]=\mathbf x^T\mathbf s,\qquad P_D=Q\Big(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-\sqrt{\mathcal E/\sigma^2}\Big)\]

\(\mathcal E=\sum s^2[n]\) is the signal energy.

  • It's called a matched filter because you can build it as an FIR filter whose impulse response is the signal flipped in time, \(h[k]=s[N-1-k]\), sampled at \(n=N-1\).
  • It gives the highest output SNR of any linear filter.
  • In white noise only the signal's energy matters. A pulse, a chirp and a sine with equal energy are equally easy to detect.
In depth: performance, max-SNR proof, why sample at N−1 derive

This is the "two Gaussians" problem with \(\boldsymbol\mu_0=\mathbf 0\), \(\boldsymbol\mu_1=\mathbf s\), \(C=\sigma^2I\). \(T\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2\mathcal E)\) under \(H_0\) and \(\mathcal N(\mathcal E,\sigma^2\mathcal E)\) under \(H_1\), so \(d^2=\mathcal E/\sigma^2\). Threshold: \(\sigma^2\ln\gamma+\mathcal E/2\) for Bayes.

Max SNR: output SNR \(=\frac{(\mathbf h^T\mathbf s)^2}{\sigma^2\mathbf h^T\mathbf h}\le\frac{\mathcal E}{\sigma^2}\) by Cauchy–Schwarz, with equality iff \(\mathbf h\propto\mathbf s\).

Why N−1: the signal part of the output is \(y_s[n]=r_{ss}[N-1-n]\), an autocorrelation, which peaks at lag 0, i.e. \(n=N-1\). This assumes the signal starts at n = 0.

Known sinusoid \(s[n]=A\cos2\pi f_0n\): \(\mathcal E\approx NA^2/2\) for \(f_0\) not near 0 or ½, so \(d^2\approx NA^2/2\sigma^2\).

11Coloured (correlated) noise

What if the noise samples are correlated, or some are noisier than others? Lec 7

  1. Whiten: apply a filter that turns the noise back into plain white noise.
  2. Run the normal matched filter on the whitened data.
Generalized matched filter
\[T=\mathbf x^TC^{-1}\mathbf s,\qquad d^2=\mathbf s^TC^{-1}\mathbf s\]
  • Now the signal's shape matters. Put the signal's energy where the noise is weakest: the best signal points along the eigenvector of C with the smallest eigenvalue, giving \(d^2=\mathcal E/\lambda_{\min}\).
  • If some samples are noisier, trust them less: with \(C=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_n^2)\), \(T=\sum x[n]s[n]/\sigma_n^2\).
  • If two noise samples are almost identical, subtracting them cancels the noise, so correlated noise can make detection easier.

12Which signal was sent?

In communications a signal is always there. The question is which one. Lec 8

The test: pick the known signal closest to what you received (the minimum-distance receiver).
Binary error rate (equal priors, white noise)

It depends only on the distance between the signals. For a fixed energy, make them as different as possible.

13Random signals

What if the signal itself is random, like speech, a fading radio signal or EEG? Lec 9

You can't correlate against a waveform you don't know. The signal \(\mathbf s\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,C_s)\) has zero mean, so both hypotheses average to zero. The only difference is in the power and correlation pattern, so the detector is quadratic.

The test (estimator-correlator): first make your best guess of the signal from the data (a Wiener filter), then correlate the data with that guess.
Estimator-correlator
\[T(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^T\hat{\mathbf s},\qquad\hat{\mathbf s}=C_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf x\]
  • If the signal is white (\(C_s=\sigma_s^2I\)), the best guess is a scaled copy of the data, and the test becomes the energy detector again.
  • If it has structure, rotate to the signal's natural directions (eigenvectors) and weight each one by how much signal it carries: \(T=\sum_n\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n+\sigma^2}y^2[n]\). Directions that are mostly noise get ignored.
In depth: derivation and canonical form derive
\[\ln L=-\tfrac12\mathbf x^T\big[(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}-\tfrac1{\sigma^2}I\big]\mathbf x+\text{const}.\]

The matrix inversion lemma gives \(\tfrac1{\sigma^2}I-(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}=\tfrac1{\sigma^2}C_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\), so \(T=\mathbf x^T\hat{\mathbf s}\). Here \(\hat{\mathbf s}=E[\mathbf s|\mathbf x]\) is the MMSE estimate from the estimation half of the course.

Canonical form: with \(C_s=V\Lambda_sV^T\) and \(\mathbf y=V^T\mathbf x\), \(T=\sum_n\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n+\sigma^2}y^2[n]\). Under \(H_0\), \(y[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\); under \(H_1\), \(\mathcal N(0,\lambda_n+\sigma^2)\), independent. T is a weighted sum of χ²₁ variables, which has no closed-form CDF (compute numerically).

White case performance: \(T=\sum x^2[n]\); \(T/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_N\) under \(H_0\) and \(T/(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)\sim\chi^2_N\) under \(H_1\).

Extensions: coloured noise \(C_w\): prewhiten, then use the MMSE estimator (Kay problems 5.10, 5.11). Nonzero signal mean: T gets a linear (matched-filter) term plus the quadratic term, \(T=\mathbf x^TC_w^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_s+\tfrac12\mathbf x^TC_w^{-1}C_s(C_s+C_w)^{-1}\mathbf x\) (Kay §5.6).

14When something is unknown

What if you don't know the amplitude, the phase or the noise level? Lec 10

The likelihood ratio needs those values, so you can't compute it directly. Performance is now a curve over the unknown value (the power function), not a single number.

Case 1: one test works for every value (UMP)

If you know A is positive but not its size, "average is big → yes" works for every A > 0, and the threshold doesn't depend on A. That's a uniformly most powerful (UMP) test, with \(P_D(A)=Q\big(Q^{-1}(\alpha)-\sqrt{NA^2/\sigma^2}\big)\).

Trap
If A could be positive or negative, no single test is best. "Big average" catches positive A and "small average" catches negative A. No UMP test exists for two-sided problems, vector unknowns, or nuisance parameters like an unknown σ².
α = 0.05, √N/σ = 1. Orange: "average is big" (best for A > 0). Teal: "average is small" (best for A < 0). Dashed: the GLRT, "|average| is big", which works on both sides at a small cost.

Case 2: two ways out

Bayesian: average over the unknown. Assume a prior for A and integrate it out. You need a prior.
GLRT: plug in your best guess. Replace the unknown with its maximum-likelihood estimate, then do the normal test. You need a computable MLE. It's usually easier to carry out than the Bayesian route.

15Sequential detection (SPRT)

What if every sample is expensive, like testing a shell that gets destroyed? Lec 11

Instead of fixing N in advance, look at samples one at a time and stop as soon as you're sure. Wald came up with this in 1943 for testing artillery shells, and it was classified until 1945. It's now used in clinical trials, spectrum sensing for cognitive radio and online A/B testing.

Keep a running score, the sum of log-likelihood ratios \(S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\frac{p_1(x_i)}{p_0(x_i)}\). Above the top line, say yes. Below the bottom line, say no. In between, take another sample.
Data is \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) vs \(\mathcal N(1,1)\), so each step adds \(x_i-\tfrac12\). Six runs of the same test stop after different numbers of samples.
Where the lines go (Wald's approximations)
\[\text{top}=\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha},\qquad\text{bottom}=\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\]

α = the false-alarm rate you want, β = the miss rate you want. You don't need anything else about the data to set them.

  • The number of samples N is now random. The goal is to make its average, \(E\{N\}\), small.
  • The test always stops eventually (as long as \(p_0\ne p_1\)).
  • The real error rates come out a bit better than requested, because the score overshoots the line. Guarantee: \(\alpha'+\beta'\le\alpha+\beta\).
  • On average it needs far fewer samples than a fixed-N test: about half in the example below, and up to 4× fewer in Poor's example. That saving is why it was kept secret.
  • Average samples ≈ (distance to the line) ÷ (how fast the score drifts). The drift is the KL divergence between the two distributions.
  • It has costs too. A single run can take a long time, you must know both distributions exactly, and the theory assumes independent samples.
Average sample number
\[E_1\{N\}\approx\frac{(1-\beta)\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}+\beta\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}{D(p_1\|p_0)},\qquad E_0\{N\}\approx\frac{\alpha\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}+(1-\alpha)\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}{-D(p_0\|p_1)}\]

\(D(p\|q)=\int p\ln\frac pq\) is the KL divergence (not symmetric). For two Gaussians with the same σ, \(D=\frac{(\mu_1-\mu_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}\).

Example: \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) vs \(\mathcal N(1,1)\), α = β = 0.05

D = 0.5, top line = ln 19 = 2.94. \(E_1\{N\}\approx\frac{0.95(2.94)-0.05(2.94)}{0.5}\approx5.3\) samples. A fixed-N NP test with the same errors needs \(\sqrt N\ge2\times1.645\), so N = 11.

In depth: notation, termination, random walk theory

Notation changes: data \(x_i\), densities \(p_0,p_1\); α = Pr₀{accept H₁}, β = Pr₁{accept H₀}. A test of strength (α, β) has four performance numbers: α, β, E₀{N}, E₁{N}.

Product form: \(\Lambda_n=\prod_{i\le n}\frac{p_1(x_i)}{p_0(x_i)}\), thresholds \(0

\(S_n\) is a random walk with iid steps \(z_i\); N is its first-passage time. Drift: \(E_1\{z\}=D(p_1\|p_0)>0\), \(E_0\{z\}=-D(p_0\|p_1)<0\).

Termination: split time into blocks of length k. Each block has some fixed probability δ > 0 of a jump bigger than a − b, which exits the interval. So \(\Pr\{N>mk\}\le(1-\delta)^m\to0\).

In depth: Wald's inequalities and the error bound derive

On every path that accepts \(H_1\), \(p_{1n}\ge A\,p_{0n}\). Summing over those paths: \(1-\beta\ge A\alpha\), so \(A\le\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}\). Similarly on paths that accept \(H_0\), \(\beta\le B(1-\alpha)\), so \(B\ge\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\). Equality would need no overshoot, which gives Wald's approximations.

Bound: designing with those thresholds and getting real errors (α′, β′), the inequalities give \(\alpha'(1-\beta)\le\alpha(1-\beta')\) and \(\beta'(1-\alpha)\le\beta(1-\alpha')\). Adding them: \(\alpha'+\beta'\le\alpha+\beta\). Individually \(\alpha'\le\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}\), \(\beta'\le\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\).

The slides' simulation got real errors of about 0.029 and 0.030 for nominal 0.05/0.05, paid for with extra samples. The SPRT is optimal (Wald–Wolfowitz): among tests with errors ≤ (α, β), it minimises both E₀{N} and E₁{N}.

Book results that extend the lectures. Kay Vol. II, Ch 4–7, 10

  • For a sine wave with unknown phase, correlate with both a cosine and a sine, then square and add. That's the periodogram (one FFT bin). If the frequency is unknown too, look for the tallest FFT peak, which is why nearly all narrowband detectors contain an FFT.
  • Each unknown costs some performance. From best to worst: known signal (matched filter), unknown amplitude, unknown phase, unknown frequency, and knowing only the energy (energy detector).

  • For weak signals, the LMP test uses the slope of the log-likelihood at "no signal". For the DC level that's the average again.
  • In non-Gaussian noise, pass each sample through a nonlinearity and then use a matched filter. With spiky noise (Laplacian, Cauchy) the nonlinearity clips or ignores huge samples, because a big spike is probably noise.

Eigenvalues \(\sigma_s^2(1\pm\rho)\), with \(y[0]=\frac{x[0]+x[1]}{\sqrt2}\), \(y[1]=\frac{x[0]-x[1]}{\sqrt2}\):

\[T=\frac{\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)}{\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)+\sigma^2}y^2[0]+\frac{\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)}{\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)+\sigma^2}y^2[1].\]

ρ = 0 gives the energy detector. As ρ → 1 the second weight vanishes and \(T\propto(x[0]+x[1])^2\).

Bayesian detectors for binomial and exponential data (Lec 5)

Binomial (n = 10, 0.5 vs 0.8, equal priors): threshold \(\frac{10\ln2.5}{\ln4}=6.61\), so say yes for x ≥ 7. \(P_e=\tfrac12(0.172+0.121)\approx0.146\).

Exponential (N = 1): \(x\gtrless\gamma'=\frac{\ln\gamma+\ln(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)}{\lambda_0-\lambda_1}\), with \(P_e=P(H_0)e^{-\lambda_0\gamma'}+P(H_1)(1-e^{-\lambda_1\gamma'})\).

From Kay

Kay 3.4: \(P_{FA}=10^{-4}\), \(P_D=0.99\), SNR −30 dB. How many samples?

\(\sqrt{d^2}=3.719+2.326=6.045\), so d² = 36.5 and N = 36.5 / 10⁻³ ≈ 36,550. Weak signals need huge N because d² grows only linearly in N.

Kay 3.6: DC level with A < 0

The test flips to \(\bar x<\gamma'\) with \(\gamma'=-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt N}Q^{-1}(P_{FA})\), and \(P_D=Q\big(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-\sqrt{NA^2/\sigma^2}\big)\), the same as for A > 0. The performance is the same but the test is different, which is why there's no UMP test for A ≠ 0.

Kay 3.7: Rayleigh samples, \(\sigma_0^2\) vs \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\)

\(T=\sum x^2[n]\). Each \(x^2\) is exponential with mean 2σ², so \(T/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{2N}\) and \(P_D=Q_{\chi^2_{2N}}\big(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_{2N}}(P_{FA})\big)\).

Kay 3.12: perfect detector, \(U[-c,c]\) vs \(U[1-c,1+c]\)

The supports don't overlap when c ≤ ½. Say yes if x > ½: \(P_{FA}=0\), \(P_D=1\).

Kay 3.17: equal priors, \(\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\) vs \(\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\sigma^2I)\) in 2-D

Say yes if \(\boldsymbol\mu^T\mathbf x>\|\boldsymbol\mu\|^2/2\): the boundary is the perpendicular bisector of 0 and μ. \(P_e=Q(\|\boldsymbol\mu\|/2\sigma)\).

Kay 3.18: MAP for \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) vs \(\mathcal N(0,2)\)

Say yes if \(x^2>4\ln\frac{P(H_0)}{P(H_1)}+2\ln2\). Equal priors: |x| > 1.18. P(H₀) = ¾: |x| > 2.40. Large |x| points to the wider PDF, and a bigger prior on H₀ pushes the tails out.

Q1: short answers (8 marks)

1(i) 1 mark: \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf m,C)\), \(\mathbf w\ne\mathbf 0\). Distribution of \(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x\)?

\(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf w^T\mathbf m,\ \mathbf w^TC\mathbf w)\), a scalar Gaussian. A linear map of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian, with mean \(E[\mathbf w^T\mathbf x]=\mathbf w^T\mathbf m\) and variance \(E[\mathbf w^T(\mathbf x-\mathbf m)(\mathbf x-\mathbf m)^T\mathbf w]=\mathbf w^TC\mathbf w\).

1(ii) 1 mark: Two known signals \(\mathbf s_0,\mathbf s_1\), equal priors, equal energies, iid WGN. Optimal detector?

The minimum-distance receiver, which here is the same as picking the bigger correlation: decide \(H_1\) if \(\mathbf x^T\mathbf s_1>\mathbf x^T\mathbf s_0\), i.e. \(T=\mathbf x^T(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0)\gtrless0\). The general threshold \(\sigma^2\ln\gamma+(\mathcal E_1-\mathcal E_0)/2\) is 0 because γ = 1 and the energies are equal. \(P_e=Q\big(\|\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\|/2\sigma\big)\).

1(iii) 1 mark: When is part of the data irrelevant?

Split the data as \((\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2)\). \(\mathbf x_2\) is irrelevant if \(p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_1)=p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_0)\). Then \(L(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2)=L(\mathbf x_1)\), so the LRT is the same with or without \(\mathbf x_2\). It's the conditional PDF given \(\mathbf x_1\) that has to match. \(\mathbf x_2\) having the same marginal under both hypotheses isn't enough (a correlated noise reference helps even though it has no signal in it).

1(iv) 1 mark: Statement of the Neyman–Fisher factorisation theorem.

If the PDF \(p(\mathbf x;\theta)\) can be factored as

\[p(\mathbf x;\theta)=g\big(T(\mathbf x),\theta\big)\,h(\mathbf x),\]

where g depends on the data only through \(T(\mathbf x)\) and h doesn't depend on θ, then \(T(\mathbf x)\) is a sufficient statistic for θ. Conversely, if T is sufficient then such a factorisation exists.

1(v) 1.5 marks: What is ROC? Full form and definition. Slope of the ROC?

Receiver Operating Characteristic. It's the curve of \(P_D\) against \(P_{FA}\) that you trace out by sweeping the threshold γ of the LRT (NP detector) from ∞ down to 0. It goes from (0, 0) to (1, 1), sits above the diagonal and is concave.

Slope: \(\dfrac{dP_D}{dP_{FA}}=\gamma\), the LRT threshold that gives that point.

1(vi) 2.5 marks: Precise statement of the Neyman–Pearson lemma for two simple hypotheses.

Test \(H_0:\mathbf x\sim p_0\) against \(H_1:\mathbf x\sim p_1\) with a (possibly randomised) rule δ(x) = probability of deciding H₁, and let \(L(\mathbf x)=p_1(\mathbf x)/p_0(\mathbf x)\).

  1. Optimality. Any rule of the form \[\delta(\mathbf x)=\begin{cases}1&L(\mathbf x)>\gamma\\ c_0&L(\mathbf x)=\gamma\\ 0&L(\mathbf x)<\gamma\end{cases}\] with \(E_0[\delta]=P_{FA}=\alpha\) has the largest \(P_D\) among all rules with \(P_{FA}\le\alpha\).
  2. Existence. For every α ∈ (0, 1) there are γ ≥ 0 and \(c_0\in[0,1]\) that make \(P_{FA}=\alpha\) exactly: \(c_0=\frac{\alpha-\Pr_0\{L>\gamma\}}{\Pr_0\{L=\gamma\}}\). For continuous data \(c_0\) isn't needed.
  3. Uniqueness. Every most powerful size-α rule has this form, except possibly on a set of zero probability under both hypotheses.

In words: to maximise \(P_D\) subject to \(P_{FA}=\alpha\), say H₁ when \(L(\mathbf x)>\gamma\), with γ fixed by \(P_{FA}=\int_{L>\gamma}p_0=\alpha\).

Q2: long answers (42 marks)

2(i) 6 pts: \(H_0:X\sim p_0\), \(H_1:X\sim p_1\), priors \(\pi_0,\pi_1\). Derive the detector that minimises the Bayes risk.

Short answer: decide H₁ if \(\dfrac{p_1(x)}{p_0(x)}>\dfrac{\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})}{\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})}\). With 0/1 costs this is MAP, threshold \(\pi_0/\pi_1\).

\(C_{ij}\) = cost of deciding \(H_i\) when \(H_j\) is true. A deterministic rule splits the data space into \(R_1\) (decide H₁) and \(R_0\) (its complement). The Bayes risk is the average cost:

\[\mathcal R=\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}\,\pi_j\Pr\{\text{decide }H_i|H_j\}=\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}\,\pi_j\int_{R_i}p_j(x)\,dx.\]

Use \(\int_{R_0}p_j=1-\int_{R_1}p_j\) to write everything over \(R_1\):

\[\mathcal R=C_{00}\pi_0+C_{01}\pi_1+\int_{R_1}\Big[\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})p_0(x)-\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})p_1(x)\Big]dx.\]

The first two terms don't depend on the rule. The integral is smallest if \(R_1\) contains exactly the points where the integrand is negative:

\[x\in R_1\iff\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})p_1(x)>\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})p_0(x).\]

Assuming errors cost more than correct decisions (\(C_{10}>C_{00}\), \(C_{01}>C_{11}\)), divide through to get the LRT

\[L(x)=\frac{p_1(x)}{p_0(x)}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma=\frac{\pi_0(C_{10}-C_{00})}{\pi_1(C_{01}-C_{11})}.\]

Points where the integrand is exactly 0 can go either way without changing \(\mathcal R\). With \(C_{00}=C_{11}=0\), \(C_{10}=C_{01}=1\), the risk is \(P_e\) and γ = \(\pi_0/\pi_1\): decide H₁ if \(\pi_1p_1(x)>\pi_0p_0(x)\), i.e. pick the larger posterior (MAP). Equal priors as well give ML.

2(ii) 7 pts: \(x[n]\) iid, \(H_0:\mathcal N(0,\sigma_0^2)\) vs \(H_1:\mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2)\), \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\). (a) NP detector (b) \(P_D\) in terms of \(P_{FA}\) (c) simplify for N = 2.

Short answer: \(T=\sum x^2[n]>\gamma'\); \(P_D=Q_{\chi^2_N}\big(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(P_{FA})\big)\); for N = 2, \(x^2[0]+x^2[1]>2\sigma_0^2\ln\frac1{P_{FA}}\) and \(P_D=P_{FA}^{\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2}\).

(a) The likelihood ratio is

\[L(\mathbf x)=\frac{(2\pi\sigma_1^2)^{-N/2}\exp\!\big(-\frac1{2\sigma_1^2}\sum x^2[n]\big)}{(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-N/2}\exp\!\big(-\frac1{2\sigma_0^2}\sum x^2[n]\big)},\] \[\ln L=-\frac N2\ln\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_0^2}+\frac12\Big(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}\Big)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n]>\ln\gamma.\]

Since \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\), the coefficient \(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}\) is positive, so dividing by it keeps the direction:

\[T(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n]\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma'=\frac{2\ln\gamma+N\ln(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_0^2)}{1/\sigma_0^2-1/\sigma_1^2}.\]

This is the energy detector, with γ′ set by \(P_{FA}\) in (b).

(b) Under \(H_i\), \(x[n]/\sigma_i\) are iid \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), so \(T/\sigma_i^2\sim\chi^2_N\). Writing \(Q_{\chi^2_N}(y)=\Pr\{\chi^2_N>y\}\),

\[P_{FA}=\Pr\Big\{\frac T{\sigma_0^2}>\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_0^2}\Big\}=Q_{\chi^2_N}\Big(\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_0^2}\Big)\ \Rightarrow\ \gamma'=\sigma_0^2\,Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(P_{FA}),\] \[P_D=Q_{\chi^2_N}\Big(\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_1^2}\Big)=Q_{\chi^2_N}\Big(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}\,Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(P_{FA})\Big).\]

(c) For N = 2, χ²₂ is exponential with mean 2, so \(Q_{\chi^2_2}(y)=e^{-y/2}\). Then

\[P_{FA}=e^{-\gamma'/2\sigma_0^2}\ \Rightarrow\ \gamma'=2\sigma_0^2\ln\frac1{P_{FA}},\qquad P_D=e^{-\gamma'/2\sigma_1^2}=P_{FA}^{\ \sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2}.\]

The detector is \(x^2[0]+x^2[1]>2\sigma_0^2\ln(1/P_{FA})\): say H₁ when the point \((x[0],x[1])\) falls outside a circle of radius \(\sqrt{2\sigma_0^2\ln(1/P_{FA})}\). Since \(\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2<1\), \(P_D>P_{FA}\), as it should be.

2(iii) 8 marks: SPRT on iid \(X_i\), \(p_0\) vs \(p_1\), finite thresholds \(\tau_0,\tau_1\), stopping time N. Can N = ∞? Prove it or give a counterexample.

Short answer: no, as long as \(p_0\ne p_1\). N is finite with probability 1 (and even \(E\{N\}<\infty\)). The only exception is the degenerate case \(p_0=p_1\), where the test never stops.

Set-up. Let \(z_i=\ln\frac{p_1(X_i)}{p_0(X_i)}\) and \(S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nz_i\). The test continues while \(\tau_0

Claim. If \(p_0\ne p_1\) then \(\Pr\{z_i\ne0\}>0\), so for some ε > 0 either \(\Pr\{z_i>\varepsilon\}=q>0\) or \(\Pr\{z_i<-\varepsilon\}=q>0\). Take the first case (the second is the same with the sign flipped).

Proof. Let \(k=\lceil D/\varepsilon\rceil+1\). If k steps in a row all have \(z_i>\varepsilon\), the walk moves up by more than D, so it must leave the band during that block, wherever it started inside it. Split time into blocks of length k: \(\{1,\dots,k\},\{k+1,\dots,2k\},\dots\) Each block is "all big steps" with probability \(\delta=q^k>0\), independently of the other blocks. To still be running after m blocks, none of them can have been all big steps:

\[\Pr\{N>mk\}\le(1-\delta)^m\ \xrightarrow{m\to\infty}\ 0.\]

So \(\Pr\{N=\infty\}=\lim_m\Pr\{N>mk\}=0\). The tail is geometric, so \(E\{N\}=\sum_n\Pr\{N>n\}\le k\sum_m(1-\delta)^m=k/\delta<\infty\). This holds under H₀ and H₁ alike.

Counterexample when the condition fails. If \(p_0=p_1\), every \(z_i=0\), so \(S_n=0\) for all n. Since \(\tau_0<0<\tau_1\), the walk never leaves the band and N = ∞ with probability 1.

Remark. Even with \(p_0\ne p_1\), single sample paths that never stop can exist. For instance, with Bernoulli data and steps \(+a,-a\), the path +, −, +, −, … stays inside forever. That set of paths has probability zero, so "N < ∞ almost surely" is the precise statement. The thresholds have to be finite for this: with \(\tau_1=\infty\), the test could stop under H₀, where the walk drifts down, but under H₁ it might never stop.

2(iv) 7 pts: Design the optimal NP detector for \(H_0: X\sim[0.2\ \ 0.3\ \ 0.5]\), \(H_1: X\sim[0.6\ \ 0.1\ \ 0.3]\), with \(P_{FA}=0.05\).

Short answer: if X = x₁, say H₁ with probability 0.25; otherwise say H₀. \(P_D=0.15\).

X takes three values x₁, x₂, x₃. Compute the likelihood ratio at each one and rank them, because NP fills the "yes" region from the largest ratio down:

x\(p_0\)\(p_1\)\(L=p_1/p_0\)rank
x₁0.20.631
x₂0.30.11/33
x₃0.50.30.62

The ratio isn't monotone in the label, so the order is x₁, x₃, x₂. The possible non-random tests give \(P_{FA}\in\{0,\ 0.2,\ 0.7,\ 1\}\). Even putting only x₁ in the "yes" region gives \(P_{FA}=0.2>0.05\), so the threshold is γ = 3 (sitting on x₁) and we randomise there:

\[\Pr\{L>3;H_0\}=0,\qquad c_0=\frac{0.05-0}{\Pr\{X=x_1;H_0\}}=\frac{0.05}{0.2}=0.25.\] \[\delta(x)=\begin{cases}H_1\text{ w.p. }0.25,\ H_0\text{ w.p. }0.75 & x=x_1\ (L=\gamma=3)\ H_0 & x\in\{x_2,x_3\}\ (L<3)\end{cases}\]

\(P_{FA}=0.25(0.2)=0.05\) and \(P_D=0.25(0.6)=0.15\). The ROC is the polygon through (0, 0), (0.2, 0.6), (0.7, 0.9), (1, 1), and this rule sits a quarter of the way along its first segment.

2(v) 8 pts: \(H_0:\mathbf x=\mathbf w\) vs \(H_1:\mathbf x=\mathbf s+\mathbf w\), \(\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\), \(\mathbf s\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,C_s)\), \(C_s=\sigma_s^2\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(|\rho|\le1\). Find \(T(\mathbf x)\), \(\hat{\mathbf s}\) and the canonical estimator-correlator.

Short answer: with \(y_0=\frac{x[0]+x[1]}{\sqrt2}\), \(y_1=\frac{x[0]-x[1]}{\sqrt2}\), \(\lambda_{0,1}=\sigma_s^2(1\pm\rho)\):

\[T(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^T\hat{\mathbf s}=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_0+\sigma^2}y_0^2+\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\sigma^2}y_1^2\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma'.\]

This is the Lec 9 homework in section 18 with more of the working shown.

1. The LRT. \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\) under H₀ and \(\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,C_s+\sigma^2I)\) under H₁. Dropping constants,

\[\ln L(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^T\Big[\tfrac1{\sigma^2}I-(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\Big]\mathbf x=\frac1{2\sigma^2}\mathbf x^TC_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf x,\]

using the matrix inversion lemma. Absorbing \(1/2\sigma^2\) into the threshold gives \(T=\mathbf x^T\hat{\mathbf s}\).

2. The signal estimate (Wiener / MMSE). \(\hat{\mathbf s}=C_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf x\). For this 2×2 case, with \(D=\det(C_s+\sigma^2I)=(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)^2-\rho^2\sigma_s^4=(\lambda_0+\sigma^2)(\lambda_1+\sigma^2)\),

\[\hat{\mathbf s}=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{D}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_s^2(1-\rho^2)+\sigma^2&\rho\sigma^2\ \rho\sigma^2&\sigma_s^2(1-\rho^2)+\sigma^2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x.\]

Check: ρ = 0 gives \(\hat{\mathbf s}=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}\mathbf x\), the scalar Wiener gain.

3. Canonical form. Diagonalise \(C_s=V\Lambda_sV^T\):

\[V=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \Lambda_s=\mathrm{diag}\big(\sigma_s^2(1+\rho),\ \sigma_s^2(1-\rho)\big).\]

V also diagonalises \(C_s+\sigma^2I\), so \(\hat{\mathbf s}=V\,\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\sigma^2}\big)V^T\mathbf x\). With \(\mathbf y=V^T\mathbf x\) the statistic is the weighted sum in the short answer. The structure: rotate (sum and difference channels), square, weight each channel by its Wiener gain \(\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\sigma^2)\), add.

4. Distributions. \(y_0,y_1\) are independent, \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) under H₀ and \(\mathcal N(0,\lambda_i+\sigma^2)\) under H₁. T is a weighted sum of two χ²₁ variables, so γ′ for a given \(P_{FA}\) is found numerically in general.

5. Special cases.

  • ρ = 0: equal weights \(w=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}\), \(T\propto x^2[0]+x^2[1]\), the energy detector. \(T/(w\sigma^2)\sim\chi^2_2\) under H₀, so \(P_{FA}=e^{-\gamma'/2w\sigma^2}\).
  • ρ → 1: \(\lambda_1=0\), only the sum channel is kept, \(T\propto(x[0]+x[1])^2\). The signal is the same in both samples, so average them.
  • ρ → −1: only the difference channel, \(T\propto(x[0]-x[1])^2\).
2(vi) 6 pts: With \(h[k]=s[N-1-k]\) for \(k=0,\dots,N-1\) (0 otherwise) and \(y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}h[k]x[n-k]\), show the output for \(x[n]=s[n]\) is maximised at \(n=N-1\) with maximum value \(\mathcal E=\sum s^2[n]\).

Short answer: the output is the signal's autocorrelation, \(y[n]=r_{ss}[N-1-n]\), and an autocorrelation peaks at lag 0 with value \(\mathcal E\).

Substitute x = s and h:

\[y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}s[N-1-k]\,s[n-k].\]

Let m = N − 1 − k (so k = N − 1 − m, and m also runs over 0, …, N − 1):

\[y[n]=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}s[m]\,s[m+n-(N-1)]=r_{ss}[\,n-(N-1)\,],\qquad r_{ss}[l]=\sum_m s[m]\,s[m+l],\]

with s = 0 outside 0, …, N − 1. (\(r_{ss}\) is even, so this equals \(r_{ss}[N-1-n]\).)

Bound. By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[|r_{ss}[l]|\le\sqrt{\sum_m s^2[m]}\sqrt{\sum_m s^2[m+l]}\le\sqrt{\mathcal E}\sqrt{\mathcal E}=\mathcal E,\]

since a shifted copy can't have more energy than the whole signal.

Attained at n = N − 1. There l = 0 and

\[y[N-1]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}s[N-1-k]\,s[N-1-k]=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}s^2[m]=\mathcal E.\]

So \(y[n]\le\mathcal E=y[N-1]\) for every n. For a general input the same substitution gives \(y[N-1]=\sum_n x[n]s[n]\), which is why the two structures (correlator, and matched filter sampled at N − 1) are equivalent.
