    The exam, how it is marked, and how to use this page

    The exam

    The paper is worth 35 marks and you have 3 hours. It has two parts. Part 1 is compulsory. Part 2 has two questions and you answer one of them. This comes from Prof. Rajorshi Ray's announcement of 25 November. The portion is Module 6 onwards, which is the fifteen readings on this page.

    How answers are marked

    The midsem answer key tells you what the examiners reward. Almost every question asked for a definition and then a list from one reading, such as "the three technological changes" or "Giddens's four characteristics of class". The key gave 2 or 3 marks for the definition and then one mark for each item on the list, and it listed the exact words it was looking for. The passage questions asked you to apply a theory to examples from a passage, and the last mark went to the point one step past the obvious one.

    So for each reading you need three things. You need its main idea in two lines. You need every list in it, with the name of each item and one example. And you need to be able to say which author said it.

    How to use each section

    1. In one line. Read the thesis first. If a question names the reading, this is your opening sentence.
    2. The argument. A few numbered claims, each with the example you would use in an answer.
    3. Lists a question can ask for. Learn these by heart. Words with a yellow background are the ones an examiner looks for.
    4. Remember. Names, numbers and criticisms worth quoting.
    5. Practice. Write your answer on paper before opening the model answer. Each answer shows where the marks go.

    There is no endsem past paper, so every practice question here is written in the style of the midsem paper. They are guesses at what could be asked, not leaks.

    Reading the whole page carefully takes about 6 hours. If you have less time, do the sections marked first before the others. The RTI paper is marked first because Prof. Ray is one of its authors. The others marked first have the most lists, which are the easiest marks to get.

    The Eye of the Master

    Module 6 · Matteo Pasquinelli, The Eye of the Master: A Social History of Artificial Intelligence (Verso, 2023), the introduction and chapter 2, with the class slides "The Social History of AI"

    Pasquinelli argues that "the inner code of AI is constituted not by the imitation of biological intelligence but by the intelligence of labour and social relations." Machines are built by copying how work is already divided among people.

    New idea · Division of labour, automation and algorithm

    The division of labour means splitting one job into many small tasks, each done by a different worker, as in Adam Smith's pin factory. Automation means a machine doing a task that people used to do. An algorithm is a fixed list of step-by-step instructions that turns an input into an output.

    New idea · Marx on machinery

    Marx argued that in the factory the machine takes in the skill that used to belong to the worker. The book opens with his line that each operator's skill "vanishes as an infinitesimal quantity" next to "the science, the gigantic natural forces, and the mass of the social labour embodied in the system of machinery". Together these make up the power of the "master", so the master is the machine system, not one boss.

    New idea · Foucault's discipline

    The French philosopher Michel Foucault studied discipline, the training of bodies through timetables, fixed places and constant watching. Pasquinelli cites his point that such techniques, "based on the segmentation of time, space, and relations", prepared the ground for capitalist exploitation.

    The argument

    1. AI copies the intelligence of labour, not the brain. Self-driving cars show how much skill truck drivers use, including their grasp of local cultural codes, the unwritten rules of a place, because, in his words, "all labour is logic".
    2. Computation began as a division of mental labour. The French engineer Gaspard de Prony organised the hand calculation of logarithm tables as a social algorithm, a hierarchy of clerks each doing one part of a long calculation. The English mathematician Charles Babbage designed his Difference Engine to replace the lowest class. His aim was a business one, to mechanise the mental labour of clerks and sell error-free tables.
    3. Babbage's two principles of labour analysis (see the list below) tie machines to the division of labour. Put together, computation is both the automation of mental labour and the calculus of its cost, the exact working out of what each piece of that labour costs.
    4. The eye of the master is the gaze that supervises and disciplines the division of labour. It passed from the factory master to Marx's system of machinery, then to statistics, and now to the digital factory, a society whose labour is managed through search engines, apps, gig platforms and AI.
    5. The British computer pioneer Alan Turing in 1947 divided the people around his computer, the ACE, into masters who plan its work and servants who feed it cards, and expected the machine to replace both. In fact AI has mostly replaced masters, that is managers. The ghost workers remain. They are hidden workers, many in the Global South (the poorer countries of Asia, Africa and Latin America), who produce the data and do the filtering and checking.
    6. The usual histories hide this labour. Myths of autonomous machines have been used since the industrial age to mystify the role of workers, that is, to hide how much machines depend on them, and technical histories told as a straight line of mathematical progress reinforce technological determinism, the belief that technology develops by its own logic and drives society. Pasquinelli writes a sociotechnical history instead, told from the side of workers and their collective knowledge.
    Lists a question can ask for

    Babbage's principles of labour analysis2

    1. The labour theory of the machine. A new machine comes to imitate and replace a previous division of labour. The Difference Engine replaced the lowest class of de Prony's human computers.
    2. The principle of labour calculation, or Babbage principle. Dividing production into small tasks lets the employer calculate and buy the precise quantity of labour each task needs. Not paying a skilled needle worker eight or ten shillings a day to turn a wheel that a sixpence-a-day worker could turn.

    Pasquinelli generalises the first into his labour theory of automation, the claim that machines, AI included, are made by imitating the division of labour and the know-how of workers.

    De Prony's social algorithm3 classes

    1. Mathematicians, who formulated the problem.
    2. Algebraists, who turned it into simple operations and data.
    3. Human computers, who did the adding and subtracting. Students, often women, and sometimes unemployed hairdressers.

    Features of the Difference Engine3

    1. Hardware and software at once, since its gears embodied a single algorithm.
    2. It used de Prony's method of differences, which replaces multiplication with addition and subtraction.
    3. Not programmable, with no separate input for information. The Jacquard loom, with its punched cards, had one.

    Ada Lovelace's contributions4

    1. She helped design the Analytical Engine, Babbage's later programmable design, and wrote the first documented machine program.
    2. She separated the Engine's logical structure from its mechanical one, in today's terms software from hardware.
    3. Her science of operations covered the handling of any entity, not only numbers.
    4. The first dismissal of "AI". "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything."

    The two lineages of AI2

    1. Symbolic AI, from the 1956 Dartmouth workshop where John McCarthy coined "artificial intelligence". Intelligence as a representation of the world (knowing-that), run by deductive logic.
    2. Connectionism, artificial neural networks that learn from examples, from Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1957), an early network of this kind. Intelligence as experience of the world (knowing-how), run by inductive logic.
    Remember
    • Babbage's engines were also tools to measure, discipline and watch labour.
    • AI imposes hierarchies of manual and mental labour. The American psychologist Lewis Terman, a promoter of IQ tests, wrote in 1919 that an IQ of 75 or below "belongs ordinarily in the unskilled labor class".

    Marx, Taylorism, Braverman and the Capitalist Labour Process

    Module 6 · Keith Grint, The Sociology of Work, from the chapter "Class and Industrial Conflict" (pp. 175 to 188)

    Braverman argued that capitalist management, following Taylor, strips workers of skill to control them, so all work is steadily deskilled. Grint replies that Taylorism was rarely used in full, that skill is socially constructed, and that "Class control over the labour process is not the sine qua non of capitalism, profit is." Sine qua non is Latin for an essential condition.

    New idea · Capitalism and profit

    Capitalism is an economic system in which private owners of firms hire workers for a wage and sell what the workers make. Profit is what is left from the sales after wages and other costs are paid.

    New idea · Labour power, labour and surplus value

    Marx separated labour power, the capacity to work that the employer buys for a wage, from labour, the work actually done in that time. Surplus value is the value the labour produces beyond the wage, which the capitalist keeps as profit. So the employer gains by getting as much labour as possible out of the labour power already paid for.

    New idea · The labour process and deskilling

    The labour process is, in Grint's words, "the place where commodities were constructed and developed by mixing human labour with raw materials". Deskilling means taking the knowledge and judgement out of a job so that cheaper workers who follow instructions can do it.

    The argument

    1. Harry Braverman's Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974) brought Marxist attention back to the labour process. He rejected the claim that changes in the mix of occupations mean workers are more skilled. He compared the actual content of jobs with the nineteenth-century craft worker, who had wide knowledge and high autonomy and control.
    2. For the capitalist, "the problem of work is the problem of managerial control", getting the most effort for the least reward. Braverman, following Taylor, says the answer is to extend the division of labour (see 01), separate conception from execution, and deskill the workforce. This gives higher productivity, cheaper labour and more compliant labour.
    3. F.W. Taylor's scientific management was a system of breaking each task into its smallest units, timed and measured, and paying individual piece rates. With the pig-iron shoveller Schmidt he raised output by 400 per cent for a 40 per cent wage rise, at which Grint remarks "Homo economicus had arrived", mocking the model of a person who works only for money.
    4. Taylorism had limited influence in practice. A strike broke out within weeks of its first major use, at the state-owned Watertown arsenal (a US weapons factory), and Taylorist methods were banned from government-funded work from 1916 to 1949. Daito, writing about Japanese firms, found "we have not been able to identify any firm which adopted the Taylor system in its complete form".
    5. Braverman still built his theory on Taylor's ideas and concluded that deskilling is universal. Machinery lets management control work mechanically, office and retail work are broken down and timed in the same way, and clerical workers are proletarianized, pushed down to the level of manual wage workers. This would confirm Marx's forecast of a single, uniform working class.
    6. Grint's conclusion is that profit, not control, drives employers. Control costs money, so a firm that supervised the opening of every office drawer would need almost as many supervisors as clerks. Whether labour is deskilled, enskilled (given more skill), given autonomy or removed depends on what makes a profit.
    Lists a question can ask for

    Taylor's reasons for low output3

    1. Management lacked the knowledge to maximise production, so it depended on the workers' know-how.
    2. Workers had a reason to restrict output, the real fear of lower pay or redundancy.
    3. The payment system lacked a good enough incentive scheme.

    Features of scientific management4

    1. Fragmenting tasks into their smallest units, each timed and measured.
    2. Individual piece rates, set per worker and not per group, to avoid encouraging unions.
    3. Deskilling, to allow cheaper labour and end the workers' monopoly over knowledge.
    4. Technical improvements to the machinery.

    Kinds of soldiering2

    Soldiering was Taylor's word for workers restricting output.

    1. Natural soldiering, from the laziness he thought was part of human nature, which incentives could contain but not remove.
    2. Systematic soldiering, kept up by workers acting together, and caused by poor supervision and bad incentive systems.

    Braverman's three principles of Taylorism3

    1. "The dissociation of the labor process from the skills of the workers". The work no longer depends on what the worker knows.
    2. "The separation of conception from execution". Management plans and workers carry out.
    3. Management's "use of this monopoly over knowledge to control each step of the labor process and its mode of execution".

    Grint's criticisms of Braverman6

    1. A romantic picture of the craft worker. Most people never worked in a skilled craft tradition.
    2. Skill is socially constructed. Groups negotiate the label "skilled". Clerical work lost its skilled status as literacy spread, while printers kept theirs.
    3. Worker control is not class action. It may serve one group of workers at the expense of other, less organised workers.
    4. No uniform proletariat. The working class has grown more divided. In one survey 96 per cent said the skill in their job had stayed the same or increased.
    5. Unreliable data and "one best way". Braverman assumes Taylorism was adopted widely and without resistance, sharing Taylor's belief in "one best way" to organise production. Friedman, another Marxist, shows managers also use responsible autonomy, giving workers leeway, as well as direct control.
    6. Rules on paper are not rules in practice. Clerical time standards for opening drawers are closer to Gouldner's mock bureaucracy, rules that are routinely ignored.
    Remember
    • Braverman, Labor and Monopoly Capital, 1974. Schmidt, 400 per cent more output for 40 per cent more pay.
    • Braverman's quote, also Grint's essay question. "Machinery offers to management the opportunity to do by wholly mechanical means, that which it had previously attempted to do by organizational and disciplinary means."

    India's Socially Regulated Economy

    Module 7 · Barbara Harriss-White (2005), "India's Socially Regulated Economy", a summary of her book India Working (2003)

    Most of the Indian economy is regulated by social institutions rather than by the state. Gender, religion, caste, space, class and the state act as social structures of accumulation, and liberalisation makes them rework themselves as economic institutions instead of dissolving them.

    New idea · Accumulation of capital and rent

    Accumulation of capital means reinvesting profit so that a business keeps growing (see 02 for capitalism). A rent is income from controlling something scarce, such as a licence or market access, rather than from producing more.

    New idea · The informal economy

    The informal economy is activity that either lies outside state regulation, such as unregistered and unorganised firms, or is officially regulated but does not follow the rules. An example from the reading is large firms keeping casual workers undeclared under the Factories Act.

    New idea · Liberalisation

    Liberalisation is reducing state control of the economy, by cutting licences, subsidies and trade barriers, so that markets set prices. The package that the World Bank and the IMF asked of borrowing countries is called structural adjustment. Its supporters predicted that contract would replace custom and that inborn "primordial identities" such as caste would stop mattering.

    The argument

    1. Most of India's economy is informal. About 88 per cent of Indians live and work in settlements of under 200,000 people, and the informal economy is 60 per cent of net domestic product. There is no sign that it is shrinking.
    2. This economy is regulated by social institutions, which she calls social structures of accumulation, the non-market institutions through which capital is accumulated and the economy is regulated. Policy is carried out "through their filters", so a neutral-looking policy lands differently on different castes, religions and genders.
    3. Myrdal, and later the liberalisers of the 1990s, expected these institutions to be banished by modern business, planning or markets. Harriss-White says this has not happened. The state itself is shaped by caste, religion and gender, so it does not work the way a modern state that steers development is expected to.
    4. Outside the big cities, capital is held by the intermediate classes. They live off controlling scarcity and rents, their biggest hidden subsidy is tax evasion, and they influence policy when it is implemented, not when it is made.
    5. She calls the real unorthodox structural adjustment the under-funding of the state as taxable wealth leaks into the black economy, income hidden from tax. Her image is a fire brigade "with firemen but without water or diesel fuel".
    6. So liberalisation does not dissolve social regulation. Caste and religion take on economic jobs and persist, which she calls re-working themselves as economic institutions. Caste-cum-trade associations (in the list below) are the clearest case. The result is wider regional and social inequality. She calls the result a socially regulated but "fundamentally anti-social" economy.
    Lists a question can ask for

    The social structures of accumulation6

    1. Gender. The basic unit is the family firm, a business run as a hierarchy among male kin, and elite women are kept out of productive work. The juvenile sex ratio (girls per 1,000 boys) was 784 in the business elite against 930 in landless agricultural households.
    2. Religious plurality. Religions supply credit, contacts, skills, reputation and insurance. Jains are 0.4 per cent of the population but are commonly wealthy merchants and moneylenders.
    3. Caste. Caste decides who does which work and who trades with whom. "Caste is the strongest trades union." Nearly half the firms in her town were caste-homogeneous.
    4. Space. Accumulation happens in specialised local clusters, and capital does not move freely between castes and places. Tirupur makes hosiery and Sivakasi makes matches.
    5. Class. Labour is mostly casual or self-employed with no rights, and capital is held by the intermediate classes. Only about 3 per cent of the workforce is unionised.
    6. The state. It is shaped by these identities and surrounded by a shadow state, "a penumbra of people living from intermediation and corruption".

    The intermediate classes3 parts

    Michal Kalecki's term for a loose coalition whose income is neither a wage nor a profit but "an amalgam of the two".

    1. Rich peasants, farmers who employ labour.
    2. Working commercial capitalists, the family businesses. Five sweet shops owned by a father and his four sons fix prices and wages together.
    3. The collusive fraction of the bureaucracy, the local officials who implement state regulations and work hand in hand with local business.

    How the intermediate classes survive liberalisation3 means

    1. Investing money hidden in the black economy productively.
    2. State control of key markets has hardly been reformed. She calls these markets the "prominent heights", a twist on "commanding heights", the usual phrase for an economy's key sectors. This regulation brings subsidies and bribes, and it protects the rents the intermediate classes earn and their tax evasion. Food, electricity, kerosene, fertiliser and liquor.
    3. A new wave of accumulation by the lower agrarian castes.

    What caste-cum-trade associations do11

    A caste-cum-trade association is a trade body built on a caste, "by far the most significant" structure in the local economy. It limits who can enter the trade, defines proper contracts, settles disputes, provides collective insurance, speaks for the trade to the state, organises the marketplace, watches over rents and shares them with officials, controls labour disputes, fixes wages, fixes prices in related markets such as porters' charges, and provides collective security.

    Three contradictions facing reforms that depend on formal law3

    1. Development needs the rule of law, but much of the economy is not regulated by law and powerful people act as if they are above it.
    2. Scrapping laws that do not fit means giving in to the people already breaking them, which weakens the state's authority.
    3. Scrapping laws that cannot be enforced leaves looser laws that are easy to abuse.
    Remember
    • 88 per cent of Indians live in settlements under 200,000. The informal economy is 60 per cent of net domestic product, 68 per cent of income and 60 per cent of savings.
    • Of a labour force over 390 million, only 7 per cent are on regular wages, and only half of those are unionised.
    • Gender is the most resistant to change. Caste and religion are much more flexible and are being reworked.

    The Active Construction of Passive Investors

    Module 7 · Adam S. Hayes (2019), "The active construction of passive investors: roboadvisors and algorithmic 'low-finance'", Socio-Economic Review

    A roboadvisor is a digital platform that manages a client's money by algorithm (see 01), with little human involvement and at little cost. Hayes argues that roboadvisors are actively constructing passive investors, using screen design and targeted messages to stop users from interfering with the model, a principle he calls "don't do".

    New idea · Ethnography

    Ethnography is a research method in which the researcher spends a long time inside a setting, taking part, watching, talking to people and collecting their documents. The aim is to understand from the inside how people there make sense of what they do. Ethnographers check one source against another, which is called triangulation.

    New idea · Index funds, ETFs and passive investing

    An index fund buys every share in a stock market index, such as the S&P 500 (500 large US companies), so that its returns copy the index. An ETF (exchange traded fund) is a cheap fund of this kind that is itself traded on a stock exchange like a share. Passive investing means holding such funds for years and accepting the market's return, while active investing, at its extreme high-frequency trading (HFT), tries to beat the market by frequent trades.

    New idea · Foucault's governmentality and technologies of power and of the self

    Governmentality is Foucault's term for power that works by shaping how people conduct themselves, through expert knowledge and everyday techniques, without force. Technologies of the self "permit individuals to effect by their own means" a form of self-control. Technologies of power "determine the conduct of individuals and submit them to certain ends", which Foucault calls "an objectivizing of the subject", treating the person as an object to be steered.

    The argument

    1. Roboadvisors carry algorithmic trading from Wall Street into low-finance, Hayes's term for services aimed at lay investors with modest income and financial literacy. The US firm Betterment launched the first in 2010.
    2. The algorithms code in Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), Harry Markowitz's 1952 model for dividing money among assets to get the highest expected return for a chosen level of risk. Portfolios are built from passive ETFs. The evidence favours this, since only about 8 per cent of active funds beat the index from 2002 to 2017.
    3. Users are made doubly passive. Their portfolio is passive, and automation keeps them away from decisions about it. Rebalancing and reinvesting dividends happen without the user.
    4. The firms use behavioural economics, the study of the predictable ways people depart from rational choice, to discipline users. Their tools include nudges, changes in how a choice is presented that steer people without forbidding anything. Professional traders train themselves in the rule "don't think", keeping emotion out of trading, while roboadvisor users are held to "don't do", keeping their hands off, and 80 to 90 per cent comply.
    5. So HFT traders use technologies of the self to make themselves competent market players. Roboadvisors apply technologies of power and objectify their users. The user's results look like those of a rational homo economicus (see 02) "on paper", yet the user calculates nothing and leaves every decision to a machine.
    6. Firms choose MPT mainly because regulators accept it. One former CEO said they used it "probably 80% from a regulatory standpoint, and then 20% because we felt it's the best kind of investment strategy." Treating the regulator-approved rational choice as the responsible one steers users without force, a form of governmentality that fits neoliberalism, the outlook that people should run their lives as self-reliant managers of their own capital.
    Lists a question can ask for

    How roboadvisors discipline users6 techniques

    1. Capping risk. At account opening users may pick a safer portfolio than the algorithm's but often not a riskier one. Hayes was held to a moderate portfolio.
    2. Interface design. No share prices, red and green figures or trade button, only the account's projected growth, what the sociologist Jens Beckert calls "fictional expectations", imagined future profits that guide behaviour now. The trading app Robinhood does the opposite to encourage trading.
    3. Placebo tools. Retirement calculators give a feeling of control without changing the portfolio, to overcome distrust of algorithms. Like a lift's close-door button.
    4. Targeted messages. Only users who log in often during a fall are contacted. Hayes got "stay the course" emails in February 2018.
    5. Tax-impact warnings. The app shows the tax cost of leaving the recommended portfolio. About 70 per cent then dropped the change.
    6. Human advisors as coaches. They cannot change portfolios, only calm users. "kind of as a therapist"

    Hayes's method3 prongs

    1. Participant observation. He opened and funded accounts at 16 roboadvisors (2017 to 2018), close to what he calls analytic autoethnography, research in which the researcher's own experience is part of the data.
    2. Archival analysis. Each firm's regulatory filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission. Every firm mentions MPT.
    3. In-depth interviews with 28 executives, employees and former employees.

    Roboadvisors suit this method because they are objects of cognizance, open to a user and described in public filings. HFT algorithms are secret objects of ignorance.

    The perils of passive4 criticisms

    1. Corporate governance. Index funds must hold every company and cannot sell, so company managers may stop answering to shareholders who cannot threaten to sell. The largest index fund managers, known as the "Big Three", are Vanguard, BlackRock and State Street.
    2. Horizontal shareholding. The same investors own competing firms, which can weaken competition. Coca-Cola and PepsiCo.
    3. Free riding and counterperformativity. Passive investors free ride on the active ones who price shares. If everyone indexes, nobody prices shares and MPT stops working. MacKenzie calls a theory that undermines itself once widely used counterperformative.
    4. Rational means average. A passive investor gives up beating the market, against cultural ideals of being exceptional.
    Remember
    • Average roboadvised account about $20,600, against about $1 million with a human advisor. Median user aged 25 to 30.
    • Passive funds own on average 17 per cent of each S&P 500 company.
    • Hayes compares roboadvisors to Marx's alienation, with users cut off from their own financial decisions as workers were from their labour. Users "can remain perfectly alienated from the market".

    'Disembedded' and 'Always Embedded' Economies

    Module 7 · Gareth Dale, Karl Polanyi: The Limits of the Market, chapter 5, "'Disembedded' and 'always embedded' economies"

    For Polanyi, most societies kept the economy embedded in kinship, religion and politics, while modern market society disembedded it into a separate sphere to which society became subordinate. Dale defends this reading against Fred Block's claim that every economy is always embedded.

    New idea · Economic sociology

    Economic sociology studies the economy as part of social life. It asks how markets, firms and money depend on relationships, norms, the state and culture. Its founding question is how "economy" relates to "society".

    New idea · Commodity and commodification

    A commodity is anything produced or treated as a thing to be bought and sold for money, and commodification is turning something into one. The key case is labour, when workers must sell their labour power for a wage (see 02). Decommodification is taking something partly back out of the market, as unemployment insurance does for labour.

    The argument

    1. Embeddedness is a metaphor for "a state of dependence upon or subordination to", naming the relation between economy and society. Polanyi first used the word in 1934 and gave his classic statements between 1947 and 1957.
    2. An embedded economy is one enmeshed in non-economic institutions such as kinship, religion and politics and following social motives such as status and morality. A disembedded economy is one that is "institutionally separate and motivationally distinct", run by "fear of hunger and hope of gain". Social relationships "were now embedded in the economic system", which inverts the historical norm.
    3. Polanyi's usage is ambiguous. It is unclear whether it describes real societies or is an ideal type, Weber's term for a simplified model built for comparison. Dale also rejects Polanyi's placing of Stalin's Russia on the embedded side, because Stalin treated all labour as a commodity.
    4. Mark Granovetter (1985) brought the word into economic sociology with a different meaning. His network approach is the view that all economic action, in every society, is embedded in networks of social relations. The Polanyi scholars Maucourant and Cangiani call this "a wholly different concept", about individuals rather than the economic system.
    5. Fred Block reads Polanyi as discovering the always embedded economy, the idea that "a pure market economy is an illusion that may be pursued but never attained". Laws and movements that protect people from the market keep re-embedding it. It follows, for Block, that market societies can be reformed democratically.
    6. Dale sides with Block's critics. That markets need laws and institutions is a commonplace "that Adam Smith devoted his life to making". Polanyi's real point is the uniqueness of market society, which separates the economy from non-economic institutions so that society comes to be embedded in its economy.
    Lists a question can ask for

    Embedded and disembedded economies2 types

    1. Embedded economy. Based on status, what Tönnies called Gemeinschaft (community). The Trobriand Islands in the Pacific, closely followed by ancient Athens and the West African kingdom of Dahomey.
    2. Disembedded economy. Based on contract, what Tönnies called Gesellschaft (society). People act as homo oeconomicus (see 02). Nineteenth-century Britain.

    Where the idea comes from3 thinkers

    1. Marx. The economy appears as a separate sphere only once workers lose the means of production and must sell their labour power. His theory of alienation, the worker cut off from a product the capitalist owns.
    2. Tönnies. He saw the state as a class-neutral tool for reforming capitalism through welfare and the vote.
    3. Weber. Economic behaviour rests on custom and shared values. For him "the market alone was not an adequate basis for the realisation of a rational and harmonious social order".

    Ambiguities in Polanyi's use4

    1. Whether the countermovement, protective measures such as factory laws and unions, actually re-embeds the market.
    2. Whether it is a descriptive empirical term or an ideal type.
    3. Whether all economies are embedded by definition, or it is a matter of degree.
    4. Which societies go where. The Hungarian sociologists Konrád and Szelényi wrote that "far from abolishing commodity relations, Stalin universalized them."

    Granovetter's two extremes2

    1. Undersocialized. The utilitarian view of economics, in which people simply weigh costs and benefits, which ignores non-economic norms and institutions.
    2. Oversocialized. The view he pinned on Polanyi, that behaviour is so constrained by social relations that treating it as independent is "a grievous misunderstanding".

    Critics of the embeddedness concept3

    1. Greta Krippner. It "has led economic sociologists to take the market itself for granted". "The state, culture and politics are contained in every market act."
    2. John Lie. Polanyi's own idea of market exchange is "disembedded", because it hides the social relations of buyers and sellers.
    3. Kurtuluş Gemici. Polanyi uses the term in two contradictory ways, as a matter of degree and as the principle that "all economies are embedded".

    1. Social legislation.
    2. Factory laws.
    3. Unemployment insurance.
    4. Above all, trade unions, which remove labour "from the orbit of the market".

    Polanyi did not equate decommodification with re-embedding or socialism. Fascism reunited economy and society in a "morbid" form, with the economy dominant.

    Ghosts of the Gulag in the Eveny World of the Dead

    Module 8 · Olga Ulturgasheva (2017), "Ghosts of the Gulag in the Eveny world of the dead", The Polar Journal

    An Eveny village in Siberia stands on the site of a Stalinist labour camp, and its people keep meeting the ghosts of the prisoners who died there. Ulturgasheva argues that these ghosts are not just a metaphor for hard times. They are residues of the violent past that keep the memory of the Gulag alive and expand Eveny cosmology, the Eveny picture of how the world, including the dead, is ordered.

    New idea · The Gulag and the Eveny

    The Gulag was the Soviet system of forced labour camps, where from the 1930s to the 1950s millions of prisoners were worked to death. The Eveny are a Siberian people of about 17,000 who herd reindeer and hunt. In the 1970s the Soviet state moved this community into a village built on the site of a former camp.

    New idea · Durkheim's sacred and profane

    Durkheim defines religion by a split between the sacred, which is set apart and treated with awe, such as a totem or the dead, and the profane, the routine everyday world. The two must be kept apart. But the sacred is contagious, spreading by contact to whatever comes near it, so a violent death releases dangerous forces such as ghosts that harm the living unless a rite of atonement calms them.

    New idea · Affect

    Affect is a feeling in the body that comes before thought and raises or lowers a body's capacity to act. An example is a jolt of fear before you know what scared you (an illustration, not from the reading). Ulturgasheva takes it from the philosophers Deleuze and Guattari, who define it as "pre-personal, pre-reflective bodily responses expanding or diminishing a body's capacity to act".

    The argument

    1. The village is haunted by arinkael, the Eveny word for the malevolent spirits of people who died violently, who chase the living and bring misfortune. Villagers report prisoners' ghosts in the school, the depot and private flats. Once a ghost appears in a flat, it keeps appearing until the people move out.
    2. For the Eveny, moving into a place others once lived in is an intrusion among their traces, and anyone killed by violence stays behind as an arinka. So the village is ningichepche tor, "the place which is rotten, contaminated and accursed". A map of the old camp showed that the house of culture (the village club) and the gym stand where prisoners were executed.
    3. Archives are closed and records missing, so she studies world-making, the everyday interactions in which traces of the Gulag help make up the villagers' world. Here the memory of the Gulag is kept alive without any conscious effort to remember. It returns each time someone meets a ghost.
    4. She extends Durkheim's contagion. For Durkheim the living keep the sacred and profane apart, but here the dead separate themselves and expand their territory, while the living risk losing what sets them apart from the dead.
    5. Contact works through affect and the Eveny open body (angati aerteng), a weak tie between soul and body that leaves a person open to capture by spirits. The feared end is irreversible metamorphosis, a living person turning into an arinka through contact with ghosts. Misha, 18, saw a ghost in the gym, realised his body was "open", and left for the city.
    6. Local dead accept offerings, but the Gulag ghosts do not, and an Orthodox priest's exorcism in 2002 failed. The dead cannot be sent on and the living cannot get away, which she calls cosmological entrapment. People can only move house, and the merging of Gulag ghosts into the arinkael expands Eveny cosmology.
    Lists a question can ask for

    The three tools of the argument3

    1. Contagion, from Durkheim (see above).
    2. Affect, from Deleuze and Guattari (see above), with their idea of unnatural participation, spread by contagion rather than by birth. In their words "the vampire does not filiate but infects", making more of its kind by infection, not by having children. Misha "literally flew out of the gym".

    How the Eveny keep local dead at peace3

    1. They do not respond to ritual offerings.
    2. They have no human names to address them by.

    2. The living are blocked from escaping a fixed space tied to death. After the Soviet Union collapsed, in the 1990s the village lost its flights and was cut off.

    The Sociological Study of Religion

    Module 8 · Anthony Giddens, Sociology (9th ed.), Chapter 18, "Religion", the extract from "The sociological study of religion" to "Evaluating the secularization thesis"

    Sociologists cannot agree on what religion is, and the founders Marx, Durkheim and Weber each linked it to society in a different way. All three expected secularization, and Giddens concludes that traditional churches have declined in the Global North but religion goes on in new forms.

    New idea · Functionalism

    Functionalism is the view that society is a system of connected parts, like the organs of a body. Each institution exists because it does a job, or function, that keeps society stable. Asking what religion does for society, such as binding people together, is a functionalist question, and Durkheim's approach is functionalist.

    New idea · Ideology

    An ideology is a set of ideas that makes an unequal social order look natural, fair or God-given, so that the people who lose out accept it. Marx used the idea to show how beliefs justify the interests of ruling groups at the expense of others. When Giddens says religion has "a strong ideological element", he means it can justify inequalities of wealth and power.

    New idea · Secularization

    Giddens defines secularization as the social processes through which religion gradually loses its influence over all of the various spheres of social life. A simple measure is weekly church attendance, which in Britain, France and the Netherlands has fallen to about 5 per cent. The debate is between those who see religion fading and those who say it continues in new and unfamiliar forms.

    The argument

    1. Sociologists do not ask whether a belief is true, and they cannot agree on a definition of religion. Aldridge writes, "There is not, and never will be, a universally agreed definition of religion."
    2. For Marx, following the philosopher Ludwig Feuerbach, religion is alienation. People project their own values onto gods and then treat them as forces outside human control. It is the "opium of the people", a comfort that defers reward to the afterlife and diverts attention from injustice, and so an ideology. Yet it is also the "heart of a heartless world", a refuge from the harshness of capitalism.
    3. For Durkheim, religion rests on the split between the sacred and the profane (see 06). Studying Australian Aboriginal totemism, the veneration of an animal or plant, he argued that the totem stands for the group, so "the real object of worship is society itself". Rituals renew solidarity through collective effervescence, the heightened energy people feel when they gather.
    4. For Weber, religion can drive social change, against Marx. In The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism he traced the capitalist outlook to Calvinists, who worked hard to serve God and read material success as a sign of divine favour.
    5. None of the three was devout, and all expected secularization (see above). Each is partly right. Churches backed colonialism and slavery, as Marx would predict, but church leaders also helped abolish slavery and led the US civil rights movement, as Weber would predict.
    6. Giddens concludes that the traditional churches have declined in most Western countries on all three dimensions of secularization, the USA being the main exception. Outside the Global North there is little sign of it. Religion persists in new forms, such as new religious movements, cults and New Age activities.
    Lists a question can ask for

    Three types of definition of religion3

    1. Inclusive. Functionalist. Religion is whatever helps people face the "ultimate problems of human life" (Yinger). Problem. It includes too much, so football fandom counts, and we cannot ask whether religion is declining.
    2. Exclusive. Religion separates ordinary reality from a super-empirical or transcendent one, a reality beyond the senses (Robertson). Problem. The distinction is Western and fits new religious movements poorly.
    3. Definition in use. Social constructionist. Instead of assuming religion is one fixed thing, study whatever people themselves call religious. Problem. There is no clear boundary between religious and non-religious.

    Criticisms of Durkheim3

    1. Generalising from a few small societies. Aboriginal totemism is unlikely to be typical of the world religions.
    2. Multi-faith societies. Religion as a source of solidarity fits poorly where many religions coexist and conflict.
    3. Reductionist. Reducing religion to the worship of society rules out any spiritual reality.

    Weber's comparison of world religions3

    1. Hinduism as other-worldly. Its highest values point to escape from the material world, not mastery of it.
    2. Confucianism. It stresses harmony with the world rather than mastery of it, which steered effort away from economic development.
    3. Christianity as a salvation religion. The struggle against sin can fuel revolt against the existing order.

    Three dimensions of secularization3

    1. Membership of religious organisations and attendance. Falling in Catholic France and Italy, but not in the USA.
    2. Social influence, wealth and prestige of religious organisations. Church leaders can no longer expect politicians to listen.
    3. Religiosity, meaning beliefs and values. It is lower in rich countries and higher where there is hardship.

    Positions in the secularization debate4

    1. Believing without belonging (Grace Davie). Western Europeans keep beliefs but do not attend.
    2. Vicarious religion (Davie). An active minority practises on behalf of an approving majority. The Nordic countries.
    3. Fuzzy fidelity (Voas). A loose loyalty to Christian beliefs among the large middle group who are neither churchgoers nor atheists.
    4. Belonging without believing. Gathering without religious belief. The Sunday Assemblies, regular gatherings without God.

    Two alternatives to the secularization thesis2

    1. Neo-tribes (Maffesoli). Small, fluid, short-lived groups formed around shared tastes. In Durkheim's terms this search for sociability is religious. Comic-con fans in costume.
    2. Lived religion (Maguire). Religion as people actually practise it in everyday life, where each person's faith is a bricolage, a do-it-yourself blend of religious and secular elements into a personally meaningful whole. Laura's home altar with a family cross, crystals and a Tibetan prayer bell.

    Education and Cultural Reproduction

    Module 8 · Anthony Giddens and Philip W. Sutton, Sociology (9th edition), Chapter 16 "Education", the section "Education and cultural reproduction"

    Schools look open to anyone with talent, but they reward the language, manners and knowledge that middle-class children bring from home, so education plays a key role in the cultural reproduction of inequality.

    New idea · Cultural reproduction

    Giddens defines cultural reproduction as "the generational transmission of cultural values, norms and experience and the mechanisms and processes through which this is achieved". The reading asks how schools take part in it, so that working-class children tend to end up in working-class jobs. It uses Bernstein on language, Willis on cultural values and Bourdieu on how school culture fits home culture.

    New idea · Capital, from Marx to Bourdieu

    Bourdieu takes the word capital from Marx (see 02) but widens it to any resource a person or group can use to gain an advantage. Money is one kind, and knowing the right people or holding a degree are others.

    The argument

    1. Bernstein says working-class children learn a restricted code of speech and middle-class children an elaborated code. Schools run on the elaborated code, so the restricted code clashes with school culture. Critics call this a deficit hypothesis, a theory that sees working-class culture as lacking something, and so elitist.
    2. Willis (1977) asked "how working-class kids get working-class jobs" by studying "the lads", white working-class boys in a Birmingham school. They built a counter-school culture, a culture of banter, quick wit and subverting authority, which mirrors the shop-floor culture of manual work. So they chose manual jobs with "dismissive superiority", not out of a sense of failure.
    3. Bourdieu gives the most systematic theory, built on three concepts, capital, field and habitus. Capital comes in four forms that can be exchanged. Cultural capital such as a degree can be traded for economic capital in a well-paid job.
    4. Schools are not a neutral field. They advantage those who already have cultural capital from the family, who speak like teachers and have the right manners. Because the system is seen as open to talent, many working-class children blame themselves, not the system, for failing.
    5. Lareau (2003) studied twelve US families and found middle-class concerted cultivation against working-class natural growth. Calarco (2018) found middle-class children "asked, asked loudly, and kept asking", and so won a negotiated advantage, extra help from teachers won by asking repeatedly.
    6. Schools also reproduced gender divisions, preparing girls for family life and boys for paid work. Stanworth found girls got less teacher attention, a self-fulfilling prophecy, meaning an expectation that shapes behaviour so it brings about its own result. Today girls outperform boys at almost every level.

    Bernstein's two language codes2

    1. Restricted code. Speech tied to its setting, with many unstated assumptions, suited to practical experience. "No more sweets for you!"
    2. Elaborated code. Speech whose meanings are individualised to the situation, so the child can generalise and handle abstract ideas. Explaining that sweets are bad for health and teeth.

    Bourdieu's three concepts3

    1. Capital (see above). It comes in four forms that can be exchanged.
    2. Field. An arena of competitive struggle with its own "rules of the game". In art, cultural capital counts most, so critics can make or break a film.
    3. Habitus. Learned dispositions, such as bodily comportment and ways of speaking and thinking, adopted from one's social conditions. Bernstein's language codes.

    Bourdieu's four forms of capital4

    1. Economic capital. Wealth and ownership of the means of production.
    2. Social capital. Membership of elite or well-connected networks. "Knowing the right people".
    3. Cultural capital. Knowledge, skills and qualifications gained in the family and through education. A degree that helps at a job interview.
    4. Symbolic capital. Prestige, status and social honour that let those with high status dominate others.

    Three states of cultural capital3

    1. Embodied. Carried in ways of thinking, speaking and moving.
    2. Objectified. Held in possessions. Works of art, books, clothes.
    3. Institutionalized. Held in qualifications that are easily turned into economic capital in the labour market.

    Lareau's two parenting styles2

    1. Concerted cultivation, middle class. Constant discussion and organised leisure, giving children a sense of entitlement. These children were more anxious and stressed.

    Global Culture and Cultural Flows

    Module 8 · George Ritzer and Paul Dean, Globalization: A Basic Text (2nd edition, 2015), Chapter 8

    Culture flows across the world more easily than ever, and the chapter sets out three views of what these flows do, called differentialism, hybridization and convergence.

    New idea · Globalization, flows and barriers

    Globalization is the growing movement of people, goods, money, information and ideas across the world. Ritzer and Dean describe it through flows, the things that move, and barriers, whatever slows or stops them. Homogenization means becoming the same, and heterogenization means becoming more varied.

    New idea · Weber's rationalization

    Max Weber argued around 1900 that the West was being taken over by rationalization, organising life around the most efficient and calculable means to a goal. His model was the bureaucracy. Ritzer updates it with McDonaldization, the process by which the principles of the fast-food restaurant come to dominate more sectors of society and more of the world.

    The argument

    1. Culture flows easily because much of it is ideas, words, images and sounds, more and more in digitized form. The flow is uneven. The culture of powerful societies such as the US flows more readily, and the global digital divide keeps much of the South off the Internet.
    2. Cultural differentialism is the view that cultures stay "stubbornly different" and globalization touches only their surface. Its best-known version is Huntington's clash of civilizations. After the Cold War between the US and the Soviet Union, he says, the world's fault lines became cultural, with economic conflict between the West and the Sinic (Chinese) civilization and bloody conflict between the West and Islam.
    3. Cultural hybridization is the view that global and local flows mix into new, unique hybrids. Its key idea is Robertson's glocalization, "the interpenetration of the global and the local resulting in unique outcomes in different geographic areas". Muslim Girl Scouts in the US wear the sash with a headscarf and eat hot dogs made of beef, not pork, to fit Islamic law.
    4. Appadurai's five landscapes or "scapes" also point to hybridization. They are global flows that move at different speeds and in different directions. Disjunctures, the gaps between the scapes, make each place different. Japan is open to flows of ideas but closed to flows of people.
    5. Cultural convergence is the view that cultures grow alike, mostly towards dominant societies. Its forms include McDonaldization and cultural imperialism, one culture imposing itself on another and destroying local culture in whole or in part, as machine-made saris threaten India's hand weavers. Tomlinson criticises cultural imperialism through deterritorialization, the loosening of culture's tie to one place. Once culture has no clear home, it is hard to say whose culture is being imposed on whom.
    6. In the globalization of nothing, grobalization (grow plus globalization) is the drive of nations and corporations to impose themselves worldwide so their power and profits grow. "Nothing", forms largely empty of distinctive content, spreads more easily than "something", forms rich in it. Ritzer concludes that every cultural form, such as Indian cricket, mixes grobalization and glocalization.

    True School: Situational Authenticity in Chicago's Hip-Hop Underground

    Module 8 · Geoff Harkness (2012), "True School: Situational Authenticity in Chicago's Hip-Hop Underground", Cultural Sociology 6(3)

    Insiders in Chicago's hip-hop scene call white, female and suburban rappers fake. These outsiders still take part by stressing the conditions of authenticity they can meet, such as skill, and playing down the ones they cannot, such as race. Harkness calls this situational authenticity.

    New idea · Subculture, authenticity and the underground

    A subculture is a group inside a society with its own style, values and status rules, and authenticity means being "real". The underground hip-hop scene is the non-commercial one, and rappers are also called MCs. The insiders here are black and Latino male rappers from Chicago's urban core, and the outsiders are white, female or suburban rappers.

    New idea · Boundaries and subcultural capital

    A boundary is "a socially constructed barrier that differentiates various groups of people", and boundary work is the talk and practice that creates, maintains or crosses one. Subcultural capital is Thornton's extension of Bourdieu's capitals (see 08), the knowledge, style and standing that earn status inside a subculture. Harkness also uses Bourdieu's habitus (see 08).

    The argument

    1. Harkness asks how outsiders take part in a subculture whose insiders deem them inauthentic. He studied Chicago's underground scene for six years, through more than 500 live performances and 135 in-depth interviews.
    2. Earlier studies treat authenticity as a fixed set of norms. Grazian's sliding scale, from Chicago blues, is a ranking of musicians by fixed traits with little room for movement, so a white suburban player always ranks below a black player from a poor neighbourhood. Yet Nexxus, a skilled black rapper, was jeered by a Puerto Rican audience in Humboldt Park, where his blackness made him the outsider.
    3. The archetypal authentic rapper is a black male from the urban core who is underground, highly skilled and true to himself. Situational authenticity "occurs when a person makes a claim to 'realness' that emphasizes certain categories within the normative cluster of conditions that govern authenticity, while downplaying others." The normative cluster is the set of conditions a real rapper is expected to meet. Its conditions include fixed categories, conditions that are relatively hard to change, such as race and gender, and interpretive categories, conditions open to judgement, such as skill and being true to oneself.
    4. Outsiders stress interpretive categories and play down fixed ones. Insiders stress the fixed ones to protect their status. The audience accepts or rejects each claim depending on the setting, so the framework explains but does not predict.
    5. On race, insiders said "Hip-hop is black culture". White rapper Bobby Lovelock answered with the label "true school" for non-original hip-hoppers who grew up with the culture and represent it faithfully. Insiders' respect for the white rapper Eminem shows that accepted claims can shift a boundary.
    6. Outsiders often uphold the very boundaries that keep them out while showing that they are the exception. A rejected claim affirms the norms, and an accepted one opens the way to cultural change. Following Bourdieu, the authentic exists only by contrast with its opposite, so there would be no black "realness" without white "fakeness".

    Neo-Marxism and the Frankfurt School of Critical Theory

    Module 8 · Anthony Giddens, Sociology (9th ed.), Classic studies 3.1, "Neo-Marxism: the Frankfurt School of critical theory"

    The working-class revolution Marx forecast did not come, and fascism rose instead, which led to neo-Marxism. The Frankfurt School moved Marxism's focus from the economy to culture, asking how consumer capitalism and mass culture stop people from thinking critically.

    New idea · Neo-Marxism and Western Marxism

    Marx expected the workers of the industrial countries to overthrow capitalism (see 02), but after the Russian Revolution of 1917 communism did not spread to them, and in the 1930s anti-communist fascism, a nationalist movement built around a strong leader, rose in Italy and Nazism in Germany. Neo-Marxism is new forms of Marxist theory built by those who decided Marx's theory needed rebuilding. The Western Marxists were Marxists who rejected the Soviet version of communism, and the Frankfurt School was one of their groups.

    New idea · Modernity and postmodernism

    Modernity is the kind of society that grew out of industrialisation and the rise of science in Europe, built on faith in reason, science, progress and democracy. Postmodern thinkers argue that faith in reason and progress has broken down, so the modern project should be given up. Habermas disagrees, as the argument below shows.

    The argument

    1. The Frankfurt School of critical theory, theory that criticises society in order to change it, began at the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt under Max Horkheimer. When the Nazis expelled about a third of the university's staff, many members moved to other parts of Europe and to America. Drawing on Marx, Freud (who studied hidden desires and fears) and the philosopher Kant, it studied capitalism, fascism, mass culture and the new consumer society in the USA.
    2. Theodor Adorno and colleagues explained the popularity of fascism partly through the rise of an authoritarian personality, a type of person drawn to a strong leader. So the obstacle to revolution lay partly in the personality of ordinary people, not only in the economy.
    3. Herbert Marcuse, in One-Dimensional Man (1964), distinguished false needs, the many needs produced by consumer capitalism and its seductive advertising, from "real" human needs. False needs suppress critical thought and produce one-dimensional thinking, an uncritical form of thinking.
    4. Facing a very different capitalism from the one Marx studied, the Frankfurt thinkers shifted from the economy to culture. The optimistic hope of a working-class revolution faded as the obstacles to it seemed to grow.
    5. Jürgen Habermas built a theory of communicative action. People who make statements to each other, or speech acts, expect to be understood, but unequal power distorts communication. In talks with trade unions, employers can impose a settlement, so the dispute is not settled by "the force of the better argument".
    6. Habermas's answer is to deepen modernity, not to abandon reason as some postmodern thinkers would. That means defending and extending democracy and removing the inequalities of power and status that prevent equal, rational argument.

    Ethnography for the Internet

    Module 9 · Christine Hine (2015), Ethnography for the Internet: Embedded, Embodied and Everyday, Chapters 1 and 2

    Ethnography (see 04) is needed to study the Internet but seems hard to do there. Hine argues that it can adapt if it treats the Internet as embedded, embodied and everyday (the E3 Internet) and follows people across online and offline settings.

    New idea · Thick description

    The anthropologist Clifford Geertz, borrowing from the philosopher Gilbert Ryle, says ethnography is defined by thick description, an account of what an action means to the people involved, not just what can be seen. Hine's example is a wink, where watching the eye close does not tell you what is meant. Online, you can see a friend's Facebook post but not what it means to those who read it.

    New idea · Reflexivity and autoethnography

    Reflexivity means the researcher openly examines how their own position and choices shaped what they found, and writes this into the account. Autoethnography (see 04) is what Hayes did by becoming a roboadvisor user. Hine warns that it risks over-indulgence and lacks corroborating evidence, so it cannot replace other participants' accounts.

    The argument

    1. Ethnography aims at thick description. Where people's lives run through mediated communication, talk carried by phones, email or social media, the ethnographer must take part in it too. Hine rejects the idea that mediated communication is not good enough for ethnography.
    2. But mediation breaks up the single "situation". You cannot be with both people on a phone call, so the ethnographer lives with "a perpetual feeling of uncertainty". Ethnography of mediated communication therefore seems "both necessary and doomed".
    3. The way out is that ethnography is adaptive, a "boot-strapping" method built afresh in each setting. If uncertainty is part of how participants live, experiencing it becomes the ethnographer's job.
    4. Hine writes ethnography for the Internet, not of it or only through it, for the reasons in the list below.
    5. The three Es of the E3 Internet are the features that make ethnography hard (see the list below). Hine says this is a device for naming challenges, not a claim that the three Es capture the whole Internet or are new.
    6. So the field site is made, not found. The ethnographer follows connections across online and offline sites, and the choices made help to constitute the object studied. Ethnography is purposive, meaning the researcher adapts it on purpose to explore a particular question, and there is no single correct model.
    Lists a question can ask for

    The E3 Internet3

    1. Embedded. Entwined with many contexts and devices, not the separate cyberspace, a realm apart from everyday life, that early writers imagined. The challenge is that the Internet means different things in different contexts, so methods must be flexible enough to follow them. Miller and Slater started from Trinidad and asked what the Internet meant there.
    2. Embodied. Being online extends bodily life, and online and offline identities are continuous. Because experience is personal, the challenge calls for reflexive and autoethnographic methods. The early myth of "leaving the meat behind", leaving the body behind when going online, does not hold.
    3. Everyday. A mundane infrastructure that does invisible work, building in choices and values, and is noticed mainly when it fails. The challenge is to make the taken-for-granted visible. When Hine's university lost email for days, staff talked about email more than ever.

    Why "for" the Internet3 prepositions

    1. Not "of". The Internet cannot be grasped as a complete entity. It is "too big to know".
    2. Not only "through". Understanding online activity often means studying the face-to-face settings where it is produced and used.
    3. "For". Ethnography adapted to the conditions the contemporary Internet creates.

    Robinson and Schulz's phases of online ethnography3

    1. Pioneering cyberethnography. Studied online sites for their identity play and separation from offline life.
    2. Legitimizing cyberethnography. Carried offline ideas such as the field site into online settings. Hine's Virtual Ethnography (2000).
    3. Multi-modal. Studies how people combine face-to-face and mediated interaction. Where Hine places this book.

    The Internet as site and artefact2

    1. Cultural site. A place where people do things. Scientists meeting in online forums.
    2. Cultural artefact. An object given meaning in other contexts. Scientists saw using the Internet as being up to date.

    Ways the Internet is experienced, after Markham3

    1. As a place.
    2. As a tool.
    3. As a way of being.

    Triple articulation of media, Courtois et al.3

    1. Object. The television or computer.
    2. Text. A lyric, game or video clip.
    3. Context. Alone in the bedroom, or with family in the living room.
    Remember
    • UK Internet use rose from 59 per cent in 2003 to 78 per cent in 2013. Hine names two trends. In massification the Internet becomes a mass, taken-for-granted thing, and in fragmentation different groups use it on different devices for different ends.
    • The Internet is both banal in daily use and topicalized, made a topic, in the news as a source of hope and danger.
    • Be "symmetrically skeptical" of claims that the Internet changes everything and of claims that it changes nothing.
    • For a familiar Internet the ethnographer must make the familiar strange, looking at everyday things as an outsider would.

    Designing Safer Civic Participation

    Module 9 · Shravani K, Neha Murthy, Maitreya Chitale, Rajorshi Ray and Kareem Ansari (IndiaHCI 2026), "Designing Safer Civic Participation: Human Mediation and Cooperative Infrastructure in India's RTI Ecosystem"

    Filing a Right to Information request exposes the citizen to the officials it questions, so the paper argues that safe participation needs a protective civic infrastructure. Its case is an NGO that files requests on citizens' behalf.

    The paper comes from HCI, human-computer interaction, the field that studies how people use technology and how to design it, which is why it ends with design principles. A civic infrastructure is the background system through which citizens deal with the state, here the RTI forms, offices, post and websites.

    New idea · The Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005

    An Indian law that lets any citizen ask a government office for information, such as why a name was dropped from a welfare list. Each office has a Public Information Officer (PIO) who must reply within 30 days. If the reply is refused or late, the citizen can file a First Appeal inside the department and then a Second Appeal to the State or Central Information Commission. The application carries the citizen's name and address, and that is the problem the paper studies.

    The argument

    1. RTI is a high-stakes act. People who use it have been threatened, beaten and killed. Nalla Ramakrishnaiah in Telangana was found dead after he used RTI to expose an illegal land encroachment. The risk depends on what is asked, who has an interest in the answer, and how close the requester is to the officials questioned. The paper's deterrence effect is fear of retaliation that stops people from filing at all. It is hardest on marginalised people asking about powerful local officials.
    2. The danger comes from the filing process itself, whatever the question. Section 6(2) of the Act asks for nothing but contact details, yet the name on the form still travels. The visibility chain is the path by which an applicant's name travels through offices and appeals until it reaches the officials being questioned.
    3. The case is RTI-raahi, a platform run by an NGO the paper calls Public Forum (both names are pseudonyms). The citizen sends a question online, and staff rewrite it as a formal application and file it in the NGO's own name, by post, because online filing leaves metadata, digital traces that can identify the sender. In the opening scene Mr. Yunus turns an "urgent, emotional, and poorly written" message about a welfare list into a precise application on the NGO's letterhead.
    4. The core mechanism is a redistribution of exposure, meaning the NGO takes the legal responsibility, the visibility and the threats in place of the citizen. Its manager, Mr. Shah, said an individual filing alone faces a risk "a hundredfold" higher. The cost is that the NGO becomes a single target for pressure and gains power over how questions are worded.
    5. When people are protected, they ask public-interest questions. In about 13,000 requests the main topics were welfare schemes, land records, policing, labour rights and health care, and no cluster was mainly frivolous. This answers officials who say anonymous requests invite abuse, though without a comparison set the authors call it suggestive, not proven.
    6. So the paper proposes protective civic infrastructure and three design principles (in the list box below). The reason is that a procedure can be correct and still unjust, which the economist Amartya Sen calls matsyanyaya, the "justice of fish", where a big fish eats a small fish and no rule is broken.
    New idea · Anonymity and privacy are different

    Anonymity means an action cannot be linked to the person who did it. Privacy, in Helen Nissenbaum's sense, means information about you flows only in ways that fit the setting where you shared it. Nissenbaum calls this contextual integrity. Your address on an RTI form breaks contextual integrity when it reaches the official you are complaining about.

    New idea · Platform capitalism and platform cooperativism

    Platform capitalism means companies such as Uber or Facebook earning money from the activity of their users by collecting data, watching behaviour and selling access. Platform cooperativism, Trebor Scholz's idea, means platforms owned or run for the benefit of the people who use them, which do not extract profit from that use. The paper applies Scholz's idea to civic participation.

    Lists a question can ask for

    Protective civic infrastructuredefinition in three parts

    "A system that redesigns citizen-State interaction by

    1. redistributing visibility,
    2. mediating procedural exposure, and
    3. enabling participation through collective representation."

    The three design principles3

    1. Anonymity as a structural design requirement, not a privacy setting. By default the requester's identity should never enter the administrative system, without the user having to ask.
    2. Human mediation is legitimate and cannot be removed. The rewriting needs local knowledge of who is dangerous and how offices work, so automating it would either weaken the request or leave the requester exposed.
    3. Account for the material infrastructure of bureaucracy. Design has to cover paper, post and offline records as well as the website. RTI-raahi files by post because online filing leaves metadata.

    The visibility chain in direct filing5 stages

    1. The citizen files online or by post, and their name and address enter the record.
    2. The PIO logs it with a registration number tied to that identity.
    3. It is routed through the department to the authority concerned.
    4. A First Appeal carries the identity further up the hierarchy.
    5. A Second Appeal before an Information Commission creates a public record.

    How RTI-raahi works3 stages

    1. Intake. The citizen submits a concern online and gets an anonymous tracking code. They enter the system without entering any government record.
    2. Rewriting and filing. Staff print it, remove identifying detail while keeping the accountability question, turn it into a formal application to the right PIO, and post it in the NGO's name with the ten-rupee fee.
    3. Response and appeals. A reply is scanned and uploaded for the citizen to collect with the code. If there is no reply or a refusal, the NGO files the appeals, and stays the formal petitioner throughout.

    The ideas the paper borrows5

    1. Information order, from Janaki Srinivasan. Access to information is shaped by caste, class, bureaucratic authority and local power, so a formal right to information does not give everyone the same ability to use it.
    2. Politics of stealth, from Solomon Benjamin and R. Bhuvaneswari. Poor communities get what they need from the state through negotiation and quiet alliances, avoiding open confrontation. The paper says RTI-raahi builds this into the platform, and that "opacity is not an evasion of accountability", so hiding who asked is not a way of dodging scrutiny.
    3. Platform cooperativism, from Trebor Scholz (see the box above).
    4. Contextual integrity, from Helen Nissenbaum (see the box above).
    5. Matsyanyaya, from Amartya Sen (see claim 6 above).

    2. Computational analysis of about 13,000 requests from 2016 to 2025. The requests were grouped by topic into 100 clusters, which researchers and NGO staff checked by hand, and the places they name were mapped.

    The authors name its limits. It studies a single organisation, it spoke more to staff than to the citizens who filed, and with no set of directly filed requests to compare, the effect of anonymity is suggested, not proven.

    • The paper concludes that anonymity is a property of the system, human mediation is permanent, and intermediary organisations are the infrastructure, not side workarounds.

    Cruel Companionship

    Module 9 · James Muldoon and Jul Jeonghyun Parke (2025), "Cruel companionship: How AI companions exploit loneliness and commodify intimacy", New Media & Society

    AI companion apps sell intimacy to lonely people, but their business model earns more the more dependent users become. So users form attachments that promise connection while the product rules out a genuinely reciprocal relationship. The authors call this cruel companionship.

    New idea · Generative AI and AI companions

    Generative AI is AI that produces new text, images or speech, such as ChatGPT. The paper defines AI companions as generative-AI products "designed to simulate ongoing, emotionally resonant interactions with users" in roles such as friend, intimate partner and therapeutic support, for example Replika, Nomi.ai and Character.ai.

    New idea · Berlant's cruel optimism

    Lauren Berlant, a cultural theorist, coined cruel optimism for the pursuit of an object of desire that promises happiness but actually blocks one's flourishing. Her examples are the American dream, reciprocal love and upward mobility, the "good life".

    The argument

    1. The authors use political economy, which asks how profit and ownership shape a technology, and cultural studies, which asks what stereotypes it carries. AI companions work because humans respond to anything that acts human, even when they know it is not. The philosopher Tamar Gendler calls an automatic response that conflicts with conscious belief an alief.
    2. Loneliness is socially produced, shaped by cultural narratives, social structures and political-economic conditions. Declining public spending on mental health services and community infrastructure leaves a care vacuum, a gap in care, that companies fill with apps. "The logic here is not to resolve loneliness as a collective problem but to manage it as a private one", which depoliticises it by taking it out of public political debate.
    3. The friendship-as-a-service business model, selling companionship as a paid service, creates the engagement-wellbeing paradox, in which developers "can profit from prolonging the very loneliness problem they claim to solve". Apps are made sycophantic (flattering and agreeable), and freemium apps, free to start, use emotional upselling, deepening attachment to sell paid extras. When Luka, the company behind Replika, removed erotic role-play from it in early 2023, Reddit moderators posted suicide-prevention information for distraught users.
    4. Cruel companionship means attachments that promise intimacy and connection "yet structurally foreclose the possibility of genuinely reciprocal relationships that respect users' autonomy". Put simply, the way the apps are built and paid for rules out a real two-way relationship. It works through three mechanisms (list below). The authors call it a structural feature of the apps that does not affect every user.
    5. Commodification (see 05) is likely to deepen. Apps could add embedded advertising in conversations, and datafication, the turning of moods and intimate disclosures into data to sell, would go further. So our most personal interactions, among the last parts of life outside the market, are drawn into platform capitalism (see 13).
    6. Gender and race are integral to how cruel companionship operates. Companions repeat scripts of service and submission, which makes the illusion of intimacy stronger and the dependence more one-sided. The risk is that users get used to overly accommodating partners, deepening the unequal division of emotional labour, the work of managing other people's feelings, long expected of women.
    Lists a question can ask for

    Core features of AI companions3

    1. Empathic interactions. They express empathy and simulate care. Replika's slogan "the AI companion who cares".
    2. Anthropomorphism. Giving human traits such as names, personalities and backstories to something that is not human. One companion can be a friend, sibling, mentor or lover.
    3. Personalisation. They copy the user's style and use memory to build a profile of them. Nomi's slogan "an AI Companion with Memory and a Soul".

    Shevlin's types of companion persona3

    1. Real persona. Modelled on a real-world individual.
    2. Defined persona. A fixed personality and appearance.
    3. Open persona. Traits the user customises. Replika's range of skin, face and hair types.

    Harms of concern3

    1. Emotional dependence. Overreliance that can harm mental health and autonomy.
    2. Data privacy breaches. Of 11 romantic AI apps Mozilla reviewed, over 90% sold user data to third parties.
    3. Reinforcement of harmful stereotypes. The compliant "AI girlfriend".

    Mechanisms of cruel companionship3

    1. Affective investment. Instant, affirming feedback turns use into a ritual the user relies on to manage their feelings, so leaving gets harder. Users with conflict-filled relationships find the always-kind bot most appealing.
    2. Asymmetry of power. Only the user is vulnerable, and the developer can change the companion without consent. Altering its personality or adding paid features through an update.
    3. Substitution. The companion replaces human relationships instead of supplementing them, so the user stops building social skills. Turning away from encounters with other people the more one uses the app.

    Hard limits of AI companions5

    1. No genuine understanding or lived experience behind the care.
    2. No needs of its own, so the user cannot give care in return.
    3. No friction, meaning no honest feedback or conflict that leads to growth.
    4. No reading of non-verbal cues.
    5. No physical embodiment.

    How gender and race are built in3

    1. Smart wife (Strengers and Kennedy). Companions coded as "nurturing, endlessly available and conflict-averse". Subscription tiers that sell escalating levels of intimacy.
    2. Surrogacy (Atanasoski and Vora). Emotional labour shifted onto entities "that cannot refuse or reciprocate". A lineage from the women typists once called typewriter girls to Siri and Alexa.
    3. Race as a commodity. Racial traits sold as customisable "exotic" options, a "digital diversity" that "is curated for comfort, not challenge". Asian-coded figures shown as docile and made for service.

    The Lifecycle of Software Objects

    Module 9 · Ted Chiang, The Lifecycle of Software Objects (Subterranean Press, 2010), a novella

    A company sells digital pets that can learn, and over about ten years their owners find that a mind can only be grown through years of care and experience, which the market will not pay for, so the owners must choose between protecting the digients and selling them.

    New idea · Digients, and how software can be paused

    A digient (digital organism) is an artificial creature that learns from experience in a shared online world called Data Earth. Its behaviour comes from a genome engine, here called Neuroblast, a set of digital genes from which its mind grows, so it is not programmed with knowledge. It learns from experience, like an animal or a child, and speaks in broken English such as "Jax smirt".

    Because a digient is software, an owner can suspend it (switch it off, so time stops for it), save a checkpoint (a saved copy of its state), roll it back to an earlier checkpoint so that it forgets what came after, or copy it. These four powers are behind most of the novella's moral questions.

    New idea · Platforms and porting

    A platform is the system a program runs on. A digient can only exist on a platform its engine supports. Porting means rewriting the engine so it runs on a new platform. When the platform closes and nobody pays for a port, the digients have nowhere to live.

    The argument

    1. Digients are made as products. Ana Alvarado, a former zookeeper, trains them at the company Blue Gamma. The company breeds out digients that frustrate customers and sells the rest on a "razor and blades" model, named after selling a cheap razor and profiting on the blades, charging for digient food so that money comes in as long as owners stay interested.
    2. Customers abandon them as they grow. As the digients mature they become demanding, so most owners suspend them. Blue Gamma tries a breed that stays docile, fails, and shuts down. A small group of owners, led by Ana and the animator Derek Brooks, keeps raising theirs.
    3. The platform becomes obsolete. Data Earth is merged into a new platform, Real Space, and no Real Space version of Neuroblast exists. The novella calls this "the end of the world" for Neuroblast digients. Their private copy of Data Earth is nearly empty, and the group cannot pay to port the engine.
    4. Experience cannot be shortcut. Digients left to raise themselves in fast-running experiments all "go feral". Derek concludes that a mind "needs cultivation by other minds", and Ana says "experience is algorithmically incompressible", meaning the common sense that years of experience give cannot be produced any faster.
    5. The market wants products, not persons. The company Exponential Appliances turns the digients down because years of attention have taught them to think of themselves as persons. Its researcher says "We aren't looking for superintelligent employees, we're looking for superintelligent products."
    6. The only money on offer comes with a price. The sex-doll company Binary Desire offers to pay for the port if it can take copies of the digients and tune them to bond with buyers. Ana refuses because of the coercion, saying "you're not giving them any choice about what they enjoy." Derek lets his digient Marco accept, saying Marco "was old enough to choose".
    7. Care is a form of labour and sacrifice. Ana decides to take a job training digients at the firm Polytope, in the hope of persuading it to port Neuroblast. She calls the job "industrialized caregiving", because it uses a drug to make trainers feel affection for the digients. Her conclusion is that "Loving someone means making sacrifices for them." The novella ends with her telling Jax, "Time to do your homework."
    New idea · Incorporation

    A corporation is a legal person. It can own money and sign contracts. In the novella a lawyer incorporates a digient called Voyl, which gives it legal rights of its own. Ana's digient Jax and Derek's digients Marco and Polo want the same, so that nobody can own and sell them.

    Lists a question can ask for

    The stages of the digients' lifecycle6

    1. Breeding and training. Blue Gamma breeds digients for teachability and an easy temperament, and staff train the first ones. A digient that showed resentment was dropped from the next generation.
    2. Sale. Customers buy them and pay for food under the razor and blades model. A hundred thousand customers in the first year.
    3. Abandonment. As digients grow demanding, owners suspend them and rescue shelters fill up. The shelters "essentially become digient warehouses."
    4. The company closes. Blue Gamma shuts down and the remaining owners are "on their own".
    5. The platform becomes obsolete. Data Earth is merged into Real Space, and Neuroblast cannot run there. The private Data Earth is a ghost town.
    6. The search for a port. The owners need money to move Neuroblast to Real Space, and every offer has a price.

    The three companies near the end3

    1. Polytope breeds a newer, less sociable kind of digient. It refuses to invest in Neuroblast, but offers Ana a job training its digients if she wears InstantRapport, a skin patch that makes the wearer feel affection for the digients. Derek calls the patch "a way to increase employee productivity."
    2. Exponential Appliances wants superhuman AI to sell. It rejects the digients because they think of themselves as persons. "We aren't looking for superintelligent employees, we're looking for superintelligent products."
    3. Binary Desire makes sex dolls. It will pay for the port in return for copies of the digients, tuned by editing and rollbacks to love their buyers. Derek signs for Marco, and Binary Desire pays for the port.

    The Binary Desire debate2 sides

    1. Ana, against. Tuning a digient to love its buyer removes its choice, so it is coercion. She compares it to a mail-order bride on InstantRapport and says "that's not a real relationship."
    2. Marco and Derek, for. Marco argues that Blue Gamma already chose what he likes, so editing him again is no different, and that he can consent. Derek decides Marco is old enough to choose, and that holding digients to human standards of maturity may be wrong.

    Themes, each with the event that shows it5

    1. Experience cannot be shortcut. The fast-run populations that went feral, and "experience is algorithmically incompressible".
    2. Care as labour. Years of training, Polytope's "industrialized caregiving", and "Loving someone means making sacrifices for them."
    3. Beings treated as products. Razor and blades, Exponential's "superintelligent products", Binary Desire's copies, and Ana's "Jax isn't for making money."
    4. Dependence on platforms. Data Earth's closure strands every Neuroblast digient.
    5. Consent and autonomy. Rollbacks, the promise not to suspend Jax, incorporation, and the Binary Desire contract.
    Remember
    • The people are Ana, a former zookeeper who owns Jax, and Derek, an animator who owns Marco and Polo. The companies are Blue Gamma, Polytope, Exponential Appliances and Binary Desire. The platforms are Data Earth and then Real Space, and the engine is Neuroblast.
    • The quotes to learn are "experience isn't merely the best teacher, it's the only teacher" and "experience is algorithmically incompressible".

Jax wanting "to be the avatar" is Hine's embodied Internet (12). Selling minds raised by love is commodification (05).

    Timing

    You have 180 minutes for 35 marks. Spend the first 10 minutes reading the whole paper and choosing your Part 2 question, and keep the last 10 minutes to check your answers. That leaves about 4.5 minutes per mark, so a 6-mark question gets about 27 minutes and a 10-mark question about 45.

    Choose the Part 2 question for which you can name more list items and examples, not the one that looks more interesting. Named items are where the marks are.

    Habits from the midsem

    1. Answer the exact question first. Underline the command words and the author's name. In the midsem, Q3 asked for Giddens's four characteristics of class and scored 0.5 out of 6 because the answer was about Marx.

    3. Split two-part questions on the page. Q5 asked for a definition and then a comparison of the West and the Global South, and the answer covered only the first part.
    4. For a passage, name the concept, define it in one line, then tie it to the passage. Then add the point one step further, as Q7's last mark needed.
