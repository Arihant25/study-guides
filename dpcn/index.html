0a · Networks

A network (or graph) is a set of nodes joined by links. In a network of friendships, each node is a person and each link joins two people who know each other. N is the number of nodes and L is the number of links.

Degree. The degree ki of node i is the number of links at that node. In the picture, kA=3, kB=kC=kD=2 and kE=1. Every link has two ends, so the degrees add up to twice the number of links. This gives the average degree.

∑iki=2L,⟨k⟩=2LN.

The symbol ∑i (a capital Greek sigma) means "add up over every node i". So ∑iki=k1+k2+⋯+kN, the sum of all the degrees. In the picture it is 3+2+2+2+1=10, which equals 2L=2×5. The angle brackets ⟨ ⟩ mean "the average of". The average degree is the sum of the degrees divided by the number of nodes, so in the picture ⟨k⟩=105=2.

Degree distribution. P(k) is the fraction of nodes that have degree k. In the picture, P(2)=35 because three of the five nodes have degree 2. The fractions add up to 1.

Power law and scale-free. A degree distribution of the form P(k)=Ck−γ is called a power law. Here γ (the Greek letter gamma) is a fixed number called the exponent, and C is a constant chosen so that all the fractions add up to 1. A network whose P(k) is a power law is called scale-free.

Why "scale-free". A power law looks the same at every scale of k. Doubling the degree always divides P(k) by the same number, 2γ, whether you go from 2 to 4 or from 200 to 400, because P(2k)P(k)=C(2k)−γCk−γ=2−γ. So there is no degree that counts as typical, and no "scale" for the degrees. An exponential such as e−k/m does have a scale. Most nodes have degree within a few m of the smallest, and P(2k)P(k)=e−k/m gets smaller as k grows.

The symbol ∝ means "is proportional to", that is, equal apart from a constant factor. So P(k)∝k−3 means P(k)=Ck−3 for some constant C.

In a power law, large degrees are rare but not extremely rare. With γ=3, going from k=m to k=20m divides P(k) by 203=8000. A large network then has a few nodes with very many links, and these are called hubs. Compare an exponential distribution such as P(k)∝e−k/m, where e=2.718… Every time k increases by m, P(k) is divided by e once more. Going from k=m to k=20m divides it by e19, about 180 million. The fraction falls so fast that there are no hubs.

Path length. The distance between two nodes is the smallest number of links you have to cross to get from one to the other. In the picture the distance from B to E is 3 (B to A, A to D, D to E). The average path length ℓ‾ is the average distance over all pairs of nodes.

Clustering coefficient. This measures how many of a node's neighbours are linked to each other. A node with ki neighbours has ki(ki−1)2 pairs of neighbours. (Each of the ki neighbours can be paired with the ki−1 others. That counts every pair twice, so divide by 2.) This number is also written (ki2), read "ki choose 2". Then

Ci=number of linked pairs of neighbours of iki(ki−1)/2.

Node A has neighbours B, C and D, so 3 pairs. Only B and C are linked, so CA=13. The average clustering coefficient C is the average of Ci over all nodes. Each linked pair of neighbours forms a triangle with node i, so high clustering means many triangles.

0b · Equations that change in time

x˙ (read "x dot") means dxdt, the rate at which x changes with time. An equation like x˙=f(x) gives that rate for every value of x.

Fixed point. A fixed point x∗ is a value where the rate is zero, f(x∗)=0. If x starts exactly there, it stays there.

Stability. A fixed point is stable if a small change away from it shrinks back to zero over time, and unstable if a small change grows. For one variable the test is the slope of f.

x˙=f(x):x∗ is stable if f′(x∗)<0 and unstable if f′(x∗)>0.

Here is the reason. Suppose f′(x∗)<0, so f is decreasing through zero at x∗. Just above x∗, f is negative, so x˙<0 and x decreases back towards x∗. Just below x∗, f is positive, so x increases back towards x∗. Either way x returns, so x∗ is stable. If f′(x∗)>0, the signs are the other way round and x moves away.

The Jacobian. With several variables, say x˙=f(x,y) and y˙=g(x,y), the test uses the Jacobian matrix. Row 1 holds the partial derivatives of f, and row 2 holds those of g.

J=(∂f∂x∂f∂y∂g∂x∂g∂y)

A partial derivative ∂f∂x is the derivative of f with respect to x, treating every other variable as a constant. You work out J with the variables left in, then put in the values at the fixed point.

Example. Take f=3x+xy and g=y2−x. Treating y as a constant, ∂f∂x=3+y. Treating x as a constant, ∂f∂y=x. In the same way, ∂g∂x=−1 and ∂g∂y=2y. So J=(3+yx−12y). At the fixed point (x,y)=(0,0), J=(30−10).

Eigenvalues. I is the identity matrix, which has 1 on the diagonal and 0 everywhere else. So J−λI is J with λ (the Greek letter lambda) subtracted from each diagonal entry. "det" means the determinant. For a 2×2 matrix (abcd) the determinant is ad−bc. The eigenvalues of J are the numbers λ that make det⁡(J−λI)=0.

Near the fixed point, small changes grow or shrink like eλt. If λ<0, eλt shrinks to 0 as t increases, and if λ>0 it grows. So the fixed point is stable if every eigenvalue is negative, and unstable if any eigenvalue is positive. (An eigenvalue can be a complex number u+iv. Then only its real part u counts in this test.)

The 2×2 shortcut. For J=(abcd), let T=a+d (the trace) and D=ad−bc (the determinant). The shortcut comes from these steps.

  1. J−λI=(a−λbcd−λ).
  2. Its determinant is (a−λ)(d−λ)−bc.
  3. Multiply out the brackets. ad−aλ−dλ+λ2−bc=λ2−(a+d)λ+(ad−bc)=λ2−Tλ+D.
  4. Set this to zero, λ2−Tλ+D=0, and solve it with the quadratic formula.
λ=T±T2−4D2,stable⟺T<0 and D>0.

The symbol ⟺ means "if and only if". The two eigenvalues add up to T and multiply to D. Two numbers are both negative exactly when their sum is negative and their product is positive, which gives the test. If D<0, the product is negative, so one eigenvalue is positive and the other is negative. That fixed point is called a saddle, and it is unstable.

A 3×3 matrix that splits. If the third row and the third column are zero except for the corner entry, that corner entry is one eigenvalue. The other two come from the top-left 2×2 block. This works because det⁡(J−λI) is then (corner entry −λ) times the determinant of (the 2×2 block −λI), and a product is zero when either factor is zero. Part 5 uses this.

0c · Maths tools

Parts 1 and 2 use the same three tools again and again.

Separating variables. To solve dkdt=k2t, put everything with k on one side and everything with t on the other, then integrate both sides.

dkk=dt2t⇒ln⁡k=12ln⁡t+c.

Here c is a constant that appears when you integrate. A known starting value fixes it. Instead of using c, you can integrate from the starting value, as in the question below. You will need ∫dxx=ln⁡x, ln⁡a−ln⁡b=ln⁡ab, and bln⁡a=ln⁡ab.

Joining times are spread evenly. In a network where one node is added at each step 1,2,…,t, every joining time occurs exactly once. Write ti for the time at which node i joined. The nodes that joined after time T are the ones from times T+1 to t, and there are t−T of them out of t. So the fraction of nodes that joined after time T is

fraction with ti>T=t−Tt=1−Tt.

From "fraction below k" to P(k). If F(k) is the fraction of nodes with degree below k, then the degree distribution is its derivative, P(k)=dFdk. The reason is that the fraction of nodes with degree between k and k+Δk is F(k+Δk)−F(k), which is about F′(k)Δk. So F′(k) is the fraction of nodes per unit of degree, which is what P(k) means when degrees are treated as smooth numbers.

Sine. Angles here are in radians, where π radians is 180°. sin⁡ϕ is always between −1 and 1, so sin⁡ϕ=a has a solution only if |a|≤1. (|a| is the size of a without its sign.) For 0≤a≤1, arcsin⁡a is the angle between 0 and π2 whose sine is a, and your calculator gives it. There are two solutions between 0 and π, namely arcsin⁡a and π−arcsin⁡a. The first is below π2, so it has cos⁡ϕ>0. The second is above π2, so it has cos⁡ϕ<0. Part 4 uses this.

1 · The Barabási–Albert (BA) model

About 2 hours. This part and the next are the largest share of the syllabus.

The BA model is a rule for building a network one node at a time. It has two ingredients.

  1. Growth. Start with a few nodes (m0 of them). At each time step t=1,2,3,…, add one new node with m links.
  2. Preferential attachment. Each of those m links is attached to an existing node i with probability
    Π(ki)=ki∑jkj,
    This formula is explained below.
Reading the formula

Π (capital pi) is just the name for this probability. It is not the number 3.14.

The top, ki, is the degree of node i, the node we are asking about.

The bottom, ∑jkj, means "add up the degree of every node in the network". The symbol ∑j says to let j run over all the nodes and add the terms. So ∑jkj=k1+k2+k3+…, the total degree.

So the probability is node i's share of the total degree. Dividing by the total makes the probabilities of all the nodes add up to 1, as they must, because the link has to attach to exactly one node.

Example. A network has 4 nodes with degrees 1, 2, 3 and 4. The total is ∑jkj=1+2+3+4=10. The probabilities are 110, 210, 310 and 410, which add up to 1010=1. The node of degree 4 is 4 times as likely to receive the link as the node of degree 1.

In general, a node with twice the degree is twice as likely to receive the link. This is why it is called preferential attachment. A node that already has many links is more likely to receive the next one.

In the BA model the total degree is easy to find. Every link adds 1 to the degree of each of its two ends, so each link adds 2 to the total. After t steps there are mt links, so ∑jkj=2mt and Π(ki)=ki2mt. Line 3 of 1A uses this.

After t steps there are about t nodes and mt links. Nodes that were added early have had more steps in which to receive links, and every link they receive makes the next one more likely. The result is a power-law degree distribution, P(k)∝k−3, so the network is scale-free with γ=3.

The board asks for three things. They are the rate dkidt, the distribution P(k), and the master equation, which is a second way to get P(k).

1A · The rate dkidt

Think of ki as a smooth number that grows with time. Its rate of change is the expected number of new links that node i receives in one step.

Write this in the exam

Show that dkidt=ki2t, and solve it.

  1. Each step adds m links. Each attaches to node i with probability Π(ki)=ki∑jkj.
  2. Node i gains mΠ(ki) links per step on average. Treating ki as continuous (the continuum approximation), dkidt=mΠ(ki)=mki∑jkj.
    Why lines 1 and 2 work (7 steps)
    1. Each step adds m new links.
    2. Each of these links attaches to node i with probability Π(ki)=ki∑jkj.
    3. So on average node i receives mΠ(ki) new links per step.
    4. One time step is Δt=1. In one step, ki changes by some amount Δki. From step 3, the average of Δki is mΠ(ki).
    5. So on average, the rate of change is ΔkiΔt=mΠ(ki)1=mΠ(ki).
    6. In reality ki goes up in whole-number jumps. But once the network is large, each jump is tiny compared with ki, and over many steps the jumps average out.
    7. So ki can be treated as a smooth curve, and ΔkiΔt becomes the derivative dkidt. This is called the continuum approximation. dkidt=mΠ(ki)=mki∑jkj.
  3. Each link adds 2 to the total degree, so ∑jkj=2mt.
  4. So dkidt=mki2mt=ki2t, which is proportional to kit.
    Why lines 3 and 4 work (4 steps)
    1. Find the total degree. After t steps there are mt links (ignoring the few starting links).
    2. Each link adds 1 to the degree of each of its two ends, so it adds 2 to the total. So ∑jkj=2mt.
    3. Substitute this into line 2. dkidt=mki2mt.
    4. Cancel m. dkidt=ki2t. This proves that the rate is proportional to kit.
  5. Separate the variables and integrate from ki(ti)=m. ∫mkidkk=12∫titdt′t′, so ln⁡kim=12ln⁡tti.
  6. So ki(t)=mt/ti.
    Why lines 5 and 6 work (7 steps)
    1. Separate the variables. Divide both sides by ki and multiply both sides by dt. dkiki=12dtt.
    2. Node i was added at time ti with m links, so ki(ti)=m. Integrate both sides from there. ∫mkidkk=12∫titdt′t′.
    3. Use ∫dxx=ln⁡x. ln⁡ki−ln⁡m=12(ln⁡t−ln⁡ti).
    4. Use ln⁡a−ln⁡b=ln⁡ab on both sides. ln⁡kim=12ln⁡tti.
    5. Use 12ln⁡y=ln⁡y. ln⁡kim=ln⁡t/ti.
    6. If ln⁡A=ln⁡B then A=B. So kim=t/ti.
    7. Multiply both sides by m. ki(t)=mt/ti. A node added earlier (smaller ti) has a larger degree.

1B · The degree distribution P(k) (continuum theory)

The degree of a node depends only on when it was added. So the question "what fraction of nodes have degree below k?" becomes "what fraction of nodes were added after a certain time?", and joining times are spread evenly.

Write this in the exam

Show that P(k)=2m2k−3.

  1. From 1A, ki=mt/ti, so ti=m2tki2.
    Why line 1 works (4 steps)
    1. From 1A, ki=mt/ti. Solve this for the joining time ti.
    2. Divide by m. kim=t/ti.
    3. Square both sides. ki2m2=tti.
    4. Rearrange. ti=m2tki2.
  2. A node has degree below k exactly when it joined after T=m2tk2.
    Why line 2 works (1 step)
    1. A node that joined later has a smaller degree. So a node has degree below k exactly when it joined after the time T=m2tk2.
  3. Joining times are spread evenly, so the fraction that joined after T is 1−Tt. So F(k)=1−m2k2.
    Why line 3 works (2 steps)
    1. Joining times are spread evenly over 1…t (Part 0c). So the fraction that joined after T is 1−Tt.
    2. Put in T. Tt=m2k2, so the fraction with degree below k is F(k)=1−m2k2. The t has cancelled, so this does not change as the network grows.
  4. P(k)=dFdk=2m2k3, a power law with γ=3.
    Why line 4 works (3 steps)
    1. Write F(k)=1−m2k−2 and differentiate. The derivative of k−2 is −2k−3.
    2. So P(k)=dFdk=−m2×(−2k−3)=2m2k3.
    3. This is a power law with γ=3, whatever the value of m.

1C · The master equation

Why "master equation". It is one equation that accounts for every way the number of degree-k nodes can change in one step. Some are gained from degree k−1 and some are lost to degree k+1. Solving it gives the whole distribution P(k) at once. In physics, any equation of this gain-and-loss form for a probability distribution is called a master equation, and the name was carried over to networks.

This method gives P(k) by counting. Let N(k,t) be the number of nodes with degree k at time t. There are t nodes at time t, so the fraction is P(k,t)=N(k,t)t. In one step, a node of degree k−1 that receives a link moves up to degree k. A node of degree k that receives a link moves up to degree k+1. The new node adds one node of degree m.

Write this in the exam

Write the master equation for the BA model and solve it for P(k).

  1. A degree-k node receives a new link with probability m⋅k2mt=k2t. With N(k,t)=tP(k,t), the expected number of degree-k nodes that receive a link in one step is k2P(k,t).
    Why line 1 works (4 steps)
    1. In one step there are m new links.
    2. Each one attaches to a particular degree-k node with probability k2mt (1A, lines 1 and 3).
    3. There are N(k,t) degree-k nodes. So the expected (average) number of degree-k nodes that receive a link in one step is m×N(k,t)×k2mt.
    4. Cancel m. This is kN(k,t)2t. Since N(k,t)=tP(k,t), it equals k2P(k,t).
  2. For k>m,N(k,t+1)−N(k,t)=k−12P(k−1,t)−k2P(k,t).N(k,t+1)−N(k,t)=k−12P(k−1,t)−k2P(k,t).
    Why line 2 works (2 steps)
    1. For k>m, the number of degree-k nodes goes up by one for each degree-(k−1) node that receives a link.
    2. It goes down by one for each degree-k node that receives a link. Using line 1 for both, N(k,t+1)−N(k,t)=k−12P(k−1,t)−k2P(k,t).N(k,t+1)−N(k,t)=k−12P(k−1,t)−k2P(k,t).
  3. For k=m,N(m,t+1)=N(m,t)+1−m2P(m,t).
    Why line 3 works (1 step)
    1. For k=m, no node can move up into degree m, because every node starts with m links. The new node adds one node of degree m. N(m,t+1)=N(m,t)+1−m2P(m,t).
  4. In the steady state P(k,t)=P(k), so N(k,t+1)−N(k,t)=P(k).
    Why line 4 works (2 steps)
    1. Look for a steady state, where P(k,t)=P(k) does not depend on t. Then N(k,t)=tP(k) and N(k,t+1)=(t+1)P(k).
    2. Subtract. N(k,t+1)−N(k,t)=(t+1)P(k)−tP(k)=P(k).
  5. At k=m, P(m)=1−m2P(m), so P(m)=2m+2.
    Why line 5 works (2 steps)
    1. Case k=m. Subtract N(m,t) from both sides of line 3 and use line 4. P(m)=1−m2P(m).
    2. Add m2P(m) to both sides. m+22P(m)=1, so P(m)=2m+2.
  6. For k>m, P(k)=k−12P(k−1)−k2P(k), so P(k)=k−1k+2P(k−1).
    Why line 6 works (3 steps)
    1. Case k>m. Do the same with line 2. P(k)=k−12P(k−1)−k2P(k).
    2. Add k2P(k) to both sides. k+22P(k)=k−12P(k−1).
    3. Multiply by 2 and divide by k+2. P(k)=k−1k+2P(k−1).
  7. Apply this repeatedly from P(m) and cancel. P(k)=2m(m+1)k(k+1)(k+2).
    Why line 7 works (5 steps)
    1. Apply this again and again, starting from P(m). P(m+1)=mm+3P(m), then P(m+2)=m+1m+4P(m+1), and so on up to P(k)=k−1k+2P(k−1).

Remember

Ingredients are growth and preferential attachment, Π(ki)=ki∑jkj. The total degree is ∑jkj=2mt.

dkidt=ki2t, so ki=mt/ti. The fraction with degree below k is 1−m2k2, so P(k)=2m2k−3.

2 · Changing the BA model

About 1 hour. The board lists "no growth", "no preferential attachment" and "nonlinear preferential attachment".

Each case changes one rule of the BA model and asks what degree distribution results. The method is the same as in Part 1. Write dkidt using the new rule, integrate from the node's starting degree, and, where needed, get P(k) from the evenly spread joining times.

2A · Growth without preferential attachment (Model A)

Nodes are still added one per step with m links each. Each link now attaches to an existing node chosen uniformly at random, so the degree of a node does not affect its chance of receiving a link.

Write this in the exam

Find ki(t) and P(k) for Model A. Is it scale-free?

  1. There are about t existing nodes, each equally likely, so Π=1t.
    Why line 1 works (2 steps)
    1. There are m0 starting nodes plus one per step, so there are about t existing nodes.
    2. Each is equally likely, so Π(ki)=1t.
  2. As in 1A, dkidt=mΠ=mt.
    Why line 2 works (1 step)
    1. As in 1A, line 2, the rate is the average number of new links per step. dkidt=mΠ=mt. This does not depend on ki.
  3. Integrate from ki(ti)=m. ki(t)=m+mln⁡tti.
    Why line 3 works (3 steps)
    1. Separate the variables. dki=mdtt.
    2. Integrate from ki(ti)=m. ki−m=m(ln⁡t−ln⁡ti)=mln⁡tti.
    3. Add m to both sides. ki(t)=m+mln⁡tti.
  4. ki<k exactly when ti>te1−k/m. Joining times are spread evenly, so F(k)=1−e1−k/m.
    Why line 4 works (5 steps)
    1. Node i has degree below k when m+mln⁡tti<k.
    2. Subtract m and divide by m. ln⁡tti<km−1.
    3. Take e to the power of both sides. tti<ek/m−1.
    4. Rearrange. ti>tek/m−1=te1−k/m.
    5. Joining times are spread evenly, so the fraction that joined after T=te1−k/m is 1−Tt. So F(k)=1−e1−k/m.
  5. P(k)=dFdk=eme−k/m. This is exponential, so there are no hubs and the network is not scale-free.
    Why line 5 works (3 steps)
    1. Differentiate. By the chain rule, the derivative of e1−k/m is e1−k/m×(−1m).
    2. So P(k)=dFdk=1me1−k/m=eme−k/m for k≥m.
    3. This is exponential, not a power law. Large degrees are extremely rare, so there are no hubs and the network is not scale-free.

2B · Preferential attachment without growth (Model B)

Now the number of nodes is fixed at N and no nodes are added. At each step, one node is chosen uniformly at random, and a new link is added from it to a node chosen by preferential attachment.

Write this in the exam

Find ki(t) for Model B and describe the degree distribution.

  1. Each step adds one link and no node, so ∑jkj=2t.
    Why line 1 works (2 steps)
    1. Each step adds one link and no node. After t steps there are t links.
    2. Each link adds 2 to the total degree, so ∑jkj=2t.
  2. Node i is the random end with probability 1N or the preferential end with probability ki2t. So dkidt=1N+ki2t.
    Why line 2 works (3 steps)
    1. Node i is the randomly chosen end of the new link with probability 1N.
    2. It is the preferentially chosen end with probability ki∑jkj=ki2t.
    3. The average number of new links per step is the sum of these, and as in 1A, line 2, that is the rate. dkidt=1N+ki2t.
  3. The solution is ki(t)=2tN+Cit. (Substitute it to check.)
    Why line 3 works (6 steps)
    1. Look for a solution that grows in proportion to t. Put ki=at, so dkidt=a.
    2. The equation becomes a=1N+at2t=1N+a2.
    3. Subtract a2. a2=1N, so a=2N. So ki=2tN is one solution.
    4. Now try ki=2tN+Cit, where Ci is a constant. The left side is dkidt=2N+Ci2t.
    5. The right side is 1N+12t(2tN+Cit)=1N+1N+Ci2t.
    6. The two sides are equal, so this solves the equation for any Ci. The value of Ci depends on the node’s starting degree.
  4. For large t the 2tN part is much larger, so every degree is close to 2tN. P(k) is a narrow peak, not a power law.
    Why line 4 works (2 steps)
    1. The 2tN part is the same for every node and grows like t. The Cit part grows only like t.
    2. So for large t every degree is close to 2tN. The distribution is a narrow peak around the average, not a power law.
  5. After about N22 steps every pair is linked, so the network becomes complete.
    Why line 5 works (1 step)
    1. There are only N(N−1)2 possible links. After about N22 steps every pair of nodes is linked, so the network is complete and never settles to a steady state.

2C · Nonlinear preferential attachment

Nodes are added as in BA, but the attachment probability uses a power α of the degree.

Π(ki)=kiα∑jkjα,α≥0.

α is the Greek letter alpha. α=0 gives Model A, because k0=1 for every node. α=1 gives the BA model. The table is what to learn. In the last column, ∼ means "grows like", ignoring constant factors.

Write this in the exam

Explain what happens for α<1 and for α>1.

  1. For α<1, ∑jkjα≈νt, so dkidt=mkiανt.
    Why line 1 works (2 steps)
    1. For α<1, degrees stay small (line 3 checks this). So the values kjα have some fixed average, called ν (the Greek letter nu).
    2. With about t nodes, ∑jkjα≈νt. So dkidt=mΠ(ki)=mkiανt.
  2. Integrating gives ki1−α=m1−α+(1−α)mνln⁡tti.
    Why line 2 works (3 steps)
    1. Separate the variables. ki−αdki=mνdtt.
    2. Integrate from ki(ti)=m, using ∫k−αdk=k1−α1−α. ki1−α−m1−α1−α=mνln⁡tti.
    3. Multiply by 1−α and add m1−α. ki1−α=m1−α+(1−α)mνln⁡tti.
  3. So kmax⁡∼(ln⁡t)1/(1−α), which grows more slowly than any power of t. There are no hubs and no power law.
    Why line 3 works (3 steps)
    1. The largest degree belongs to the oldest node, ti=1. For large t the ln⁡t term is much bigger than m1−α, so kmax⁡1−α≈(1−α)mνln⁡t.
    2. Raise both sides to the power 11−α. kmax⁡∼(ln⁡t)1/(1−α).
    3. This grows more slowly than any power of t. So degrees do stay small, and there are no hubs and no power law.
  4. For α>1, if one node has k1=ct, then Π(k1)=cαtαcαtα+bt→1.
    Why line 4 works (4 steps)
    1. For α>1, suppose one node has degree k1=ct for some constant c. Its term in the sum is k1α=cαtα.
    2. The other nodes (about t of them) have small degrees, so their terms add up to about bt for some constant b.
    3. Π(k1)=cαtαcαtα+bt. Divide the top and bottom by cαtα. Π(k1)=11+bcαtα−1.
    4. Since α>1, tα−1 grows without limit. So bcαtα−1 goes to 0, and Π(k1) goes to 1.
  5. So that node receives almost every new link, and ends up with a finite fraction of all the links.
    Why line 5 works (2 steps)
    1. So almost every new link attaches to node 1. It gains about m links per step, so k1≈mt, which agrees with the starting assumption.
    2. One node ends up with a finite fraction of all the links, and every other node keeps a small degree.
Remember

Both ingredients are needed for a power law. Growth alone (Model A) gives ki=m+mln⁡tti and an exponential P(k)=eme−k/m. Preferential attachment alone (Model B) gives ki≈2tN for every node, and eventually a complete graph.

Nonlinear: α<1 gives no hubs, α=1 gives BA, and α>1 gives one node with most of the links.

3 · Small-world graphs

About 90 minutes. The board asks for the average clustering coefficient and the path length at p=0 and p=1, and the rewiring probability. Part 0a defines clustering and path length.

The Watts–Strogatz model starts from a ring lattice. Place n nodes in a circle and link each node to its k nearest nodes on each side, so every node has degree 2k. Then go through the links one at a time and, with probability p, move one end of the link to a node chosen at random. This is called rewiring, and p is the rewiring probability.

  • At p=0 nothing is rewired, and the graph is the ring lattice.
  • At p=1 every link is rewired, and the graph is a random graph with the same average degree 2k.

The ring lattice has high clustering and long paths. The random graph has low clustering and short paths. For small p, a few rewired links connect distant parts of the ring, so paths become short, while almost all triangles remain, so clustering stays high. A graph with both properties is called a small world.

Watch the letter k

On this page k is the number of neighbours on each side, so the degree is 2k. If a question gives the degree as K, use k=K/2.

3A · Clustering of the ring lattice, C(0)

Write this in the exam

Show that C(0)=3(k−1)2(2k−1).

  1. A node has 2k neighbours, so there are k(2k−1) pairs of neighbours.
    Why line 1 works (1 step)
    1. Each node has 2k neighbours. The number of pairs of neighbours is 2k(2k−1)2=k(2k−1).
  2. Two neighbours are linked when they are at most k apart. Pairs on the same side are all linked, which gives 2×k(k−1)2=k(k−1).
    Why line 2 works (3 steps)
    1. Two neighbours are linked when they are at most k positions apart on the ring.
    2. Pairs on the same side. The right-hand neighbours are at positions 1,2,…,k. Any two of them are at most k−1 apart, so every such pair is linked.
    3. That side has k(k−1)2 pairs, and the left side has the same number. Together this gives k(k−1) linked pairs.
  3. Pairs on opposite sides, at −a and +b, are linked when a+b≤k. There are (k−1)+(k−2)+⋯+1=k(k−1)2 of them.
    Why line 3 works (3 steps)
    1. Pairs on opposite sides. Take positions −a and +b, with a and b between 1 and k. They are a+b apart, so they are linked when a+b≤k.
    2. Count these. For a=1, b can be 1…k−1, which is k−1 choices. For a=2 there are k−2, and so on down to 0 for a=k.
    3. The total is 1+2+⋯+(k−1)=k(k−1)2.
  4. So there are 32k(k−1) linked pairs, and C(0)=32k(k−1)k(2k−1)=3(k−1)2(2k−1)→34.
    Why line 4 works (4 steps)
    1. Linked pairs in total. k(k−1)+k(k−1)2=32k(k−1).
    2. Divide by the number of pairs. C(0)=32k(k−1)k(2k−1).
    3. Cancel k. C(0)=3(k−1)2(2k−1).
    4. For large k, k−1≈k and 2k−1≈2k, so C(0)≈3k4k=34. It does not depend on n.

3B · Path length of the ring lattice, ℓ‾(0)

Write this in the exam

Show that ℓ‾(0)≈n4k.

  1. There are 2k nodes at each distance ℓ=1,2,…,D, with D=n2k.
    Why line 1 works (3 steps)
    1. From any node, the nodes 1 to k positions away on either side are linked to it, so they are at distance 1.
    2. The nodes k+1 to 2k positions away are at distance 2, and so on. So there are 2k nodes at each distance ℓ=1,2,…
    3. The farthest node is halfway round the ring, n2 positions away. Each step covers up to k positions, so the largest distance is D=n2k.
  2. The sum of distances from one node is ∑ℓ=1D2kℓ≈kD2=n24k.
    Why line 2 works (3 steps)
    1. Add up the distances from one node to all the others. ∑ℓ=1D2kℓ=2k(1+2+⋯+D).
    2. Use 1+2+⋯+D=D(D+1)2≈D22 for large D. The sum is about 2k⋅D22=kD2.
    3. Put in D=n2k. kD2=k⋅n24k2=n24k.
  3. Divide by the n other nodes. ℓ‾(0)≈n4k, which grows in proportion to n.
    Why line 3 works (2 steps)
    1. Divide by the number of other nodes, n−1≈n, to get the average. ℓ‾(0)≈n24k⋅1n=n4k.
    2. Every node gives the same value, so this is the average over all pairs. It grows in proportion to n, so a large ring lattice has long paths.

3C · The random graph at p=1

Write this in the exam

  1. Each pair is linked independently with probability q≈2kn, so C=q≈2kn→0.
    Why line 1 works (4 steps)
    1. Each node has 2k links, and each goes to one of the other n−1 nodes.
    2. So the probability that a particular pair of nodes is linked is q=2kn−1≈2kn.
    3. In a random graph the links are placed independently. So two neighbours of a node are linked with the same probability q as any other pair.
    4. So C=q≈2kn, which goes to 0 for large n.
  2. About (2k)ℓ nodes are at distance ℓ. Setting (2k)ℓ=n gives ℓ‾≈ln⁡nln⁡2k, which is short.
    Why line 2 works (4 steps)
    1. From a node there are about 2k nodes at distance 1. Each of those has about 2k neighbours, so there are about (2k)2 nodes at distance 2.
    2. Continuing in the same way, there are about (2k)ℓ nodes at distance ℓ.
    3. All n nodes are reached when (2k)ℓ≈n. Take ln⁡ of both sides. ℓln⁡(2k)=ln⁡n, so ℓ‾≈ln⁡nln⁡2k.

3D · Clustering for 0<p<1

Write this in the exam

    Why line 1 works (2 steps)
    1. In the ring lattice, each linked pair of neighbours of node i forms a triangle with i. So clustering counts triangles.
    2. A triangle is made of three links.
  2. A triangle survives only if none of its three links is rewired, which has probability (1−p)3.

    1. Each link is left alone with probability 1−p, independently of the others.
    2. A triangle remains only if all three of its links are left alone. That has probability (1−p)×(1−p)×(1−p)=(1−p)3.

4 · Two coupled phase oscillators

About 45 minutes. The board writes θ˙1=f1(ω1,θ1,θ2,k1) and θ˙2=f2(ω2,θ1,θ2,k2) and asks for the critical condition.

An oscillator here is anything that repeats a cycle, such as a pendulum or a flashing light. Its state is an angle θ, called the phase, which increases by 2π every cycle. On its own, the phase increases at a constant rate, θ˙=ω. The constant ω is the natural frequency.

Two oscillators are coupled when the rate of each phase depends on the other phase.

θ˙1=ω1+k1sin⁡(θ2−θ1),θ˙2=ω2+k2sin⁡(θ1−θ2).

k1 and k2 are the coupling strengths. If θ2 is slightly ahead of θ1, the term k1sin⁡(θ2−θ1) is positive, so θ1 increases faster, and the term in the second equation is negative, so θ2 increases more slowly. The coupling therefore reduces the difference between the phases.

The oscillators are phase-locked (synchronised) when the phase difference ϕ=θ2−θ1 stays constant. Then both phases increase at the same rate Ω, the common frequency. The question is how large the coupling must be for this to happen.

Write this in the exam

Find the condition for the two oscillators to lock, and the common frequency Ω.

  1. Let ϕ=θ2−θ1 and Δω=ω2−ω1. Subtracting the equations and using sin⁡(−ϕ)=−sin⁡ϕ gives ϕ˙=Δω−(k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ.
    Why line 1 works (3 steps)
    1. Let ϕ=θ2−θ1 and Δω=ω2−ω1.
    2. Subtract the first equation from the second. ϕ˙=θ˙2−θ˙1. Put in the two equations. ϕ˙=Δω+k2sin⁡(θ1−θ2)−k1sin⁡(θ2−θ1).ϕ˙=Δω+k2sin⁡(θ1−θ2)−k1sin⁡(θ2−θ1).
    3. Now sin⁡(θ2−θ1)=sin⁡ϕ and sin⁡(θ1−θ2)=sin⁡(−ϕ)=−sin⁡ϕ. So ϕ˙=Δω−(k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ.
  2. Locked means ϕ˙=0, so sin⁡ϕ∗=Δωk1+k2.
    Why line 2 works (1 step)
    1. Locked means ϕ is constant, so ϕ˙=0. Then (k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ∗=Δω, so sin⁡ϕ∗=Δωk1+k2.
  3. Since |sin⁡ϕ∗|≤1, they lock if and only if |Δω|≤k1+k2. With k1=k2=k, kc=|Δω|2.
    Why line 3 works (2 steps)
    1. A sine is never more than 1 in size. So a solution exists only if |Δωk1+k2|≤1, that is, |Δω|≤k1+k2. This is the critical condition.
    2. With equal couplings k1=k2=k, the condition reads |Δω|≤2k. So the critical coupling is kc=|Δω|2.
  4. The stable solution has cos⁡ϕ∗>0, because the slope −(k1+k2)cos⁡ϕ∗ must be negative.
    Why line 4 works (3 steps)
    1. There are two solutions, arcsin⁡Δωk1+k2 and π minus that (Part 0c).
    2. To see which is stable, treat ϕ˙=g(ϕ) like x˙=f(x) in Part 0b. The slope is g′(ϕ)=−(k1+k2)cos⁡ϕ.
    3. Stable needs g′(ϕ∗)<0, which means cos⁡ϕ∗>0. So the stable one is ϕ∗=arcsin⁡Δωk1+k2, the one below π2.
  5. When locked, Ω=ω1+k1sin⁡ϕ∗=ω2−k2sin⁡ϕ∗. Eliminating sin⁡ϕ∗ gives Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2.
    Why line 5 works (3 steps)
    1. In the locked state both phases increase at the same rate Ω. Put θ2−θ1=ϕ∗ into each equation. Ω=ω1+k1sin⁡ϕ∗ and Ω=ω2−k2sin⁡ϕ∗.
    2. Multiply the first by k2 and the second by k1. k2Ω=k2ω1+k1k2sin⁡ϕ∗ and k1Ω=k1ω2−k1k2sin⁡ϕ∗.
    3. Add them. The sin⁡ϕ∗ terms cancel. (k1+k2)Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2, so Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2.
  6. If |Δω|>k1+k2, ϕ˙ is never zero, so they do not lock.
    Why line 6 works (1 step)
    1. If the condition fails, ϕ˙ is never zero. The phase difference keeps changing, so the oscillators do not lock.
Remember

ϕ˙=Δω−(k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ. Lock if and only if |Δω|≤k1+k2. Equal couplings give kc=|Δω|2.

sin⁡ϕ∗=Δωk1+k2, taking the root with cos⁡ϕ∗>0. Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2, which is the plain average when k1=k2.

5 · The Lorenz system

About 45 minutes. This uses the fixed points, Jacobian and eigenvalues from Part 0b.

The Lorenz system has three variables x, y and z that change together. The constants σ, ρ and β are all positive.

x˙=σ(y−x),y˙=ρx−y−xz,z˙=xy−βz.

The question asks for the fixed points and the Jacobian at each one. Solve the simplest equations first.

Write this in the exam

Find the fixed points of the Lorenz system, the Jacobian at each, and show that the origin is stable if and only if ρ<1.

  1. Fixed points have σ(y−x)=0, ρx−y−xz=0 and xy−βz=0.
    Why line 1 works (1 step)
    1. At a fixed point all three rates are zero. (i) σ(y−x)=0. (ii) ρx−y−xz=0. (iii) xy−βz=0.
  2. The first gives y=x. The third then gives z=x2β. The second gives x(ρ−1−z)=0.
    Why line 2 works (4 steps)
    1. From (i), since σ>0, y−x=0, so y=x.
    2. Put y=x into (iii). x2−βz=0, so z=x2β.
    3. Put y=x into (ii). ρx−x−xz=0.
    4. Take out the common factor x. x(ρ−1−z)=0. So either x=0 or z=ρ−1.
  3. So P0=(0,0,0), and for ρ>1, P±=(±c,±c,ρ−1) with c=β(ρ−1).

    2. Row 1, from σy−σx, is −σ, σ, 0.
    3. Row 2, from ρx−y−xz, is ρ−z, −1, −x.

In the exam hall

  1. Before you answer, read each question and match it to a part of this page. "Find dkidt" is 1A. "Master equation" is 1C. "No growth" is 2B. "p=0 and p=1" is 3A to 3C. "Critical condition" is 4.
  2. Start each answer by writing the model or the definition, for example the two BA ingredients or the two oscillator equations. This usually earns the first mark.
  3. Write every step of the derivation, with a few words saying why each step follows. A final formula on its own may get 0 marks.
  4. For a numbers question, write the formula, say where it comes from in one line, then put the numbers in. That is the layout of every answer on this page.
  5. Check that the answer makes sense. A probability must be between 0 and 1. sin⁡ϕ cannot exceed 1. Ω must lie between ω1 and ω2.
