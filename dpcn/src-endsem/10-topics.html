Formulas to remember

Know these by heart. Under each one, "where" says what every letter means. Every result is proved further down.

1 · Phase synchronisation

  • The model\(\dot\theta_1 = \omega_1 + k_1\sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\), \(\dot\theta_2 = \omega_2 + k_2\sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\)where \(\theta_{1,2}\) are the phases (angles) of the two oscillators. Each \(\omega\) is a natural frequency, how fast that oscillator turns on its own, and each \(k\) says how strongly it is pulled towards the other.
  • Phase difference\(\dot\phi = \Delta\omega - (k_1 + k_2)\sin\phi\)where \(\phi = \theta_2 - \theta_1\) and \(\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1\).
  • Critical conditionLock iff \(|\Delta\omega|\le k_1 + k_2\). Equal couplings \(k\): \(k_c = \frac{|\Delta\omega|}2\)where locking means \(\phi\) stops changing, so both turn at one common frequency.
  • Common frequency\(\Omega = \frac{k_2\omega_1 + k_1\omega_2}{k_1 + k_2}\)a weighted average of the two natural frequencies. With \(k_1 = k_2\) it is the plain average.

2 · The Lorenz system

  • The model\(\dot x = \sigma(y - x)\), \(\dot y = \rho x - y - xz\), \(\dot z = xy - \beta z\)where all the constants are positive.
  • Fixed points\((0,0,0)\), and for \(\rho>1\): \(\big(\pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho-1)},\ \pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho-1)},\ \rho - 1\big)\)where the two signs are taken together.
  • Jacobian\(J = \begin{pmatrix}-\sigma & \sigma & 0\\ \rho - z & -1 & -x\\ y & x & -\beta\end{pmatrix}\)where row \(i\) holds the slopes of the \(i\)-th rate, taken with respect to each variable in turn. Put each fixed point into it.
  • OriginStable iff \(\rho<1\)because \(J(0)\) splits into \(-\beta\) and a 2×2 block with trace \(-(\sigma + 1)<0\) and determinant \(\sigma(1 - \rho)\).

3 · Scale-free networks

  • BA modelGrowth + preferential attachment \(\Pi(k_i) = \frac{k_i}{\sum_jk_j}\)where each step adds one node with \(m\) links, and \(k_i\) is the degree of node \(i\).
  • Degree of one node\(\frac{dk_i}{dt} = \frac{k_i}{2t}\), \(k_i(t) = m\big(\frac t{t_i}\big)^{1/2}\)where \(t\) is the time now (= number of nodes added) and \(t_i\) the time node \(i\) joined, with \(k_i(t_i) = m\).
  • Degree distribution\(P(k) = 2m^2k^{-3}\), or exactly \(\frac{2m(m+1)}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\) from the master equationwhere \(P(k)\) is the fraction of nodes with degree \(k\). Exponent \(\gamma = 3\) for every \(m\), and it does not change as the network grows.
  • Master equation\((N+1)P(k,t+1) = NP(k,t) + \frac{k-1}2P(k-1,t) - \frac k2P(k,t)\)for \(k>m\). The equation at \(k = m\) is \((N+1)P(m,t+1) = NP(m,t) + 1 - \frac m2P(m,t)\). Here \(P(k,t)\) is the fraction of degree-\(k\) nodes at time \(t\), and \(N = t\).
  • No preferential attachment\(k_i = m + m\ln\frac t{t_i}\), \(P(k) = \frac em e^{-k/m}\)Model A keeps growth, but each new link picks a node uniformly. The result is an exponential, so there are no hubs.
  • No growth\(k_i(t)\approx\frac{2t}N\) for every nodeModel B fixes \(N\) and adds one link per step. All degrees become about equal, and after about \(N^2/2\) steps the graph is complete.
  • Nonlinear attachment\(\Pi(k_i) = \frac{k_i^\alpha}{\sum_jk_j^\alpha}\). Below \(\alpha = 1\) hubs stay small, and above it one hub takes almost every linkwhere \(\alpha\ge0\), and \(\alpha = 1\) is the BA model. The largest hub grows like \((\ln t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\) below 1 and like \(t\) above 1, against \(t^{1/2}\) for BA.

4 · Small-world graphs

  • Clustering\(C_i = \frac{\text{links among the neighbours of } i}{\binom{k_i}2}\), \(C = \frac1n\sum_iC_i\)where \(\binom{k_i}2 = \frac{k_i(k_i-1)}2\) is the number of pairs of neighbours.
  • Path length\(\bar\ell = \frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{u\ne v}\ell(u,v)\)where \(\ell(u,v)\) is the number of steps on a shortest path from \(u\) to \(v\).
  • \(p = 0\), ring lattice\(C(0) = \frac{3(k-1)}{2(2k-1)}\to\frac34\), \(\bar\ell(0)\approx\frac n{4k}\)where each of the \(n\) nodes links to \(k\) neighbours on each side, so the degree is \(2k\).
  • \(p = 1\), random graph\(C\approx\frac{2k}n\), \(\bar\ell\approx\frac{\ln n}{\ln2k}\)same \(n\) and the same degree \(2k\).
  • In between\(C(p) = C(0)(1-p)^3\)where \(p\) is the rewiring probability. For small \(p\), \(\bar\ell\) is already near the random value while \(C\) is still near \(C(0)\). That range of \(p\) is the small world.

1 · Phase synchronisation

Two oscillators each turn at their own speed. Coupling pulls each towards the other. If the pull is strong enough compared with the difference in speeds, they lock and turn together. The board's \(f_1(\omega_1, \theta_1, \theta_2, k_1)\) is \(\omega_1 + k_1\sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\).

In plain words

Each oscillator has a phase \(\theta\), an angle that increases at its natural frequency \(\omega\) when the oscillator is on its own. The coupling term \(k_1\sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\) is positive when \(\theta_2\) is slightly ahead of \(\theta_1\), so \(\theta_1\) then increases faster. The term in the second equation has the opposite sign, so \(\theta_2\) increases more slowly. Together the two terms reduce the phase difference \(\phi = \theta_2 - \theta_1\).

The oscillators lock when \(\phi\) stays constant, so you set \(\dot\phi = 0\). The coupling terms are \(\sin\phi\) times the coupling strengths, and \(|\sin\phi|\le1\). So the coupling can cancel a difference in natural frequency of at most \(k_1 + k_2\), and the oscillators lock only when \(|\omega_2 - \omega_1|\le k_1 + k_2\).

The common frequency \(\Omega\) lies between \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\). It is closer to the natural frequency of the oscillator with the smaller coupling strength.

Recipe. Subtract the two equations to get \(\dot\phi\). Set \(\dot\phi = 0\) and use \(|\sin\phi|\le1\). Then put \(\sin\phi^*\) back into either equation to get \(\Omega\).

Numbers

Take \(\omega_1 = 2\) and \(\omega_2 = 10\). With \(k_1 = k_2 = 2\) the coupling adds up to only 4, which is less than the gap of 8, so they never lock. They lock once \(k_1 + k_2\ge8\), so with equal couplings the critical value is \(k_c = 4\). At \(k_1 = k_2 = 5\) the locked state has \(\sin\phi^* = \frac8{10}\), so \(\phi^* = 0.93\) rad (the stable root, since \(\cos\phi^*>0\)), and the common frequency is \(\Omega = \frac{5\cdot2 + 5\cdot10}{10} = 6\).

2 · The Lorenz system

There are three equations in three unknowns. Solve for the fixed points from the easy equations first, then write one general Jacobian and put each point into it.

In plain words

Three numbers \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) change together. A fixed point is where all three rates are zero at once, so nothing moves. Solve the easy equations first. The first one, \(\sigma(y - x) = 0\), forces \(y = x\). Put that into the third to get \(z = x^2/\beta\). The second then becomes \(x(\rho - 1 - z) = 0\). Either \(x = 0\), which is the origin, or \(z = \rho - 1\), which gives \(x^2 = \beta(\rho - 1)\). That only has a solution when \(\rho>1\), so the two extra points appear when \(\rho\) passes 1.

The Jacobian is the table of slopes, with one row per equation and one column per variable. Write it once with \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) left in, then put each fixed point into it. At the origin it splits into the number \(-\beta\), which is always stable, and a 2×2 block. That block has a negative trace, so it is stable exactly when its determinant \(\sigma(1 - \rho)\) is positive, which means \(\rho<1\).

3 · Scale-free networks and the Barabási–Albert model

Real networks grow, and new nodes prefer well-connected nodes. Those two ingredients give a power law with exponent 3. The board asks for the rate \(\frac{dk_i}{dt}\) and the distribution \(P(k)\), with the master equation as a second way to get \(P(k)\). It then asks what happens when you remove an ingredient or change one.

In plain words

The network grows one node at a time. Each new node brings \(m\) links and attaches them to nodes already there. A link is more likely to attach to a node of high degree. The probability that a link attaches to node \(i\) is \(k_i\) divided by the total degree.

How fast one node grows. Each step adds \(m\) links, and each link adds 2 to the total degree, so after \(t\) steps the total is \(2mt\). Node \(i\) receives each new link with probability \(\frac{k_i}{2mt}\), and \(m\) links arrive per step, so \(\frac{dk_i}{dt} = \frac{k_i}{2t}\). Node \(i\) had degree \(m\) when it joined at time \(t_i\). Separating and integrating gives \(k_i = m\sqrt{t/t_i}\), so older nodes are bigger.

The degree distribution. A node has degree below \(k\) exactly when it joined late, after \(t_i = m^2t/k^2\). Nodes join at evenly spread times, so the fraction with degree below \(k\) is \(1 - \frac{m^2}{k^2}\). Differentiating with respect to \(k\) gives \(P(k) = \frac{2m^2}{k^3}\).

The master equation gets the same answer by counting. Instead of following one node, count how many nodes have degree \(k\). In one step, some degree-\((k-1)\) nodes get a link and become degree \(k\). Some degree-\(k\) nodes get a link and move up to \(k+1\). The new node adds one node of degree \(m\). When the fractions stop changing, what flows in equals what flows out. That gives \(\frac{P(k)}{P(k-1)} = \frac{k-1}{k+2}\), and multiplying these ratios together gives \(P(k)\sim k^{-3}\).

The After mid-sem guide, §19–21, has longer versions.

In plain words

Each variant takes the BA model and changes one rule. The method stays the same. Write \(\frac{dk_i}{dt} = m\,\Pi\) with the new \(\Pi\), integrate from \(k_i(t_i) = m\), and see what kind of growth you get.

No preference (3a). Every old node is equally likely, so \(\Pi = \frac1t\). Big nodes no longer grow faster, the degree grows only like a logarithm, and \(P(k)\) falls off exponentially. There are no hubs.

No growth (3b). The number of nodes is fixed and links keep being added. Every node's degree rises like \(\frac{2t}N\), the same for all, so everyone ends up alike and the network fills in until every pair is linked.

Nonlinear (3c). With \(\Pi\propto k^\alpha\), the power \(\alpha\) sets how strongly the degree of a node raises its chance of receiving a link. Below 1 the effect is too weak for hubs to form. At 1 you get BA. Above 1 the effect is so strong that one node receives almost every new link.

3a · Growth without preferential attachment (Model A)

Keep growth, but let each new link pick an existing node uniformly at random, whatever its degree. There are \(m_0 + t - 1\approx t\) nodes to choose from, so

\[\Pi(k_i) = \frac{1}{m_0 + t - 1}\approx\frac1t,\qquad\frac{dk_i}{dt} = m\,\Pi = \frac mt.\]

The rate no longer depends on \(k_i\), so a big node gains links no faster than a small one. Integrating from \(k_i(t_i) = m\) gives a logarithm instead of a square root, \(k_i(t) = m + m\ln(t/t_i)\). Old nodes still have more links, but only logarithmically more. The same "late joiners have small degree" argument as for BA then gives an exponential:

\[P(k) = \frac em\,e^{-k/m}\qquad(k\ge m)\]

It adds up to 1, since \(\int_m^\infty\frac1me^{1 - k/m}dk = e\cdot e^{-1} = 1\).

3b · Preferential attachment without growth (Model B)

Now keep preferential attachment but fix the number of nodes at \(N\). Nothing is ever added except links. At each step one node chosen at random gets a new link, and preferential attachment chooses the other end. After \(t\) steps there are \(t\) links, so \(\sum_jk_j = 2t\), and node \(i\) gains a link either by being the random end (probability \(\frac1N\)) or by being picked (probability \(\frac{k_i}{2t}\)):

\[\frac{dk_i}{dt} = \frac1N + \frac{k_i}{2t}.\]

Trying \(k_i = at\) gives \(a = \frac1N + \frac a2\), so \(a = \frac2N\). Without the \(\frac1N\) the equation is the BA one, which \(\sqrt t\) solves. So the full solution is

\[k_i(t) = \frac{2t}N + C_i\sqrt t\;\approx\;\frac{2t}{N}\ \text{ for large } t.\]

The part that differs between nodes, \(C_i\sqrt t\), becomes negligible next to \(\frac{2t}N\), which is the same for everyone. So all degrees end up about equal, and the distribution is a narrow peak around the average instead of a power law. Since there are only \(\frac{N(N-1)}2\) possible links, after about \(\frac{N^2}{2}\) steps every pair is linked and the network is a complete graph. There is no stationary state.

What to remember

Both ingredients are needed. Growth alone gives an exponential. Preferential attachment alone gives a network where everyone ends up alike and then complete. Only together do they give \(P(k)\sim k^{-3}\).

3c · Nonlinear preferential attachment

Keep growth, and change the rule to a power of the degree:

\[\Pi(k_i) = \frac{k_i^\alpha}{\sum_jk_j^\alpha},\qquad\alpha\ge0.\]

\(\alpha = 0\) is Model A (every node equal) and \(\alpha = 1\) is the BA model. The question is what happens in between and beyond.

\(\alpha\)NameDegree distributionLargest hub after \(t\) steps
0no preferential attachmentexponential\(\sim\ln t\)
\(0<\alpha<1\)sublineara stretched exponential, about \(e^{-ck^{1-\alpha}}\), so no power law and few hubs\(\sim(\ln t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\)
1linear (BA)power law \(k^{-3}\)\(\sim t^{1/2}\)
\(\alpha>1\)superlinearwinner takes all, with one hub and the rest small\(\sim t\)

Why \(\alpha<1\) gives small hubs. When the degrees stay small, \(k_j^\alpha\) has a finite average \(\nu = \langle k^\alpha\rangle\), so the sum over the \(\approx t\) nodes is \(\sum_jk_j^\alpha\approx\nu t\). Then

\[\frac{dk_i}{dt} = \frac{m\,k_i^\alpha}{\nu\,t}\;\Rightarrow\;\int_m^{k_i}k^{-\alpha}dk = \frac m\nu\int_{t_i}^t\frac{dt'}{t'}\;\Rightarrow\;k_i^{1-\alpha} - m^{1-\alpha} = \frac{(1-\alpha)\,m}{\nu}\ln\frac{t}{t_i}.\]

For the oldest node (\(t_i = 1\)) this gives \(k_{\max}\sim(\ln t)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\), which grows more slowly than any power of \(t\). Putting \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\nu = 1\) gives back Model A's \(k_i = m + m\ln(t/t_i)\), which is a useful check.

Why \(\alpha>1\) gives one giant hub. Suppose one node has a degree that grows like \(t\), say \(k_1 = ct\), while the others stay small. Then \(k_1^\alpha = c^\alpha t^\alpha\), and since \(\alpha>1\) this outgrows the sum over all the other nodes, which is only about \(t\). So \(\Pi(1)\to1\). Almost every new node links to node 1, which keeps \(k_1\) growing like \(t\). The guess is self-consistent. One node, usually one of the first, ends up linked to a finite fraction of the network.

4 · Small-world graphs

Start from a ring lattice (\(p = 0\)), which has high clustering and long paths. Rewire each link with probability \(p\). At \(p = 1\) the graph is random, with short paths and almost no clustering. In between, a few rewired links make the paths short while the clustering stays high. The board asks for the average clustering coefficient and the path length at both ends.

In plain words

Put \(n\) people in a circle, each friends with the \(k\) nearest people on each side. Your friends are mostly friends with each other, so the clustering is high, about \(\frac34\). To reach someone on the far side, though, you have to hop around the circle, so paths are long, about \(\frac n{4k}\). That is \(p = 0\).

Now take each link and, with probability \(p\), move one of its ends to a random person. At \(p = 1\) the graph is random. Paths are short, about \(\frac{\ln n}{\ln2k}\), because the number of people you can reach multiplies by about \(2k\) with each step. Your friends rarely know each other, so \(C\approx\frac{2k}n\).

The surprise is in between. A few random links act as shortcuts across the circle, so paths become short almost at once. They hardly change the clustering, because a triangle only breaks when one of its own three links is moved. All three links stay with probability \((1-p)^3\), so \(C(p) = C(0)(1-p)^3\).

Convention

Here \(k\) is the number of neighbours on each side, so the degree is \(2k\). If a question writes \(K\) for the degree, put \(k = K/2\). Then \(C(0) = \frac{3(K-2)}{4(K-1)}\) and \(\bar\ell(0)\approx\frac{n}{2K}\).

Numbers · \(n = 1000\), \(k = 5\) (degree 10)

At \(p = 0\), \(C = \frac{3\cdot4}{2\cdot9} = 0.667\) and \(\bar\ell = \frac{1000}{20} = 50\). At \(p = 1\), \(C = \frac{10}{1000} = 0.01\) and \(\bar\ell = \frac{\ln1000}{\ln10} = 3\). At \(p = 0.1\) the clustering is still \(0.667\times0.9^3 = 0.486\), although there are already \(nkp = 500\) shortcuts.

2024 class quiz 2 · Q1 · 1 markIn a social network, node A has neighbours B, C and D. If B and C are not connected to each other, what is the minimum possible local clustering coefficient of A? (0, 1, 2/3, 1/2, 1/3)›

A has 3 neighbours, so there are \(\binom32 = 3\) pairs of them, and the pair B–C is missing. The smallest value comes when the other two pairs (B with D, and C with D) are missing as well. That leaves 0 links out of 3, so \(C_A = 0\). The largest possible value is \(\frac23\).

Practice · 2 + 2 marksWhat happens to the BA model if (a) the number of nodes is fixed, (b) the attachment probability is \(\Pi\propto k^\alpha\) with \(\alpha>1\)?›

Practice · 3 marksTwo oscillators have \(\omega_1 = 1\), \(\omega_2 = 4\), \(k_1 = 1\), \(k_2 = 2\). Do they lock? If so, at what frequency, and what is the locked phase difference?›

Numbers · 2 marksIn a BA network with \(m = 3\), node \(i\) joins at \(t_i = 100\). What is its degree at \(t = 10\,000\)? When does it reach degree 60?›

\(k_i(t) = m\sqrt{t/t_i}\). At \(t = 10\,000\), \(k_i = 3\sqrt{100} = 30\).

For degree 60 you need \(\sqrt{t/100} = 20\), so \(t/100 = 400\) and \(t = 40\,000\). Doubling the degree takes four times as long, because \(k\) grows like \(\sqrt t\).

Derive · 3 marksStarting from \(P(k) = 2m^2k^{-3}\) for \(k\ge m\), show that it adds up to 1, that \(\langle k\rangle = 2m\), and that \(\langle k^2\rangle\) is infinite.›
  1. \(\int_m^\infty2m^2k^{-3}\,dk = 2m^2\cdot\frac1{2m^2} = 1\).
  2. \(\langle k\rangle = \int_m^\infty k\cdot2m^2k^{-3}\,dk = 2m^2\int_m^\infty k^{-2}\,dk = 2m^2\cdot\frac1m = 2m\). This must be true, since each new node brings \(m\) links and each link has two ends.
  3. \(\langle k^2\rangle = \int_m^\infty2m^2k^{-1}\,dk = 2m^2\big[\ln k\big]_m^\infty\), which diverges. In a finite network it grows like \(\ln k_{\max}\), so the spread of degrees keeps growing as the network grows.
Explain · 2 + 1 marks(a) Two nodes joined at \(t = 10\) and \(t = 1000\). What is the ratio of their degrees at any later time? (b) What fraction of nodes have degree at least \(4m\)?›

(a) \(\frac{k_{10}}{k_{1000}} = \frac{m\sqrt{t/10}}{m\sqrt{t/1000}} = \sqrt{100} = 10\). The ratio never changes, so the older node stays ten times bigger forever. That is "first come, first served".

(b) From the proof, the fraction with degree below \(k\) is \(1 - \frac{m^2}{k^2}\), so the fraction with degree at least \(k\) is \(\frac{m^2}{k^2}\). At \(k = 4m\) that is \(\frac1{16}\), about 6%.

2 · The master equation

Proof: master equation

Numbers · 2 marksFor \(m = 1\), find \(P(1)\), \(P(2)\) and \(P(3)\) from \(P(k) = \frac{2m(m+1)}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\), and check them against the recursion \(P(k) = \frac{k-1}{k+2}P(k-1)\).›

With \(m = 1\), \(P(k) = \frac4{k(k+1)(k+2)}\).

  • \(P(1) = \frac46 = \frac23\). This matches the starting value \(P(m) = \frac2{m+2} = \frac23\).
  • \(P(2) = \frac4{24} = \frac16\). The recursion gives \(\frac14\cdot\frac23 = \frac16\).
  • \(P(3) = \frac4{60} = \frac1{15}\). The recursion gives \(\frac25\cdot\frac16 = \frac1{15}\).

So two thirds of the nodes in a BA tree have only one link.

Explain · 3 marksIn \(N(k, t+1) - N(k, t) = \frac{k-1}2P(k-1, t) - \frac k2P(k, t) + \delta_{k,m}\), say where each term comes from. Why is it \(\frac k2P(k)\)?›

\(N(k, t)\) is the number of nodes with degree \(k\) at step \(t\), and \(P(k, t) = N(k, t)/t\) is the fraction.

  1. Out term. Each of the \(m\) new links lands on a given degree-\(k\) node with probability \(\frac k{2mt}\). Summed over the \(N(k)\) such nodes and the \(m\) links, the expected number of hits is \(m\cdot N(k)\cdot\frac k{2mt} = \frac k2\cdot\frac{N(k)}t = \frac k2P(k)\). Each hit moves a node from degree \(k\) to \(k+1\), so it is a loss.
  2. In term. The same count with \(k-1\) instead of \(k\) gives \(\frac{k-1}2P(k-1)\). Those nodes move up into degree \(k\).
  3. \(\delta_{k,m}\). The new node itself arrives with degree \(m\), so the count at \(k = m\) goes up by one. For \(k\ne m\) this term is 0.

It assumes no node gets two of the new links in one step, which is very unlikely when \(t\) is large.

Derive · 3 marksShow that \(P(k) = \frac{2m(m+1)}{k(k+1)(k+2)}\) adds up to 1 over \(k = m, m+1, \dots\).›
  1. Split the fraction. \(\frac1{k(k+1)(k+2)} = \frac12\left[\frac1{k(k+1)} - \frac1{(k+1)(k+2)}\right]\). You can check this by putting the right side over a common denominator.
  2. Summing from \(k = m\), each negative term cancels the positive term of the next \(k\). Only the first positive term is left, so \(\sum_{k\ge m}\frac1{k(k+1)(k+2)} = \frac12\cdot\frac1{m(m+1)}\).
  3. Multiply by \(2m(m+1)\) to get exactly 1.

3 · No preference, no growth, nonlinear \(\Pi\)

Proof: Model A, Model B, nonlinear

Numbers · 2 + 2 marksModel A (growth, uniform attachment) with \(m = 2\). (a) A node joins at \(t_i = 10\). What is its degree at \(t = 1000\), and what would it be in the BA model? (b) What fraction of nodes have degree above \(10m\) in each model?›

(a) Model A gives \(k_i = m + m\ln(t/t_i) = 2 + 2\ln100 = 2 + 9.2 = 11.2\). BA gives \(k_i = 2\sqrt{100} = 20\). Preference makes old nodes grow much faster.

(b) In Model A the fraction above \(k\) is \(e^{1 - k/m}\), so at \(k = 10m\) it is \(e^{-9}\approx0.0001\). In BA it is \(\frac{m^2}{k^2} = \frac1{100} = 0.01\), a hundred times more. That is the difference between an exponential tail and a power-law tail. Only BA has hubs.

Explain · 3 marksModel B has \(N = 100\) nodes and no growth. Each step adds one link, with one end at a random node and the other chosen by preferential attachment. (a) What is the average degree after 2000 steps? (b) Why do all degrees become about equal? (c) Roughly when does the network become complete?›

(a) Each step adds one link and so 2 to the total degree. After 2000 steps the total is 4000, and \(\langle k\rangle = \frac{4000}{100} = 40\).

(b) \(\frac{dk_i}{dt} = \frac1N + \frac{k_i}{2t}\) is solved by \(k_i = \frac{2t}N + C_i\sqrt t\). The first part is the same for every node and grows like \(t\). The part that differs between nodes grows only like \(\sqrt t\), so it becomes small in comparison and the degrees bunch up around \(\frac{2t}N\).

(c) A complete graph on 100 nodes has \(\frac{100\cdot99}2 = 4950\) links, so after about 5000 steps every pair is linked. Nothing settles down before that, so there is no stationary distribution.

Explain · 3 marksWith \(\Pi(k_i) = \frac{k_i^\alpha}{\sum_jk_j^\alpha}\), how does the largest degree grow with \(t\) for \(\alpha = 0.5\), \(\alpha = 1\) and \(\alpha = 2\)? Which ones give a power law?›
  • \(\alpha = 0.5\) is sublinear. The largest hub grows like \((\ln t)^{1/(1-\alpha)} = (\ln t)^2\), which is slower than any power of \(t\). The distribution is a stretched exponential, so there is no power law.
  • \(\alpha = 1\) is BA. The largest hub grows like \(t^{1/2}\) and \(P(k)\sim k^{-3}\). This is the only power law of the three.
  • \(\alpha = 2\) is superlinear. One node's \(k^2\) outgrows the sum over everyone else, so it receives almost every new link and its degree grows like \(t\). The rest stay small. This is winner takes all.

4 · Small world at \(p = 0\) and \(p = 1\)

Proof: \(C(0)\), \(\bar\ell(0)\), \(p = 1\)

Numbers · 3 marksA ring of \(n = 600\) nodes, each linked to \(k = 3\) neighbours on each side. Find \(C\) and \(\bar\ell\) at \(p = 0\) and at \(p = 1\).›

\(p = 0\): \(C = \frac{3(k-1)}{2(2k-1)} = \frac{3\cdot2}{2\cdot5} = 0.6\) and \(\bar\ell\approx\frac n{4k} = \frac{600}{12} = 50\).

\(p = 1\): \(C\approx\frac{2k}n = \frac6{600} = 0.01\) and \(\bar\ell\approx\frac{\ln n}{\ln2k} = \frac{\ln600}{\ln6} = \frac{6.40}{1.79}\approx3.6\).

Rewiring everything cuts the path length from 50 to under 4 and the clustering from 0.6 to 0.01.

Derive · 2 marksFor a ring with \(k = 2\) (degree 4), count the links between the neighbours of one node directly and find \(C(0)\). Check it against the formula.›

Call the node 0. Its neighbours are −2, −1, 1 and 2, which make \(\binom42 = 6\) pairs. Two of them are linked when they are at most 2 apart on the ring.

  • Linked: (−2, −1), (−1, 1) and (1, 2).
  • Not linked: (−2, 1) and (−1, 2) are 3 apart, and (−2, 2) is 4 apart.

So \(C(0) = \frac36 = \frac12\). The formula gives \(\frac{3(2-1)}{2(4-1)} = \frac12\), which matches.

Explain · 2 + 1 marks(a) A question gives the degree as \(K = 10\). Find \(C(0)\). (b) Show that \(C(0)\to\frac34\) for large \(k\), and say why \(\bar\ell(0)\) grows with \(n\) but \(\bar\ell(1)\) barely does.›

(a) \(k = K/2 = 5\), so \(C(0) = \frac{3\cdot4}{2\cdot9} = \frac23\). The degree formula gives the same, \(\frac{3(K-2)}{4(K-1)} = \frac{24}{36} = \frac23\).

(b) For large \(k\), \(\frac{3(k-1)}{2(2k-1)}\approx\frac{3k}{4k} = \frac34\). On the ring each step moves you at most \(k\) places, so crossing the ring takes about \(\frac n{2k}\) steps, which grows in proportion to \(n\). In the random graph the number of nodes you can reach multiplies by about \(2k\) each step, so you reach all \(n\) after about \(\frac{\ln n}{\ln2k}\) steps. That grows only like \(\ln n\).

5 · \(C(p) = C(0)(1-p)^3\)

Proof: \(C(p)\)

Numbers · 2 marksAt what rewiring probability \(p\) has the clustering fallen to half of \(C(0)\)?›

Solve \((1-p)^3 = \frac12\). Then \(1 - p = 2^{-1/3} = 0.794\), so \(p\approx0.21\). About a fifth of the links have to be moved before the clustering halves. The path length has already fallen a long way by \(p = 0.01\).

Explain · 2 marksWhy is the power in \(C(p) = C(0)(1-p)^3\) equal to 3 and not 1?›

The clustering counts triangles, that is, pairs of your neighbours who are linked to each other. A triangle is made of three links. It survives only if none of the three is rewired, and each one stays with probability \(1-p\), independently. So a triangle survives with probability \((1-p)^3\).

The formula ignores the few new triangles that random links happen to make. That is fine because there are only about \(\frac{2k}n\) of them, the same as the clustering of a random graph.

Numbers · 2 + 1 marks\(n = 1000\), \(k = 5\), \(p = 0.01\). (a) How many links are rewired, and what is \(C\)? (b) Why is this called a small world?›

(a) The ring has \(nk = 5000\) links, so about \(5000\times0.01 = 50\) are rewired. \(C(0) = \frac{3\cdot4}{2\cdot9} = 0.667\), so \(C = 0.667\times0.99^3 = 0.667\times0.970 = 0.647\). The clustering has barely changed.

(b) Those 50 shortcuts join far-apart parts of the ring, so most paths can use one, and the average path length drops to close to the random value. A graph with clustering as high as a lattice but paths as short as a random graph is a small world.

6 · Two coupled oscillators

Proof: two oscillators

Numbers · 3 marks\(\omega_1 = 3\), \(\omega_2 = 7\) and \(k_1 = k_2 = k\). (a) Find the critical \(k\). (b) At \(k = 4\), find the locked phase difference and \(\Omega\), and say which root is stable.›

(a) Lock iff \(|\Delta\omega|\le k_1 + k_2\), that is \(4\le2k\). So \(k_c = 2\).

(b) \(\sin\phi^* = \frac{\Delta\omega}{k_1 + k_2} = \frac48 = \frac12\), which has two roots, \(\phi^* = \frac\pi6\) and \(\frac{5\pi}6\). The slope of \(\dot\phi\) is \(-(k_1 + k_2)\cos\phi\). It is negative at \(\frac\pi6\), where the cosine is positive, so that root is stable and \(\frac{5\pi}6\) is unstable.

\(\Omega = \frac{k_2\omega_1 + k_1\omega_2}{k_1 + k_2} = \frac{4\cdot3 + 4\cdot7}8 = 5\), the plain average, because the couplings are equal.

Numbers · 3 marks\(\omega_1 = 0\), \(\omega_2 = 6\), \(k_1 = 2\), \(k_2 = 6\). Do they lock? If so, find \(\phi^*\) and \(\Omega\), and check \(\Omega\) using both equations.›

\(|\Delta\omega| = 6\le k_1 + k_2 = 8\), so they lock. \(\sin\phi^* = \frac68 = \frac34\), so \(\phi^* = 0.85\) rad (the root with \(\cos\phi^*>0\)).

\(\Omega = \frac{6\cdot0 + 2\cdot6}8 = 1.5\). Check with oscillator 1: \(\omega_1 + k_1\sin\phi^* = 0 + 2\cdot\frac34 = 1.5\). Check with oscillator 2: \(\omega_2 - k_2\sin\phi^* = 6 - 6\cdot\frac34 = 1.5\).

\(\Omega\) is much closer to \(\omega_1\), because oscillator 1 has the smaller coupling strength (\(k_1 = 2\)), so its rate changes less than the rate of oscillator 2.

Derive · 2 marksOnly oscillator 1 feels the other, so \(k_2 = 0\). Find the condition for locking and the common frequency.›

\(\dot\phi = \Delta\omega - k_1\sin\phi\), so locking needs \(|\Delta\omega|\le k_1\). Then \(\Omega = \frac{0\cdot\omega_1 + k_1\omega_2}{k_1} = \omega_2\).

Oscillator 2 is not affected by oscillator 1, so it keeps its natural frequency, and oscillator 1 locks to that frequency.

7 · Lorenz

Proof: Lorenz

Numbers · 2 marksFind all the fixed points for \(\sigma = 10\), \(\beta = \frac83\), \(\rho = 28\).›

The origin \((0, 0, 0)\) is always one. Since \(\rho>1\) there are two more, with \(z^* = \rho - 1 = 27\) and \(x^* = y^* = \pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho-1)} = \pm\sqrt{\frac83\cdot27} = \pm\sqrt{72} = \pm8.49\).

So \(C_\pm = (\pm8.49,\ \pm8.49,\ 27)\), with the signs taken together.

Numbers · 3 marksWith \(\sigma = 10\) and \(\beta = \frac83\), find the eigenvalues of the Jacobian at the origin for \(\rho = 0.5\) and for \(\rho = 28\). Is the origin stable?›

At the origin \(J\) splits into \(-\beta = -\frac83\) and the block \(\begin{pmatrix}-10 & 10\\ \rho & -1\end{pmatrix}\), which has trace \(-11\) and determinant \(10(1 - \rho)\). Its eigenvalues are \(\lambda = \frac{-11\pm\sqrt{121 - 40(1-\rho)}}2\).

  • \(\rho = 0.5\): the determinant is 5, so \(\lambda = \frac{-11\pm\sqrt{101}}2 = -0.47\) and \(-10.5\). All three eigenvalues are negative, so the origin is stable.
  • \(\rho = 28\): the determinant is \(-270\), so \(\lambda = \frac{-11\pm\sqrt{1201}}2 = 11.8\) and \(-22.8\). One eigenvalue is positive, so the origin is an unstable saddle.
Derive · 2 + 1 marks(a) Write the Jacobian at \(C_+\) in terms of \(\sigma\), \(\beta\) and \(x^*\). (b) Why do \(C_\pm\) exist only for \(\rho>1\), and what happens at \(\rho = 1\)?›

(a) Put \(y^* = x^*\) and \(z^* = \rho - 1\) into the general Jacobian. Then \(\rho - z^* = 1\), so

\[J(C_+) = \begin{pmatrix}-\sigma & \sigma & 0\\ 1 & -1 & -x^*\\ x^* & x^* & -\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad x^* = \sqrt{\beta(\rho - 1)}.\]

(b) They need \(x^{*2} = \beta(\rho - 1)\), and a square can't be negative, so \(\rho\ge1\). At \(\rho = 1\) they sit at the origin. As \(\rho\) passes 1 they split off from it and the origin turns unstable. That is a pitchfork bifurcation.
