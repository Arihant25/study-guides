Part 1Module 1 · Foundations of quantum mechanics and solid-state physics

1A · Light as particles, electrons as waves

About 20 minutes. Lecture 1.1, and the Davisson–Germer part of 1.2.

What the course is about. A quantum material is one whose large-scale properties (how it conducts, shines or responds to a magnet) are set by quantum effects. The course lists three signs of this. Quantum confinement: electrons squeezed into 1, 2 or 3 small dimensions get discrete energy levels. Quantum coherence: electrons keep their wave behaviour over 100 to 1000 nm, which allows superconductivity and quantum computing. Topological protection: some states are protected by the geometry of the material and survive defects and disorder. Examples are quantum dots, graphene, superconductors and topological insulators. Everyday uses are MRI (superconducting magnets), LEDs (quantum wells), QLED screens (quantum dots), hard-disk read heads (giant magnetoresistance) and GPS (atomic clocks).

The course gives these reasons why classical physics is not enough. Transistors are approaching the size of atoms (about 5 nm), where electrons leak through barriers by tunnelling. Classical computers lose most of their energy as heat. A classical bit is only 0 or 1, while a qubit can be in a combination of both, and \(N\) qubits can represent \(2^N\) states at once (300 qubits represent more states than there are atoms in the universe).

A quantum device is made in three steps: design the material, fabricate it at the nanoscale (lithography, deposition, etching), then integrate it (contacts, packaging, often cooling, and classical control electronics).

Wave–particle duality of light

Interference shows that light is a wave. The photoelectric effect shows that light also comes in particles, called photons. When light shines on a metal it can knock electrons out. Heinrich Hertz saw this in 1887, and Einstein explained it in 1905. Each photon carries energy \(E = h\nu\). An electron is freed only if one photon gives it at least the work function \(\phi\) (phi), the minimum energy needed to pull an electron out of that metal. Whatever is left over becomes the electron's kinetic energy.

\[KE_{\max} = h\nu - \phi\]
Reading the formula

\(h\nu\) is the energy of one incoming photon. \(\phi\) is the cost of getting the electron out. \(KE_{\max}\) is the most kinetic energy an ejected electron can have; it is a maximum because some electrons lose extra energy on the way out.

The formula explains the four observations on the slide. Below the threshold frequency \(\nu_0 = \frac\phi h\), \(KE_{\max}\) would be negative, so no electrons come out however bright the light. Above it, emission is instant, because one photon does the job. A brighter light has more photons, so more electrons, but each has the same energy. A higher frequency gives each electron more energy.

Example. A metal has \(\phi = 2.3\) eV. Light of 400 nm has photons of \(\frac{1240}{400} = 3.1\) eV, so \(KE_{\max} = 3.1 - 2.3 = 0.8\) eV. Light of 620 nm has photons of 2.0 eV, below 2.3 eV, so no electrons come out. ARPES (4B) uses this same equation.

Matter waves: the de Broglie wavelength

De Broglie suggested that if light waves behave like particles, particles such as electrons should also behave like waves, with a wavelength set by their momentum \(p = mv\).

Write this in the exam

Derive the de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda = \frac hp\).

  1. For a photon, Planck gives \(E = h\nu = \frac{hc}\lambda\), and Einstein gives \(E = mc^2\).
  2. Equate them: \(mc^2 = \frac{hc}\lambda\), so \(\lambda = \frac h{mc} = \frac hp\).
    Why (covers lines 1 and 2, 4 steps)
    1. A photon's frequency and wavelength are linked by \(c = \nu\lambda\), so \(\nu = \frac c\lambda\) and \(E = h\nu = \frac{hc}\lambda\).
    2. The same photon has energy \(E = mc^2\), where \(m\) is its effective mass.
    3. Both expressions are the energy of the same photon, so \(mc^2 = \frac{hc}\lambda\).
    4. Divide both sides by \(mc^2\) and multiply by \(\lambda\). \(\lambda = \frac{hc}{mc^2} = \frac h{mc}\). For a photon, \(mc\) is its momentum \(p\), so \(\lambda = \frac hp\).
  3. De Broglie's idea: the same holds for a particle moving at speed \(v\), with \(p = mv\). So \(\lambda = \frac h{mv}\).
  4. For a particle of kinetic energy \(E_k\), \(p = \sqrt{2mE_k}\). An electron accelerated through \(V\) volts has \(E_k = eV\), so \(\lambda = \frac h{\sqrt{2meV}}\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Kinetic energy is \(E_k = \frac12mv^2\). Multiply top and bottom by \(m\): \(E_k = \frac{(mv)^2}{2m} = \frac{p^2}{2m}\).
    2. Rearrange. \(p^2 = 2mE_k\), so \(p = \sqrt{2mE_k}\).
    3. Moving through a voltage \(V\) gives a charge \(e\) the energy \(eV\). Put \(E_k = eV\) into \(\lambda = \frac hp\): \(\lambda = \frac h{\sqrt{2meV}}\).

Because \(\lambda = \frac hp\), a lighter particle with the same kinetic energy has a smaller momentum (\(p = \sqrt{2mE_k}\)) and so a longer wavelength. Of an electron, proton, neutron and alpha particle with the same kinetic energy, the electron has the longest wavelength.

The Davisson–Germer experiment (1927)

Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer fired a beam of electrons from an electron gun at a nickel crystal and measured how many bounced off at each angle with a detector that could rotate. If electrons were plain particles, they would scatter evenly. Instead, at 54 V the scattered beam had a sharp peak at 50°. This is diffraction from the rows of nickel atoms, so electrons are waves. It was the first proof of matter waves for a particle that has mass. It is the basis of electron diffraction methods (LEED, TEM, and RHEED in 4D).

The peak matches Bragg's law for waves reflecting off planes of atoms a distance \(d\) apart: \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the beam and the planes and \(n = 1, 2, \dots\) The scattering angle of 50° gives \(\theta = 65^\circ\) (since \(\theta + 50^\circ + \theta = 180^\circ\)), and for nickel \(d = 0.91\) Å.

1B · The uncertainty principle

About 20 minutes. Lecture 1.2.

In classical physics a particle has a definite position and momentum. A quantum particle is a wave packet, a bump made by adding many waves of slightly different wavelengths. A short packet has a well-defined position, but it needs a wide range of wavelengths, so its momentum is spread out. A long packet has nearly one wavelength (sharp momentum) but a spread-out position. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle puts a number on this.

\[\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac\hbar2,\qquad \Delta E\,\Delta t\ge\frac\hbar2\]
Reading the formula

\(\Delta x\) is the spread (uncertainty) in position and \(\Delta p\) the spread in momentum. Their product can never be smaller than \(\frac\hbar2\). Squeeze one and the other must grow. The same holds for energy and time: a state that lasts only a short time \(\Delta t\) has an energy spread \(\Delta E\).

The slides also derive the rougher form \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge h\) (shown below). It has the same meaning. Use \(\frac\hbar2\) unless the question says otherwise, and state which form you used.

Because \(\hbar\) is about \(10^{-34}\) J s, the limit does not matter for a cricket ball. For a 1 kg ball known to within 1 µm, \(\Delta v\ge\frac{10^{-34}}{2\times10^{-6}}\approx5\times10^{-29}\) m/s. It matters hugely for an electron (see the question below).

Write this in the exam

Derive the uncertainty principle from the wave packet (the slide's method).

  1. The packet moves at the group velocity \(u = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta k}\).
  2. With \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\) and \(k = \frac{2\pi p}h\): \(u = \frac{2\pi\Delta\nu}{2\pi\Delta p/h} = \frac{h\Delta\nu}{\Delta p}\).
    Why (covers lines 1 and 2, 4 steps)
    1. A wave packet is a group of waves. Its speed, the group velocity, is \(u = \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta k}\), the change in angular frequency over the change in wavenumber across the group.
    2. \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\), so \(\Delta\omega = 2\pi\Delta\nu\).
    3. \(k = \frac{2\pi}\lambda\) and \(\lambda = \frac hp\) (1A), so \(k = \frac{2\pi p}h\) and \(\Delta k = \frac{2\pi\Delta p}h\).
    4. Divide. \(u = \frac{2\pi\Delta\nu}{2\pi\Delta p/h}\). Cancel \(2\pi\): \(u = \frac{h\Delta\nu}{\Delta p}\).
  3. The packet also crosses its own length \(\Delta x\) in a time \(\Delta t\), so \(u = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\).
  4. Equate the two: \(\Delta x\,\Delta p = h\,\Delta\nu\,\Delta t\).
  5. For any wave packet, \(\Delta\nu\,\Delta t\ge1\) (a Fourier property). So \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge h\). The exact treatment gives \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\frac\hbar2\).
    Why (covers lines 3 to 5, 4 steps)
    1. A packet of length \(\Delta x\) moving at \(u\) takes \(\Delta t = \frac{\Delta x}u\) to pass a point, so \(u = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\).
    2. Set this equal to line 2. \(\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{h\Delta\nu}{\Delta p}\). Multiply both sides by \(\Delta t\,\Delta p\): \(\Delta x\,\Delta p = h\,\Delta\nu\,\Delta t\).
    3. To build a pulse that lasts \(\Delta t\), you must mix frequencies spread over at least \(\Delta\nu\approx\frac1{\Delta t}\). This is a general fact about waves (the Fourier uncertainty), so \(\Delta\nu\,\Delta t\ge1\).
    4. Put this in. \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge h\). Using \(E = h\nu\), the same steps give \(\Delta E\,\Delta t\ge h\). A careful calculation with Gaussian packets lowers the bound to \(\frac\hbar2\).

1C · Wavefunctions and operators

About 15 minutes. Lectures 1.2 and 1.5.

A quantum particle is described by a wavefunction \(\Psi(x, t)\) (capital psi). It holds everything that can be measured about the particle. It can be complex, and on its own it has no direct physical meaning. Its size squared does (the Born interpretation, after Max Born).

\[P(x\text{ to }x+dx) = |\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi|^2dx = 1.\]
Reading the formula

\(|\Psi|^2\) is a probability density, a probability per unit length. Multiply it by a small length \(dx\) to get a probability. The second equation says the particle must be somewhere, so the probabilities over all space add up to 1. Making a wavefunction obey this is called normalising it. 1E uses this to find the constant in front of the box wavefunctions.

So the wavefunction is a probability amplitude. It does not give the particle's trajectory, its energy directly, or the force on it.

Operators. Each measurable quantity (an observable) has an operator, an instruction that acts on \(\Psi\). A hat marks an operator.

ObservableOperatorWhat it does to \(\psi\)
Position\(\hat x = x\)multiply by \(x\)
Momentum\(\hat p = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\)differentiate, then multiply by \(-i\hbar\)
Energy (the Hamiltonian)\(\hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V\)kinetic energy part plus potential energy \(V\)
Angular momentum about z\(\hat L_z = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\)differentiate with respect to the angle \(\phi\)

Operators are linear: \(\hat A(c_1\psi_1 + c_2\psi_2) = c_1\hat A\psi_1 + c_2\hat A\psi_2\). Operators for observables are Hermitian, which guarantees that their results are real numbers. A state with a definite value \(a\) of the observable satisfies the eigenvalue equation \(\hat A\psi = a\psi\). Here \(a\) is the eigenvalue (the value measured) and \(\psi\) the eigenfunction.

Example. Apply \(\hat p\) to \(\psi = e^{ikx}\). \(\hat p\psi = -i\hbar\times ik\,e^{ikx} = \hbar k\,e^{ikx}\), because \(-i\times i = -i^2 = 1\). So \(e^{ikx}\) has definite momentum \(p = \hbar k\), which is de Broglie's \(p = \frac h\lambda\) again, since \(\hbar k = \frac h{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}\lambda\).

1D · The Schrödinger equations

About 25 minutes. Lectures 1.3 and 1.4a. Asked as a 10-mark question in the surprise test and as Q3 of Quiz 1.

The Schrödinger equation is to a quantum particle what \(F = ma\) is to a ball: it tells you how the wavefunction behaves. There are two versions. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) says how \(\Psi(x, t)\) changes in time. The time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE) is used when the potential energy \(V\) does not change with time. It gives the allowed energies and the shapes \(\psi(x)\) of the states that have those energies.

Write this in the exam

Derive the time-independent Schrödinger equation from the classical wave equation.

  1. Classical wave equation: \(\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2} = \frac1{v^2}\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2}\). Try \(y = \psi(x)e^{-i\omega t}\).
  2. Substituting gives \(\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{\omega^2}{v^2}\psi = 0\).
    Why (covers lines 1 and 2, 5 steps)
    1. The classical wave equation describes any wave \(y(x, t)\) moving at speed \(v\), such as a wave on a string.
    2. Look for a standing wave, a fixed shape \(\psi(x)\) that oscillates in time: \(y = \psi(x)e^{-i\omega t}\).
    3. Left side. Only \(\psi\) depends on \(x\), so \(\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2} = \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}e^{-i\omega t}\).
    4. Right side. Differentiating \(e^{-i\omega t}\) twice brings down \((-i\omega)^2 = -\omega^2\). So \(\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2\psi e^{-i\omega t}\).
    5. Put both into the wave equation and cancel \(e^{-i\omega t}\), which is never zero. \(\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -\frac{\omega^2}{v^2}\psi\). Move the term to the left: \(\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{\omega^2}{v^2}\psi = 0\).
  3. \(\frac\omega v = \frac{2\pi\nu}{\nu\lambda} = \frac{2\pi}\lambda\), and de Broglie gives \(\lambda = \frac hp\), so \(\frac{\omega^2}{v^2} = \frac{p^2}{\hbar^2}\).
  4. Total energy \(E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V\), so \(p^2 = 2m(E - V)\).
  5. Therefore \[\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\psi = 0,\quad\text{that is}\quad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V\psi = E\psi.\]
    Why (covers lines 3 to 5, 6 steps)
    1. \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\) and \(v = \nu\lambda\), so \(\frac\omega v = \frac{2\pi}\lambda\).
    2. Put in the de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda = \frac hp\). \(\frac\omega v = \frac{2\pi p}h = \frac p\hbar\), since \(\hbar = \frac h{2\pi}\).
    3. Square it. \(\frac{\omega^2}{v^2} = \frac{p^2}{\hbar^2}\).
    4. Energy is kinetic plus potential, \(E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V\) (kinetic energy is \(\frac{p^2}{2m}\), see 1A). Rearrange: \(p^2 = 2m(E - V)\).
    5. Substitute into line 2. \(\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{2m(E-V)}{\hbar^2}\psi = 0\).
    6. To get the second form, multiply every term by \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\) and move \(E\psi\) to the right. The left side is \(\hat H\psi\) (1C), so the TISE reads \(\hat H\psi = E\psi\): an eigenvalue equation whose eigenvalues are the allowed energies.
Write this in the exam

Derive the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

  1. A free particle is a plane wave \(\Psi = Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)}\), with \(E = \hbar\omega\) and \(p = \hbar k\).
  2. Differentiate in time: \(i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \hbar\omega\Psi = E\Psi\).
  3. Differentiate twice in space: \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\Psi = \frac{p^2}{2m}\Psi\).
    Why (covers lines 1 to 3, 6 steps)
    1. Planck: \(E = h\nu = \frac h{2\pi}\cdot2\pi\nu = \hbar\omega\). De Broglie: \(p = \frac h\lambda = \frac h{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}\lambda = \hbar k\).
    2. Time derivative of the plane wave. Differentiating \(e^{-i\omega t}\) brings down \(-i\omega\): \(\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -i\omega\Psi\).
    3. Multiply both sides by \(i\hbar\). \(i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = i\hbar(-i\omega)\Psi = \hbar\omega\Psi\), because \(i\times(-i) = 1\). And \(\hbar\omega = E\).
    4. Space derivative. Differentiating \(e^{ikx}\) once brings down \(ik\), twice brings down \((ik)^2 = -k^2\): \(\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} = -k^2\Psi\).
    5. Multiply both sides by \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\). \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\Psi\).
    6. Since \(\hbar k = p\), the right side is \(\frac{p^2}{2m}\Psi\), the kinetic energy times \(\Psi\).
  4. Energy is \(E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V\). Multiply by \(\Psi\) and use lines 2 and 3: \[i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} + V\Psi.\]
  5. If \(V\) does not depend on time, write \(\Psi = \psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\). Substituting and cancelling \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\) gives back the TISE, \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi = E\psi\).
    Why (covers lines 4 and 5, 5 steps)
    1. Multiply \(E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V\) by \(\Psi\). \(E\Psi = \frac{p^2}{2m}\Psi + V\Psi\).
    2. Replace \(E\Psi\) by line 2 and \(\frac{p^2}{2m}\Psi\) by line 3. This gives the TDSE. It is assumed to hold for any potential, not only the free particle it was built from.
    3. Now let \(V\) depend on \(x\) only. Try \(\Psi = \psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), a fixed shape times an oscillating phase. This is called separation of variables.
    4. Left side: \(i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = i\hbar\cdot\left(-\frac{iE}\hbar\right)\psi e^{-iEt/\hbar} = E\psi e^{-iEt/\hbar}\). Right side: \(\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi\right)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\).
    5. Cancel \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\) from both sides. What is left is the TISE. Such states are called stationary states, because \(|\Psi|^2 = |\psi|^2\) does not change in time.
Remember
  • TDSE: \(i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \hat H\Psi\). TISE: \(\hat H\psi = E\psi\), with \(\hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + V\).
  • Use the TISE when the potential does not depend on time.
  • Ingredients of both derivations: \(E = \hbar\omega\), \(p = \hbar k\), \(E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V\).

1E · Particle in a box

About 35 minutes. Lecture 1.4b. Asked in every 2025 paper: a 10-mark derivation in the mid-sem and end-sem, and "find the errors in this diagram" in Quiz 1 and the surprise test.

A particle of mass \(m\) moves along a line between two walls at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\). Inside, the potential energy is \(V = 0\). The walls are infinitely high (\(V = \infty\) outside), so the particle can never be outside. This is the simplest model of an electron trapped in a small region, such as a quantum well (2A) or a quantum dot.

Write this in the exam

Solve the Schrödinger equation for a particle in a 1D box of length \(L\) with infinite walls. Show that the energy is quantised.

  1. Outside the box \(\psi = 0\). Inside, \(V = 0\), so the TISE is \(\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + k^2\psi = 0\) with \(k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Where \(V = \infty\), the particle would need infinite energy, so the probability \(|\psi|^2\) there is 0, and \(\psi = 0\).
    2. Inside, put \(V = 0\) in the TISE (1D, line 5): \(\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\psi = 0\).
    3. Name the constant \(k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\) to keep the writing short.
  2. General solution: \(\psi(x) = A\sin kx + B\cos kx\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. We need a function whose second derivative is \(-k^2\) times itself. Both \(\sin kx\) and \(\cos kx\) do this (0b).
    2. Any mix \(A\sin kx + B\cos kx\) also works, because the equation is linear. \(A\) and \(B\) are constants fixed by the walls.
  3. \(\psi\) must be continuous, so \(\psi(0) = 0\). This gives \(B = 0\).
  4. \(\psi(L) = 0\) gives \(A\sin kL = 0\), so \(kL = n\pi\) with \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)
    Why (covers lines 3 and 4, 5 steps)
    1. The wavefunction cannot jump, and it is 0 just outside, so it is 0 at each wall.
    2. At \(x = 0\): \(\sin0 = 0\) and \(\cos0 = 1\), so \(\psi(0) = B\). Setting \(\psi(0) = 0\) gives \(B = 0\). Now \(\psi = A\sin kx\).
    3. At \(x = L\): \(A\sin kL = 0\). We cannot take \(A = 0\), because then \(\psi = 0\) everywhere and there is no particle.
    4. So \(\sin kL = 0\). Sine is zero at whole multiples of \(\pi\), so \(kL = n\pi\) and \(k = \frac{n\pi}L\).
    5. \(n = 0\) would give \(\psi = 0\) again, so it is not allowed. Negative \(n\) only flips the sign of \(\psi\), the same state. So \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)
  5. So the energy can only take the values \[E_n = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}.\]
    Why (3 steps)
    1. From line 1, \(k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\). Solve for the energy: \(E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\).
    2. Put in \(k = \frac{n\pi}L\). \(E_n = \frac{\hbar^2n^2\pi^2}{2mL^2}\).
    3. Replace \(\hbar = \frac h{2\pi}\), so \(\hbar^2\pi^2 = \frac{h^2}{4\pi^2}\pi^2 = \frac{h^2}4\). Then \(E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}\). Only these energies are allowed: the energy is quantised.
  6. Normalise: \(\int_0^L A^2\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx = A^2\frac L2 = 1\), so \[\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}L.\]
    Why (4 steps)
    1. The particle must be somewhere in the box, so \(\int_0^L|\psi|^2dx = 1\) (1C).
    2. Use \(\sin^2\theta = \frac{1-\cos2\theta}2\). The integral of \(\frac12\) from 0 to \(L\) is \(\frac L2\).
    3. The cosine term goes through \(n\) whole cycles between 0 and \(L\), so its integral is 0. So the integral is \(A^2\frac L2\).
    4. Set \(A^2\frac L2 = 1\). Then \(A^2 = \frac2L\) and \(A = \sqrt{\frac2L}\).
Reading the formula

\(E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}\). The top has \(n^2\): the levels go as \(1, 4, 9, 16, \dots\) times \(E_1\), so the gaps between them grow (\(3E_1, 5E_1, 7E_1, \dots\)). They are not evenly spaced. The bottom has \(L^2\): a smaller box gives higher and more widely spaced levels. This is why shrinking a quantum dot shifts its light towards blue (2A, 3B). The bottom also has \(m\): lighter particles have more widely spaced levels.

The lowest energy is \(E_1\), not zero. A trapped particle always has some kinetic energy, as the uncertainty principle requires. This is called the zero-point energy.

Example. An electron in a box of \(L = 1\) nm: \(E_1 = \frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})^2}{8\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times(10^{-9})^2} = \frac{4.39\times10^{-67}}{7.29\times10^{-48}} = 6.0\times10^{-20}\) J \(= 0.376\) eV. Then \(E_2 = 1.50\) eV and \(E_3 = 3.38\) eV.

Remember (and draw this way)
  • \(E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}\), \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\); \(\psi_n = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L)\).
  • Level spacing grows as \(n^2\). Smaller box or lighter particle means bigger spacing.
  • Every \(\psi_n\) is zero at both walls. \(\psi_n\) has \(n\) half-waves and \(n - 1\) nodes. \(\psi_1\) is a single hump, highest in the middle.
  • \(|\psi|^2\) is never negative.

1F · Quantum statistics

About 15 minutes. Lecture 1.5.

A solid has an enormous number of electrons. Quantum statistics says how many particles, on average, sit in a state of energy \(E\) at temperature \(T\). There are three kinds of particle, and each has its own rule.

StatisticsOccupation \(n(E)\)Applies to
Maxwell–Boltzmann\(e^{-(E-\mu)/k_BT}\)classical particles that can be told apart; valid at high temperature or low density
Fermi–Dirac\(\dfrac{1}{e^{(E-\mu)/k_BT} + 1}\)fermions (electrons, protons, neutrons). The Pauli exclusion principle allows at most one per state
Bose–Einstein\(\dfrac{1}{e^{(E-\mu)/k_BT} - 1}\)bosons (photons, phonons, helium-4 atoms). Any number can share a state, giving Bose–Einstein condensation

Here \(\mu\) (mu) is the chemical potential. For electrons at \(T = 0\) it is the Fermi energy \(E_F\) (the Fermi level), the energy of the highest filled state.

Reading the Fermi–Dirac formula

The "+1" in the bottom keeps \(n(E)\) between 0 and 1, which is the Pauli principle: a state holds at most one electron (of each spin).

At \(T = 0\): for \(E < \mu\) the exponent is \(-\infty\), so \(n = \frac1{0+1} = 1\); for \(E > \mu\) it is \(+\infty\), so \(n = 0\). It is a step: every state below \(E_F\) full, every state above empty.

At \(E = \mu\): \(n = \frac1{1+1} = \frac12\) at any temperature.

Warmer: the step is smeared over a few \(k_BT\). Example at 300 K, 0.1 eV above \(\mu\): \(\frac{0.1}{0.0259} = 3.86\), \(e^{3.86} = 47.5\), so \(n = \frac1{48.5} = 0.021\).

Far above \(\mu\) (\(E - \mu\gg k_BT\)) the exponential is huge, the "+1" does not matter, and \(n\approx e^{-(E-\mu)/k_BT}\), the Maxwell–Boltzmann form. ARPES (4B) sees this step as the "Fermi cut-off".

1G · Crystals

About 30 minutes. Lecture 1.6, and the "Derivation of hkl" reading.

A crystal is a solid whose atoms repeat in a regular 3D pattern. The repeating points form a lattice. Every lattice point can be reached from the origin by whole-number steps along three primitive vectors \(\vec a_1, \vec a_2, \vec a_3\).

\[\vec R = n_1\vec a_1 + n_2\vec a_2 + n_3\vec a_3,\qquad n_1, n_2, n_3\text{ whole numbers.}\]

The smallest box that builds the whole crystal by repetition is the unit cell. It is described by its edge lengths \(a, b, c\) (the lattice parameters) and the angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) between the edges. The atoms placed at each lattice point are called the motif (or basis).

There are 7 crystal systems and 14 Bravais lattices.

SystemEdges and anglesBravais latticesExample
Cubic\(a = b = c\), all angles 90°simple, body-centred, face-centredNaCl, Fe, Cu
Tetragonal\(a = b\neq c\), all 90°simple, body-centredSnO₂, TiO₂
Orthorhombic\(a\neq b\neq c\), all 90°simple, base-, body-, face-centredsulfur
Monoclinic\(a\neq b\neq c\), \(\alpha = \gamma = 90^\circ\neq\beta\)simple, base-centredgypsum
Triclinic\(a\neq b\neq c\), no angle 90°simpleK₂Cr₂O₇
Hexagonal\(a = b\neq c\), \(\alpha = \beta = 90^\circ\), \(\gamma = 120^\circ\)simplegraphite, ZnO
Rhombohedral (trigonal)\(a = b = c\), equal angles not 90°simplecalcite, quartz

The three cubic cells

Atoms at a corner are shared by 8 cells, so each corner counts \(\frac18\). An atom on a face is shared by 2 cells and counts \(\frac12\). An atom in the body counts 1.

CellAtomsAtoms per cellNearest neighboursPacking efficiency
Simple cubic (SC)8 corners\(8\times\frac18 = 1\)652%
Body-centred cubic (BCC)8 corners + 1 at the centre\(1 + 1 = 2\)868%
Face-centred cubic (FCC)8 corners + 6 face centres\(1 + 6\times\frac12 = 4\)1274%

The packing efficiency is the fraction of the cell volume filled by the atoms, treated as touching spheres. FCC and hexagonal close-packed (HCP) tie for the highest value, 74%. Both are stacks of the densest possible layers of spheres. If a question lists both FCC and HCP, say so in a line, and choose FCC if only one answer is allowed (the 2025 papers listed this question; the examiners' key is not public).

Planes and Miller indices

Planes of atoms are named by three whole numbers \((hkl)\), the Miller indices. To find them: (1) write where the plane cuts the three axes, in units of the cell edges; (2) take the reciprocal of each (a plane parallel to an axis cuts it at \(\infty\), reciprocal 0); (3) multiply up to the smallest whole numbers. A bar over a number means it is negative. For example, a plane cutting at \(1, \infty, \infty\) is \((100)\); at \(1, 1, \infty\) it is \((110)\); at \(1, 1, 1\) it is \((111)\); at \(\frac12, 1, \infty\): reciprocals \(2, 1, 0\), so \((210)\). In a cubic crystal the spacing between neighbouring \((hkl)\) planes is \(d_{hkl} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}\). This \(d\) goes into Bragg's law \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\) for X-ray diffraction (4D).

The reciprocal lattice and the Brillouin zone

Waves in a crystal are labelled by their wavevector \(\vec k\). The natural grid for \(\vec k\) is the reciprocal lattice, the Fourier transform of the real lattice. Its vectors are

\[\vec b_1 = 2\pi\frac{\vec a_2\times\vec a_3}{\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)},\]\[\vec b_2 = 2\pi\frac{\vec a_3\times\vec a_1}{\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)},\]\[\vec b_3 = 2\pi\frac{\vec a_1\times\vec a_2}{\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)}.\]
Reading the formula

The bottom, \(\vec a_1\cdot(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3)\), is the volume of the unit cell. The cross product on top makes \(\vec b_1\) perpendicular to \(\vec a_2\) and \(\vec a_3\). Together these give \(\vec a_i\cdot\vec b_j = 2\pi\) when \(i = j\) and 0 otherwise.

Example, simple cubic with \(\vec a_1 = a\hat x, \vec a_2 = a\hat y, \vec a_3 = a\hat z\). Then \(\vec a_2\times\vec a_3 = a^2\hat x\) and the volume is \(a^3\), so \(\vec b_1 = 2\pi\frac{a^2\hat x}{a^3} = \frac{2\pi}a\hat x\). The reciprocal lattice is also simple cubic, with side \(\frac{2\pi}a\). A big real cell gives a small reciprocal cell.

Any reciprocal lattice vector is \(\vec G = h\vec b_1 + k\vec b_2 + l\vec b_3\) with whole numbers \(h, k, l\). It is perpendicular to the \((hkl)\) planes.

Remember the pairs: the reciprocal of SC is SC, the reciprocal of BCC is FCC, and the reciprocal of FCC is BCC. The reciprocal lattice is used for Brillouin zones, X-ray diffraction (the Laue conditions) and band structures.

The first Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. To draw it, mark the reciprocal lattice points, draw the perpendicular bisector of the line from the origin to each neighbouring point, and take the smallest region around the origin enclosed by them. It contains every distinct \(\vec k\) once. For a square lattice it is a square; for a hexagonal lattice, a hexagon.

Bloch's theorem

Inside a crystal the potential energy repeats with the lattice: \(V(\vec r + \vec R) = V(\vec r)\). Bloch's theorem says the solutions of the Schrödinger equation then have this form.

\[\psi_{n\vec k}(\vec r) = e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}\,u_{n\vec k}(\vec r),\qquad u_{n\vec k}(\vec r + \vec R) = u_{n\vec k}(\vec r).\]
Reading the formula

\(e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}\) is a plane wave, like a free electron. \(u_{n\vec k}\) is a function with the period of the lattice, peaked near each atom. So an electron in a crystal is a plane wave whose height is modulated by a function that repeats every lattice spacing. The label \(n\) numbers the energy bands, and \(\vec k\) can be restricted to the first Brillouin zone.

Why it matters. It explains why electrons in a crystal do not behave like free electrons, and it leads to energy bands and band gaps (1H). It is the foundation of solid-state physics.

1H · Bonds, bands and effective mass

About 20 minutes. Lectures 1.6 and 1.7, and the "explanation on bonds" reading.

Bonds in solids

Atoms hold together because their electrons rearrange. Real solids are a mix of five kinds of bond.

BondHow it worksPropertiesExamples
Ionicone atom gives electrons to another; the + and − ions attractlarge electronegativity difference, closed shells, large band gap (over 3 eV), insulatingNaCl, MgO
Covalentneighbouring atoms share electronsstrong and directional; sp³ hybrid bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron; the (8 − N) rule gives the number of bonds; bonding states form the valence band and antibonding states the conduction banddiamond, Si, Ge
Metallica "sea" of free electrons moves between positive ion coresgood electrical and thermal conductors, shiny, ductile; conduction falls as temperature risesCu, Al, Fe
Van der Waalsweak attraction between induced dipolesweak; holds layered and molecular solids togethergraphite (between layers), solid argon
HydrogenH attached to an electronegative atom (O, N, F) attracts another such atomstronger than van der Waals, weaker than covalentwater (ice), DNA base pairs

Energy bands

An isolated atom has sharp energy levels. Bring \(N\) atoms together and, because of the Pauli principle, each level splits into \(N\) levels very close together. With about \(10^{23}\) atoms these form continuous bands. Between bands there can be band gaps, energies no electron can have. Bloch's theorem (1G) is the formal reason.

  • The valence band is the highest band that is filled at 0 K. The conduction band is the next one up, empty at 0 K. The band gap is \(E_g = E_c - E_v\), the bottom of the conduction band minus the top of the valence band.
  • Metal: the bands overlap or one is half full, so there is no gap and electrons move freely.
  • Semiconductor: a small gap (about 0.5 to 3 eV, Si 1.1 eV, GaAs 1.42 eV). Heat lifts some electrons across, so raising the temperature increases the number of carriers and the conductivity.
  • Insulator: a large gap (over about 3 eV). Almost no electrons can cross.

Direct and indirect gaps. Plot energy against \(k\). If the bottom of the conduction band and the top of the valence band are at the same \(k\), the gap is direct (GaAs, monolayer MoS₂). An electron can drop across and emit a photon directly, so direct-gap materials make good LEDs and lasers. If they are at different \(k\), the gap is indirect (Si). The electron must also give momentum to a lattice vibration (a phonon), so light emission is weak.

Effective mass

An electron in a crystal responds to a force as if it had a different mass, the effective mass \(m^*\). The crystal's effect is folded into this one number, so the electron can then be treated as free.

\[m^* = \frac{\hbar^2}{d^2E/dk^2}\]
Reading the formula

\(\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}\) is the curvature of the band. A sharply curved band gives a small \(m^*\) (a light, fast electron, high mobility). A flat band gives a large \(m^*\). Near the top of a band the curvature is negative, so \(m^*\) is negative there, which is why we describe those states with positive "holes". \(m^*\) can also differ by direction.

Check with a free electron. \(E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\), so \(\frac{dE}{dk} = \frac{\hbar^2k}m\) and \(\frac{d^2E}{dk^2} = \frac{\hbar^2}m\). Then \(m^* = \frac{\hbar^2}{\hbar^2/m} = m\), as it should be. Typical values are 0.01 to 10 times \(m_e\); in GaAs \(m^* = 0.067m_e\).

1I · Density of states in 3D

About 30 minutes. Lecture 1.7. Quiz 1 asked a variant (count the states in a cube instead of a sphere).

The density of states \(Z(E)\) is the number of allowed electron states per unit volume per unit energy near the energy \(E\). It tells you how many seats are available at each energy; Fermi–Dirac (1F) tells you how many are filled. Multiplying the two gives the number of electrons at each energy.

The derivation uses a cube of side \(L\) as a 3D particle in a box. Each state has three whole numbers \(n_x, n_y, n_z\ge1\) and energy

\[E = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}(n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2) = \frac{h^2n^2}{8mL^2},\qquad n^2 = n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2.\]

Think of each state as a point in a 3D grid with axes \(n_x, n_y, n_z\). All states with energy below \(E\) lie inside a sphere of radius \(n\). Only the part with all three numbers positive counts, which is \(\frac18\) of the sphere.

Write this in the exam

Derive the density of states for a bulk (3D) solid and show that \(Z(E)\propto E^{1/2}\).

  1. States inside radius \(n\): \(\frac18\cdot\frac43\pi n^3\). Between \(n\) and \(n + dn\): \(\frac18\cdot4\pi n^2dn = \frac\pi2n^2dn\).
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Each state is one point of the grid, and each point takes up one unit of volume in \(n\)-space. So counting states means measuring volume.
    2. A sphere of radius \(n\) has volume \(\frac43\pi n^3\). Only the positive octant counts, so \(\frac18\cdot\frac43\pi n^3\).
    3. The states with energy between \(E\) and \(E + dE\) lie in a thin shell between \(n\) and \(n + dn\). Its volume is the difference \(\frac18\cdot\frac43\pi[(n+dn)^3 - n^3]\).
    4. Expand \((n + dn)^3 = n^3 + 3n^2dn + \dots\) and drop the tiny terms with \(dn^2\) and \(dn^3\). The shell holds \(\frac18\cdot\frac43\pi\cdot3n^2dn = \frac\pi2n^2dn\) states.
  2. From \(E = \frac{h^2n^2}{8mL^2}\): \(n^2 = \frac{8mL^2}{h^2}E\) and \(dn = \frac12\left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{1/2}E^{-1/2}dE\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Rearrange the energy formula for \(n^2\): multiply by \(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\).
    2. Take the square root: \(n = \left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{1/2}E^{1/2}\).
    3. Differentiate with respect to \(E\). The derivative of \(E^{1/2}\) is \(\frac12E^{-1/2}\), so \(dn = \frac12\left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{1/2}E^{-1/2}dE\).
  3. Substitute: \(\frac\pi2n^2dn = \frac\pi4\left(\frac{8m}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}L^3E^{1/2}dE\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. \(\frac\pi2\cdot\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}E\cdot\frac12\left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{1/2}E^{-1/2}dE\).
    2. Collect the numbers: \(\frac\pi2\cdot\frac12 = \frac\pi4\). Collect the brackets: power \(1 + \frac12 = \frac32\). Collect \(E\): power \(1 - \frac12 = \frac12\).
    3. \(\left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{3/2} = \left(\frac{8m}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}L^3\), since \((L^2)^{3/2} = L^3\).
  4. Pauli allows two electrons (spin up and down) per state: multiply by 2.
  5. Divide by the volume \(V = L^3\) to get states per unit volume: \[Z(E)\,dE = \frac\pi2\left(\frac{8m}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}\,dE,\qquad Z_{3D}(E)\propto E^{1/2}.\]
  6. The same in the form used in lecture 2.1: \(Z_{3D}(E) = \frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}\), with the effective mass \(m^*\) in place of \(m\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Replace \(h = 2\pi\hbar\): \(\frac{8m}{h^2} = \frac{8m}{4\pi^2\hbar^2} = \frac{2m}{\pi^2\hbar^2}\).
    2. Raise to the \(\frac32\): \(\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\cdot\frac1{\pi^3}\).
    3. Multiply by \(\frac\pi2\): \(\frac{\pi}{2\pi^3} = \frac1{2\pi^2}\). Both forms are the same.
Reading the result

\(Z(E)\propto\sqrt E\): there are few states near the bottom of the band and more and more as the energy rises, because the shell in \(n\)-space gets bigger as its radius grows. The graph is a rising curve that bends over (see 2B). The \(m^{3/2}\) says heavier electrons have more states per energy, because their levels are closer together.

  2. Put in \(n\) from line 2: \(N = \left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{3/2}\).
  3. States between \(E\) and \(E + dE\): \(\frac{dN}{dE}dE = \frac32\left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}dE\).
  4. Multiply by 2 for spin and divide by \(L^3\): \(Z(E) = 3\left(\frac{8m}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}\).
  5. Still \(Z(E)\propto E^{1/2}\). Only the number in front changes (3 instead of \(\frac\pi2\)). The power of \(E\) is set by the number of dimensions, not by the shape of the region.
  6. Check the units of both: the same \(\left(\frac{8m}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}\) appears, so they agree.

1J · How carriers move

About 15 minutes. Lecture 1.7, carrier transport. The 2025 end-sem had three questions on this.

Charge is carried by electrons and by holes (missing electrons in the valence band, which act as positive charges). The slide lists six ways they move.

MechanismWhat happensFormula or where it matters
Driftan electric field \(E\) pushes carriers; they keep scattering off impurities, phonons and defects, so they settle at an average drift velocity\(v_d = \mu E\), where \(\mu\) is the mobility; current density \(J_{\text{drift}} = qn\mu_nE + qp\mu_pE\)
Diffusioncarriers spread from high to low concentration\(J_{\text{diff}} = qD_n\frac{dn}{dx} - qD_p\frac{dp}{dx}\); Einstein relation \(\frac D\mu = \frac{k_BT}q\)
Thermionic emissioncarriers gain enough thermal energy to go over a barriermetal–semiconductor junctions, heterostructures, a hot filament
Tunnellingcarriers pass through a thin barrier without enough energy to go over ittunnel diodes, thin MOSFET gates, the STM (4A)
Ballistic transportcarriers cross the device without scattering, because the mean free path is at least the device lengthnanowires, graphene, 2D materials
Hoppingin disordered materials carriers jump between localised statesamorphous and organic semiconductors

In the formulas, \(q\) is the carrier charge, \(n\) and \(p\) are the electron and hole concentrations, and \(D\) is the diffusion coefficient. The minus sign for holes in \(J_{\text{diff}}\) is because holes are positive: when both move down their gradients, they carry current in opposite directions.
