2A · Wells, wires and dots

About 15 minutes. Lecture 2.1.

When a material is made so thin that its size is close to the de Broglie wavelength of its electrons (a few nanometres), the electrons behave like a particle in a box in that direction (1E). Their energy in that direction becomes a set of discrete levels. This is quantum confinement. Low-dimensional systems are also cleaner than doped bulk material, which is disordered by its ionised impurities, and quantum effects are stronger in them.

Bulk (3D)no directionx, y, zlarge\(\propto E^{1/2}\)

"Dimension" counts the directions of free motion. A quantum well is called 2D because electrons move freely in two directions, even though it is confined in one. Note the trap: a quantum well confines carriers in one dimension.

Quantum wells. A thin layer of a low-band-gap semiconductor sandwiched between layers of a higher-gap one (for example GaAs between AlGaAs). Properties: discrete levels across the well, continuous motion along it, stronger radiative recombination, lower laser threshold current, better temperature stability. Material pairs: GaAs/AlGaAs (near infrared), InGaAs/InP (telecom, 1.3 to 1.55 µm), InGaN/GaN (blue and UV LEDs), GaInP/AlGaInP (red and yellow LEDs).

Quantum wires. Properties: van Hove singularities in the density of states, larger exciton binding energy, polarised light emission, ballistic transport in short wires, and quantised conductance in steps of \(\frac{2e^2}h\). Made by e-beam lithography and etching, self-organised growth (Stranski–Krastanov), growth on patterned substrates (V-grooves, cleaved-edge overgrowth) or templates (carbon nanotubes, porous alumina, DNA). Challenges: uniform size, clean interfaces, scaling up, making electrical contacts.

Quantum dots are "artificial atoms": confined in every direction, they have only discrete levels. Smaller dots have larger level spacing (1E: \(E\propto\frac1{L^2}\)), so they emit bluer light. The colour of a quantum-dot LED is tuned by the dot size. Properties: size-tunable emission, quantum yield up to 95%, narrow emission (width under 30 nm), resistance to photobleaching, strong absorption. Types: colloidal dots made in solution (CdSe/ZnS core–shell, 450 to 650 nm; InP/ZnS, cadmium-free, 500 to 700 nm; PbS, 900 to 2000 nm infrared), self-assembled dots grown epitaxially (InAs on GaAs), and carbon dots (biocompatible).

2B · Density of states in 2D, 1D and 0D

About 35 minutes. Lecture 2.1. This was a 10-mark question in the 2025 end-sem (Q36).

The method is the one in 1I, with the sphere replaced by a quarter circle (2D) or a line (1D). For a quantum well of area \(L\times L\), the free motion has \(E = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}(n_x^2 + n_y^2) = \frac{h^2n^2}{8mL^2}\). For a wire of length \(L\), \(E = \frac{h^2n^2}{8mL^2}\) with one number \(n\).

Write this in the exam

Derive the density of states for 2D and 1D electron systems, and write it for 0D.

2D (quantum well), per unit area

  1. States inside radius \(n\): \(\frac14\pi n^2\) (positive quadrant). Between \(n\) and \(n + dn\): \(\frac14\cdot2\pi n\,dn = \frac\pi2n\,dn\).
  2. From \(n^2 = \frac{8mL^2}{h^2}E\): \(2n\,dn = \frac{8mL^2}{h^2}dE\), so \(n\,dn = \frac{4mL^2}{h^2}dE\).
  3. States in \(dE\): \(\frac\pi2\cdot\frac{4mL^2}{h^2}dE = \frac{2\pi mL^2}{h^2}dE\). Times 2 for spin, divided by the area \(L^2\): \[Z_{2D}(E) = \frac{4\pi m^*}{h^2} = \frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2}.\]
    This is independent of \(E\).
    Why (covers lines 1 to 3, 6 steps)
    1. In 2D the states are points in a flat grid \((n_x, n_y)\). States with energy below \(E\) lie inside a circle of radius \(n\), and only the quarter with both numbers positive counts: area \(\frac14\pi n^2\).
    2. The thin ring between \(n\) and \(n + dn\) has area \(\frac14\pi[(n+dn)^2 - n^2] = \frac14\pi(2n\,dn)\), dropping the tiny \(dn^2\). That is \(\frac\pi2n\,dn\).
    3. Differentiate \(n^2 = \frac{8mL^2}{h^2}E\) on both sides: \(2n\,dn = \frac{8mL^2}{h^2}dE\). Halve both sides: \(n\,dn = \frac{4mL^2}{h^2}dE\). (Here the whole product \(n\,dn\) is needed, which is why the \(n\) cancels.)
    4. Substitute: \(\frac\pi2\cdot\frac{4mL^2}{h^2}dE = \frac{2\pi mL^2}{h^2}dE\).
    5. Two spins per state: \(\frac{4\pi mL^2}{h^2}dE\). Divide by the area \(L^2\): \(\frac{4\pi m}{h^2}\).
    6. With \(h = 2\pi\hbar\): \(\frac{4\pi m}{4\pi^2\hbar^2} = \frac m{\pi\hbar^2}\). No \(E\) is left, so the 2D density of states is a constant.
  4. Each confined level \(E_n\) of the well starts its own subband, so the total is a staircase: \(Z_{2D,\text{total}} = \frac{m^*}{\pi\hbar^2}\sum_n\Theta(E - E_n)\), where \(\Theta\) is a step that switches on at \(E_n\).

1D (quantum wire), per unit length

  1. States lie on a line; between \(n\) and \(n + dn\) there are \(dn\) states.
  2. \(n = \left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{1/2}E^{1/2}\), so \(dn = \frac12\left(\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}\right)^{1/2}E^{-1/2}dE = \frac{L\sqrt{2m}}hE^{-1/2}dE\).
  3. Times 2 for spin, divided by \(L\): \[Z_{1D}(E) = \frac2h\sqrt{\frac{2m^*}E} = \frac1{\pi\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{2m^*}E}\ \propto\ E^{-1/2}.\]
    Why (covers lines 1 to 3, 5 steps)
    1. In 1D there is only \(n_x = n\), so the states are evenly spaced points on a line, one per unit of \(n\). Between \(n\) and \(n + dn\) there are \(dn\) of them.
    2. Solve \(E = \frac{h^2n^2}{8mL^2}\) for \(n\) and differentiate, as in 1I line 2.
    3. Simplify the constant: \(\frac12\sqrt{\frac{8mL^2}{h^2}} = \frac12\cdot\frac{2L\sqrt{2m}}h = \frac{L\sqrt{2m}}h\), since \(\sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2\).
    4. Multiply by 2 for spin and divide by the length \(L\): \(\frac{2\sqrt{2m}}hE^{-1/2}\).
    5. With \(h = 2\pi\hbar\) this is \(\frac{\sqrt{2m}}{\pi\hbar}E^{-1/2}\). It falls as \(\frac1{\sqrt E}\) and is infinite at \(E = 0\) (a van Hove singularity). The slide writes it as \(\frac L{\pi\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{m^*}{2E}}\), the same without the spin factor 2 and per whole wire; the \(E^{-1/2}\) is what matters.
  4. Each subband of the wire adds its own \(\frac1{\sqrt{E - E_n}}\) spike, so the total is a series of spikes that each fall off.

0D (quantum dot)

  1. No direction is free, so there is no \(n\)-space to count. Only the discrete energies \(E_i = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}(n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2)\) exist, each holding 2 electrons: \[Z_{0D}(E) = 2\sum_i\delta(E - E_i).\]

The power of \(E\) goes \(\frac12, 0, -\frac12\) as the dimension goes 3, 2, 1: each dimension removed lowers the power by \(\frac12\). Fewer dimensions concentrate the states at the band edge, which is why quantum wells make better lasers (3A).

2C · Graphene and other 2D materials

About 20 minutes. Lecture 2.1.

A 2D material is a crystal one or a few atoms thick (about 0.3 to 1 nm) but large sideways.

Graphene

Graphene is one layer of carbon atoms in a honeycomb (hexagonal) lattice. It was isolated in 2004 by Geim and Novoselov (Nobel Prize 2010) by peeling graphite with sticky tape. Its numbers: mobility above 200,000 cm²/V s at room temperature, strength 130 GPa (200 times steel), thermal conductivity above 5000 W/m K, 97.7% of light transmitted, and current density about \(10^8\) A/cm² (1000 times copper). It shows a quantum Hall effect even at room temperature.

Graphene has zero band gap. The valence and conduction bands touch at points called the Dirac points, at the corners K and K′ of its hexagonal Brillouin zone. Near them the energy is linear in momentum, not quadratic:

\[E = \pm\hbar v_F|\vec k|,\qquad v_F\approx10^6\text{ m/s}\approx\frac c{300}.\]
Reading the formula

For an ordinary electron \(E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m^*}\), a parabola. Graphene's \(E = \hbar v_F|k|\) is a cone (a Dirac cone), the same form as light, \(E = pc\). By the effective mass formula (1H), a straight line has zero curvature, so the electrons act as massless Dirac fermions moving at the fixed speed \(v_F\), about 1/300 of the speed of light. The \(\pm\) gives the upper (conduction) and lower (valence) cones. Graphene is called a semimetal. Its problem for logic chips is the lack of a gap.

Uses: transparent electrodes (touch screens, solar cells, OLED displays), fast RF and terahertz electronics, composites, supercapacitors and batteries. Challenges: opening a band gap, making large clean sheets, transferring them without contamination, and cost.

Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)

TMDs have the formula MX₂: M is a transition metal (Mo, W) and X a chalcogen (S, Se, Te). Each layer is an X–M–X sandwich about 0.7 nm thick, and layers are held together by weak van der Waals forces. Examples: MoS₂, WS₂, MoSe₂, WSe₂, MoTe₂. Their key property: the band gap is indirect in the bulk but becomes direct in a single layer, which makes monolayers good for light emission (optoelectronics). Gaps: MoS₂ 1.8 eV, WS₂ 2.0 eV, WSe₂ 1.6 eV, MoTe₂ 1.0 eV. Other properties: two valleys K and K′ that can store information (valleytronics), strong spin–orbit coupling, strongly bound excitons (0.5 to 1 eV), piezoelectricity. Mobility 1 to 200 cm²/V s, on/off ratio \(10^6\) to \(10^8\). Uses: low-power transistors, photodetectors, LEDs, single-photon sources, memristors, gas sensors, catalysis.

Other 2D materials: h-BN (hexagonal boron nitride), a wide-gap insulator used as a substrate; phosphorene (black phosphorus), with a tunable gap and properties that differ by direction.

Making them. Mechanical exfoliation (the sticky-tape method: best quality, small flakes), liquid-phase exfoliation (large amounts, lower quality), chemical vapour deposition (large area, controlled thickness, scalable for industry) and molecular beam epitaxy (highest quality, atomic precision, expensive).

Heterostructures and twisted bilayers

Different 2D layers can be stacked like Lego into van der Waals heterostructures: graphene on h-BN (higher mobility, less disorder), graphene on a TMD (charge transfer), TMD on TMD (type-II alignment and interlayer excitons, 3B).

Stacking two layers with a small twist angle \(\theta_t\) makes a larger repeating pattern called a moiré pattern, with period

\[\lambda_M\approx\frac{a}{2\sin(\theta_t/2)}.\]

In twisted bilayer graphene at the "magic angle" \(\theta_t\approx1.1^\circ\), the bands become nearly flat. Flat bands mean the electrons' kinetic energy is tiny, so their repulsion dominates, giving correlated (Mott-like) insulators and superconductivity. The bandwidth can be tuned electrically. Magic-angle twisted trilayer graphene shows even more robust superconductivity.

2D · The Hall effects

About 25 minutes. Lecture 2.2. A 10-mark question in the 2025 mid-sem and a "find the errors" question in the end-sem.

In every Hall effect, a current flows along a strip and a voltage or current appears across it. They differ in what pushes the carriers sideways.

Reading the Hall voltage formula

\(V_H = \frac{BI}{ned}\). \(B\) is the field, \(I\) the current, \(n\) the number of carriers per volume, \(e\) the charge and \(d\) the thickness. Why: carriers pile up on one edge until the electric field \(E_H\) of the pile-up balances the magnetic force, \(eE_H = evB\). The current is \(I = nevA\), with \(A = wd\) (width times thickness), so \(v = \frac I{newd}\) and \(V_H = E_Hw = vBw = \frac{BI}{ned}\). Fewer carriers give a bigger voltage, which is why semiconductors show a much bigger Hall voltage than metals. The sign of \(V_H\) tells you whether the carriers are electrons or holes.

Example. \(B = 0.5\) T, \(I = 1\) mA, \(n = 10^{22}\) m⁻³, \(d = 1\) µm: \(V_H = \frac{0.5\times10^{-3}}{10^{22}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{-6}} = \frac{5\times10^{-4}}{1.6\times10^{-3}} = 0.31\) V.

2E · Topological materials

About 35 minutes. Lectures 2.2 and 2.3.

Topology, in one paragraph

Topology studies properties that do not change when a shape is smoothly stretched or bent without tearing. A coffee mug and a doughnut are the same topologically (each has one hole), but a sphere is different (no hole). The number of holes is a whole number, so it cannot change gradually: it can only jump. In a topological material, the electron bands have a whole-number property of this kind, called a topological invariant. Because it cannot change smoothly, small disorder cannot change it, and the special states it guarantees are protected.

Three ingredients

Time-reversal symmetry (TRS). Imagine running a film of the electrons backwards (\(t\to-t\)). Momentum flips and spin flips. If the physics looks the same, the system has time-reversal symmetry. A magnetic field or magnetisation breaks it, because reversing time would also reverse the field. So doping a material with magnetic atoms breaks TRS.

Spin–orbit coupling (SOC). An electron orbiting a nucleus sees, in its own frame, the charged nucleus going round it, which acts like a current and makes a magnetic field. That field couples to the electron's spin, so spin and orbital motion (\(\vec L\) and \(\vec S\), adding to \(\vec J = \vec L + \vec S\)) are linked. SOC is strong in heavy elements such as bismuth.

Berry phase. When a quantum state is carried slowly around a closed loop in some parameter space and comes back to where it started, it can pick up an extra phase that depends only on the geometry of the loop, not on how long it took. This is the Berry phase: a geometric phase acquired over a closed loop. For electrons in a crystal the loop is in momentum space. The building blocks are the Berry connection \(\vec A_n(\vec k) = i\langle u_n|\nabla_{\vec k}u_n\rangle\), the Berry curvature \(\Omega_n = \nabla_{\vec k}\times\vec A_n\), and the Berry phase \(\gamma_n = \oint\vec A_n\cdot d\vec k\). Adding up the Berry curvature over the whole Brillouin zone and dividing by \(2\pi\) gives a whole number, the Chern number \(C\). The quantum Hall conductance is \(\sigma_{xy} = C\frac{e^2}h\). For materials with time-reversal symmetry the invariant is the \(\mathbb Z_2\) number, which is 0 (ordinary) or 1 (topological).

Topological insulators

A topological insulator (TI) is an insulator inside (a bulk band gap) but conducts on its surface. The cause is band inversion: strong spin–orbit coupling pushes the bands past each other, so their usual order is swapped. Where the inverted inside meets the ordinary outside (vacuum), the gap must close, and this gives metallic surface states.

  • The surface states form a Dirac cone: energy linear in momentum, crossing at the Dirac point, which sits inside the bulk gap.
  • Spin–momentum locking: on the surface, an electron's spin is always perpendicular to its momentum. Electrons moving the opposite way have opposite spin.
  • So an electron cannot simply bounce backwards off a non-magnetic impurity, which would need its spin to flip. The surface states are protected by time-reversal symmetry.
  • Well-known 3D TIs: Bi₂Se₃ and Bi₂Te₃. ARPES (4B) measured the Dirac cone, confirming the prediction, and spin-resolved ARPES showed the two branches have opposite spin.
  • Adding magnetic impurities breaks time-reversal symmetry and opens a small gap at the Dirac point: a "massive Dirac fermion".

Semimetals: Dirac and Weyl

A semimetal sits between a semiconductor and a metal: its conduction and valence bands just touch or overlap slightly at the Fermi level.

Higher-order topological insulators (HOTI)

An ordinary (first-order) TI in \(d\) dimensions has protected states on its \((d-1)\)-dimensional boundary: a 2D TI has edge states, a 3D TI has surface states. An \(n\)th-order TI has protected states on boundaries of dimension \(d - n\). So a 2D second-order TI has states only at its corners. A 3D second-order TI has states along its hinges (edges where two faces meet). A 3D third-order TI has states at its corners. The bulk and the faces stay insulating.

2F · Superconductors

About 40 minutes. Lectures 2.3 and 2.4. The 2025 end-sem had a 10-mark question on this (Q37).

A superconductor has exactly zero electrical resistance below a critical temperature \(T_c\), and pushes magnetic field out of itself. Heike Kamerlingh Onnes discovered it in 1911: the resistance of mercury vanished below 4.2 K, after he had learned to liquefy helium.

The transition is a phase change. Properties change abruptly at \(T_c\). The heat capacity jumps: in the normal state \(C_v\propto T\), at \(T_c\) it jumps up, and below \(T_c\) it falls off exponentially, as \(e^{-\Delta/k_BT}\).

The Meissner effect

Discovered by W. Meissner and R. Ochsenfeld in Germany in 1933. Put a sample in a weak magnetic field (\(H < H_c\)) and cool it below \(T_c\). The field is spontaneously pushed out of the bulk. It penetrates only a thin skin, the London penetration depth \(\lambda\approx100\) nm, decaying exponentially inside. Inside, \(B = 0\), so the material is a perfect diamagnet with susceptibility \(\chi = -1\).

This is more than zero resistance. A perfect conductor would only resist changes of field (Lenz's law): a field already inside before cooling would stay trapped. A superconductor expels the field even if it was there before cooling. So the Meissner effect is a property of the superconducting state itself, not ordinary diamagnetism. It is also how a superconductor levitates a magnet.

Critical field and the two types

A strong enough field destroys superconductivity. The critical field depends on temperature as

\[H_c(T) = H_0\left[1 - \left(\frac T{T_c}\right)^2\right].\]
Reading the formula

\(H_0\) is the critical field at 0 K, the largest it can be. At \(T = T_c\), the bracket is \(1 - 1 = 0\), so any field destroys it. The curve is a downward parabola and separates the superconducting region (below it) from the normal region. Why this shape: the superconducting state has lower free energy, but a field raises its free energy by an amount that grows as \(H^2\), while the normal state's free energy is hardly affected. Superconductivity is lost when the two become equal.

Example. At \(T = \frac{T_c}2\): \(H_c = H_0(1 - \frac14) = \frac34H_0\).

Most first-generation superconductors only work below about 30 K.

BCS theory: why it happens

An early "two-fluid model" (normal plus superconducting electrons) had no firm basis and was dropped. The first successful quantum theory was BCS theory (1957), by John Bardeen, Leon Cooper and Robert Schrieffer at the University of Illinois (Nobel Prize 1972).

  • Below \(T_c\), electrons form Cooper pairs. A moving electron pulls the positive ions slightly towards it, leaving a region of extra positive charge, which attracts a second electron. So the pairing is caused by lattice vibrations (phonons): the electron–lattice interaction here helps rather than scatters.
  • The two electrons of a pair are about 100 nm apart, much more than the lattice spacing (0.1 to 0.4 nm). The pairs act together and move through the lattice without scattering, giving zero resistance.
  • The pair's binding energy must beat thermal vibrations, which is why superconductivity needs low temperature.
  • Evidence: the isotope effect. Heavier isotopes of the same metal have a lower \(T_c\) (\(T_c\propto M^{-1/2}\)), so the lattice must be involved. It is strong in Type I and weaker in Type II.

High-temperature superconductors

  • 1986: Georg Bednorz and Karl Müller (IBM Zurich) found superconductivity at 35 K in a lanthanum-based copper oxide (a cuprate). Nobel Prize 1987.
  • 1987: replacing La with Y gave YBa₂Cu₃O₇₋ₓ (YBCO) with \(T_c\approx92\) K, above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (77 K), so it can be cooled cheaply. Superconductivity disappears near \(x\approx0.6\), where the structure changes from orthorhombic to tetragonal.
  • Thallium and mercury cuprates have even higher \(T_c\) (HgBa₂Ca₂Cu₃O₈, 134 K). Lanthanum superhydride LaH₁₀ reaches about 250 K, but only under about 200 GPa of pressure.
  • All high-\(T_c\) superconductors are Type II. Their mechanism is still unsettled. Two proposals: spin fluctuations (antiferromagnetic, giving d-wave pairing, \(d_{x^2-y^2}\)) and interlayer coupling (BCS-like s-wave pairing helped by the layers).

Applications. Maglev trains (Meissner expulsion and strong superconducting magnets levitate and drive the train with no friction; running in Japan); MRI (strong uniform superconducting magnets); lossless power transmission (limited by the cost of cooling); superconducting YBCO tapes grown on nickel for strength (still being developed).

Josephson tunnelling

A Josephson junction is two superconductors separated by a thin insulating barrier (S–I–S). Cooper pairs tunnel through it, so a supercurrent flows with zero voltage, up to a critical current \(I_c\): \(I = I_c\sin\phi\), where \(\phi\) is the difference in quantum phase between the two superconductors. Leo Esaki, Ivar Giaever and Brian Josephson shared the 1973 Nobel Prize for tunnelling in semiconductors and superconductors.

  • Normal metal–insulator–normal metal (N–I–N): current rises in proportion to voltage.
  • Superconductor–insulator–normal (S–I–N): almost no current until \(V = \frac\Delta e\), where \(\Delta\) is the superconducting energy gap.
  • S–I–S: a vertical spike of supercurrent at \(V = 0\) (Josephson tunnelling of pairs), then single-electron (Giaever) tunnelling once \(V = \frac{2\Delta}e\).

Josephson junctions are the heart of SQUIDs and superconducting qubits (3D).
