01The one recipe

"Everything from here on is about computing \(L(\mathbf{x})\) for richer and richer data models. Not about inventing new decision rules."

\(L(\mathbf{x})=\dfrac{p(\mathbf{x};H_1)}{p(\mathbf{x};H_0)}\)→ take \(\ln\)→ cancel terms without \(\mathbf{x}\)→ isolate \(T(\mathbf{x}) \gtrless \gamma'\)→ find \(p(T;H_0),\ p(T;H_1)\)→ \(P_{FA},\ P_D,\ P_e\)

Monotone transformations

If \(g\) is strictly increasing, \(L\gtrless\gamma \iff g(L)\gtrless g(\gamma)\). So you may take logs, drop additive constants, and divide by positive constants. Dividing by something of unknown sign (an unknown amplitude \(A\)) flips the inequality and breaks the test. That is exactly why two-sided composite problems have no UMP test.

02Setup & error types

Lec 1. Binary hypothesis test: \(H_0: \mathbf{x}\sim p(\mathbf{x};H_0)\) vs \(H_1:\mathbf{x}\sim p(\mathbf{x};H_1)\), both fully known (a "simple" test).

A decision rule \(\delta:\mathbb{R}^N\to\{H_0,H_1\}\) is just a partition \(\mathbb{R}^N = R_0\cup R_1\). Designing an optimal detector means choosing the critical region \(R_1\).

\[P_{FA}=\int_{R_1}p(\mathbf{x};H_0)\,d\mathbf{x},\qquad P_D=\int_{R_1}p(\mathbf{x};H_1)\,d\mathbf{x},\qquad P_M=1-P_D.\]
H₀ trueH₁ true
decide H₀correct rejection, \(1-P_{FA}\)miss, \(P_M=\beta\) (Type II)
decide H₁false alarm, \(P_{FA}=\alpha\) (Type I, size)detection, \(P_D=1-\beta\) (power)

\(P_e = P(H_0)P_{FA} + P(H_1)P_M\). A trivial detector can make one error zero (always say \(H_0\): \(P_{FA}=0\) but \(P_D=0\)), so "optimal" only means something once you fix a scalar objective: \(P_e\)/Bayes risk, or \(P_D\) at fixed \(P_{FA}\).

Three ingredients, always
  1. A data model (PDF under each hypothesis)
  2. A criterion of optimality
  3. A performance analysis (closed-form \(P_{FA},P_D,P_e\), ROC)

Radar: errors are wildly asymmetric and priors are meaningless → Neyman–Pearson. Comms: errors are symmetric and priors are known by design → Bayes / min \(P_e\).

03Bayesian hypothesis testing

Lec 1–3, 6. The hypothesis is random with prior \(P(H_i)\); \(C_{ij}\) = cost of deciding \(H_i\) when \(H_j\) is true.

Derivation: minimising Bayes risk gives the LRT derive
\[\mathcal{R}=\sum_{i=0}^{1}\sum_{j=0}^{1}C_{ij}\,P(H_j)\Pr\{\text{decide }H_i\mid H_j\}.\]
  1. Write \(\Pr\{\text{decide }H_0\mid H_j\} = 1-\int_{R_1}p(\mathbf{x}|H_j)d\mathbf{x}\) and collect everything: \[\mathcal{R}=C_{00}P(H_0)+C_{01}P(H_1)+\int_{R_1}\Big[(C_{10}-C_{00})P(H_0)p(\mathbf{x}|H_0)-(C_{01}-C_{11})P(H_1)p(\mathbf{x}|H_1)\Big]d\mathbf{x}.\]
  2. The first two terms are fixed. To minimise, put \(\mathbf{x}\) in \(R_1\) exactly when the integrand is negative (pointwise minimisation).
  3. Assuming \(C_{10}>C_{00}\), \(C_{01}>C_{11}\) (errors cost more): \[L(\mathbf{x})=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}|H_1)}{p(\mathbf{x}|H_0)}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \frac{(C_{10}-C_{00})P(H_0)}{(C_{01}-C_{11})P(H_1)}=\gamma.\]

Special cases. \(C_{00}=C_{11}=0,\ C_{01}=C_{10}=1\) gives \(\mathcal{R}=P_e\) and \(\gamma=P(H_0)/P(H_1)\); equivalently decide the larger posterior \(P(H_i|\mathbf{x})\) (MAP). Equal priors give \(\gamma=1\) (ML).

Posterior-cost form (generalises to M hypotheses): decide \(H_1\) if \(C_{10}P(H_0|\mathbf{x})+C_{11}P(H_1|\mathbf{x}) < C_{00}P(H_0|\mathbf{x})+C_{01}P(H_1|\mathbf{x})\). For M hypotheses decide \(\arg\min_i C_i(\mathbf{x})\), \(C_i=\sum_j C_{ij}P(H_j|\mathbf{x})\).

Remember
Costs and priors only move the threshold. They never change the statistic.

Example A: DC level in WGN (the canonical one)

\(H_0: x[n]=w[n]\), \(H_1: x[n]=A+w[n]\), \(A>0\) known, \(w[n]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) iid, \(n=0..N-1\).

Detector and \(P_e\) derive
\[\ln L(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n\big[(x[n]-A)^2-x[n]^2\big]=\frac{A}{\sigma^2}\sum_n x[n]-\frac{NA^2}{2\sigma^2}\ \gtrless\ \ln\gamma\] \[\Rightarrow\quad T(\mathbf{x})=\bar{x}=\frac1N\sum_n x[n]\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma'=\frac{\sigma^2}{NA}\ln\gamma+\frac{A}{2}.\]

\(T\) is linear in Gaussian data: \(\bar x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2/N)\) under \(H_0\), \(\mathcal{N}(A,\sigma^2/N)\) under \(H_1\). So

\[P_{FA}=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right),\qquad P_M=1-Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-A}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right)=Q\!\left(\frac{A-\gamma'}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right).\]

Equal priors, min \(P_e\): \(\gamma=1\Rightarrow\gamma'=A/2\), and both errors equal:

\[P_e=Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{NA^2}{4\sigma^2}}\right)=Q\!\left(\tfrac12\sqrt{d^2}\right),\qquad d^2=\frac{NA^2}{\sigma^2}.\]

Moving \(\gamma'\) right lowers \(P_{FA}\) and raises \(P_M\). That is the basic trade-off.

Example B: two multivariate Gaussians, same covariance

\(H_0:\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu_0,C)\), \(H_1:\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu_1,C)\). The quadratic terms \(\mathbf{x}^TC^{-1}\mathbf{x}\) cancel, leaving a linear detector:

\[T(\mathbf{x})=(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)^TC^{-1}\mathbf{x}\ \gtrless\ \ln\gamma+\tfrac12\big(\boldsymbol\mu_1^TC^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0^TC^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_0\big).\]

With \(\mathbf h = C^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)\), \(T\mid H_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf h^T\boldsymbol\mu_i,\ \mathbf h^TC\mathbf h)\), so \(P_{FA}=Q\big(\frac{\gamma'-\mathbf h^T\boldsymbol\mu_0}{\sqrt{\mathbf h^TC\mathbf h}}\big)\), \(P_D=Q\big(\frac{\gamma'-\mathbf h^T\boldsymbol\mu_1}{\sqrt{\mathbf h^TC\mathbf h}}\big)\) and \(d^2=(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)^TC^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)\) (Mahalanobis distance).

Example C: DC level in Laplacian noise (classwork)

\(w[n]\sim\) Laplace\((0,b)\): \(f(w)=\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|w|/b}\). Then

\[\ln L(\mathbf{x})=\frac1b\sum_{n}\Big(|x[n]|-|x[n]-A|\Big)\ \gtrless\ \ln\gamma.\]

Each term \(g(x)=|x|-|x-A|\) is a limiter: \(-A\) for \(x\le 0\), \(2x-A\) for \(0\le x\le A\), \(+A\) for \(x\ge A\). So the detector clips each sample, then sums. The sample mean is not sufficient here: heavy-tailed noise makes big samples untrustworthy, and the clipper ignores how big they are. Performance has no clean closed form for general \(N\); for large \(N\) use the CLT on \(\sum g(x[n])\).

M hypotheses

Decide \(H_k\), \(k=\arg\min_i\sum_j C_{ij}P(H_j\mid\mathbf{x})\). With 0/1 costs this is \(\arg\max_i p(\mathbf{x}|H_i)P(H_i)\) (M-ary MAP), and with equal priors \(\arg\max_i p(\mathbf{x}|H_i)\) (M-ary ML). The worked 3-level example is in the homework bank.

Irrelevant data

Add extra data \(\mathbf x_2\): \(L(\mathbf x_1,\mathbf x_2)=\frac{p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_1)}{p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_0)}L(\mathbf x_1)\). \(\mathbf x_2\) is irrelevant iff \(p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_1)=p(\mathbf x_2|\mathbf x_1;H_0)\). It is not enough for \(\mathbf x_2\) alone to be independent of the hypothesis: a noise-only reference channel whose noise is correlated with the signal channel lets you cancel noise and helps detection.

04Toolkit

The digressions every derivation leans on: sufficient statistics, Gaussian facts, the Q function, χ².

Sufficient statistics

\(T(\mathbf{x})\) is sufficient for \(\theta\) if \(\mathbf{x}\) is independent of \(\theta\) given \(T(\mathbf x)\): \(T\) holds all the information \(\mathbf x\) has about \(\theta\).

Neyman–Fisher factorisation
\[p(\mathbf{x};\theta)=g\big(T(\mathbf{x}),\theta\big)\,h(\mathbf{x})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\text{ sufficient}.\] For detection: if \(p(\mathbf{x};H_i)=g_i(T(\mathbf x))h(\mathbf x)\), then \(L=g_1(T)/g_0(T)\) depends on \(\mathbf x\) only through \(T\). You compute \(P_{FA},P_D\) from a scalar PDF, never an N-dimensional integral.
  • Coin tosses, \(\theta=P(X_i=1)\): \(T=\sum X_i\). (\(p=\theta^{T}(1-\theta)^{n-T}\), so \(h=1\).)
  • DC level in WGN: \(T=\sum x[n]\) (or \(\bar x\)). See Tutorial Q2.
  • Unknown-variance problem: \(T=\sum x^2[n]\), the energy detector.

Gaussian facts

\[p(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\det^{1/2}(C)}\exp\!\Big[-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^TC^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\Big]\]
  1. Fully specified by \((\boldsymbol\mu,C)\).
  2. Uncorrelated ⇔ independent. Diagonal \(C\) means independent components.
  3. Linear maps: \(\mathbf y=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\sim\mathcal N(A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b,\ ACA^T)\). Hence \(\mathbf h^T\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf h^T\boldsymbol\mu,\mathbf h^TC\mathbf h)\).
  4. Marginals are Gaussian.
  5. Conditionals: \(\mathbf x_1|\mathbf x_2\sim\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_1+C_{12}C_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf x_2-\boldsymbol\mu_2),\ C_{11}-C_{12}C_{22}^{-1}C_{21}\big)\). The conditional covariance doesn't depend on \(\mathbf x_2\).

Why Gaussians dominate: thermal noise is Gaussian by the CLT, the family is closed under linear operations, log-LRs become quadratic forms with tabulated distributions, and for a fixed covariance the Gaussian is the worst case (max entropy).

Linear-Gaussian detectors: one formula

If \(T=\mathbf h^T\mathbf x\) and \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_i,C)\) under \(H_i\), eliminating the threshold gives

The formula you'll use most
\[P_D=Q\Big(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-\sqrt{d^2}\Big),\qquad d^2=\frac{\big(E[T;H_1]-E[T;H_0]\big)^2}{\mathrm{var}(T;H_0)}=\frac{\big(\mathbf h^T(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_0)\big)^2}{\mathbf h^TC\mathbf h}.\] The deflection coefficient \(d^2\) is the only thing that matters for a linear-Gaussian detector.

The Q function

\[Q(x)=\int_x^\infty\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2/2}dt=\Pr\{\mathcal N(0,1)>x\},\qquad \Pr\{\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)>\gamma\}=Q\!\left(\tfrac{\gamma-\mu}{\sigma}\right).\]
  • \(Q(0)=\tfrac12\), \(Q(-x)=1-Q(x)\), strictly decreasing so \(Q^{-1}\) exists; \(Q(x)=\tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(x/\sqrt2)\).
  • Bounds for \(x>0\): \(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi}(1+x^2)}e^{-x^2/2}

Chi-squared

Central \(\chi^2_N\)
\(x_i\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) iid, \(Y=\sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2\).
\(E[Y]=N,\ \mathrm{var}(Y)=2N\).
Non-central \(\chi'^2_N(\lambda)\)
\(x_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i,1)\), \(\lambda=\sum\mu_i^2\).
\(E[Y]=N+\lambda,\ \mathrm{var}(Y)=2N+4\lambda\).
  • \(N=2\): \(\chi^2_2\) is exponential with mean 2, right tail \(Q_{\chi^2_2}(y)=e^{-y/2}\). This is what gives closed-form ROCs.
  • Large \(N\) (≳30): \(Q_{\chi^2_N}(y)\approx Q\big((y-N)/\sqrt{2N}\big)\). Fine in the body, poor in the far tail.
  • \(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum x^2[n]\sim\chi^2_N\) if \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\); \(\sim\chi'^2_N(\boldsymbol\mu^T\boldsymbol\mu/\sigma^2)\) if the mean is \(\boldsymbol\mu\).

05Neyman–Pearson & the ROC

Lec 3–5. No priors, no costs: maximise \(P_D\) subject to \(P_{FA}\le\alpha\).

NP theorem (continuous)
To maximise \(P_D\) subject to \(P_{FA}=\alpha\), decide \(H_1\) if \[L(\mathbf x)=\frac{p(\mathbf x;H_1)}{p(\mathbf x;H_0)}>\gamma,\qquad\text{where }\int_{\{\mathbf x:L(\mathbf x)>\gamma\}}p(\mathbf x;H_0)\,d\mathbf x=\alpha.\] It's the same statistic as Bayes. Only the threshold rule changes, and γ is computed from \(p(T;H_0)\) alone.
Proof sketch (Lagrangian) derive

Maximise \(F=P_D+\lambda(P_{FA}-\alpha)=\int_{R_1}\big[p(\mathbf x;H_1)+\lambda p(\mathbf x;H_0)\big]d\mathbf x-\lambda\alpha\). Include \(\mathbf x\) in \(R_1\) whenever the integrand is positive: \(p(\mathbf x;H_1)>-\lambda p(\mathbf x;H_0)\). Need \(\lambda<0\) for a non-trivial region; set \(\gamma=-\lambda>0\) and pick it to meet \(P_{FA}=\alpha\).

Optimality via any competitor \(\tilde\delta\) with \(P_{FA}(\tilde\delta)\le\alpha\): \(\int(\delta-\tilde\delta)(p_1-\gamma p_0)\,d\mathbf x\ge0\) pointwise (where \(p_1>\gamma p_0\), \(\delta=1\ge\tilde\delta\); where \(p_1<\gamma p_0\), \(\delta=0\le\tilde\delta\)). So \(P_D(\delta)-P_D(\tilde\delta)\ge\gamma\big(P_{FA}(\delta)-P_{FA}(\tilde\delta)\big)\ge0\).

Randomised rules (discrete data)

When \(T\) is discrete, \(P_{FA}(\gamma)\) jumps, and you may not hit α exactly. Randomise on the boundary:

\[\delta(\mathbf x)=\begin{cases}1 & L(\mathbf x)>\gamma\\ c_0 & L(\mathbf x)=\gamma\quad(\text{decide }H_1\text{ w.p. }c_0)\\ 0 & L(\mathbf x)<\gamma\end{cases}\qquad c_0=\frac{\alpha-\Pr\{L>\gamma;H_0\}}{\Pr\{L=\gamma;H_0\}}.\]

The full theorem: optimality (any size-α rule of this form beats every rule with \(P_{FA}\le\alpha\)), existence (for each α∈(0,1) there is one with constant \(c_0\)), uniqueness (every NP rule has this form except on a set of zero probability). Worked example: binomial.

DC level in WGN, NP version

Same statistic \(T=\bar x\). Set the threshold from \(H_0\): \(\gamma'=\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}\,Q^{-1}(P_{FA})\). Then

\[P_D=Q\!\left(\frac{\gamma'-A}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\right)=Q\Big(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-\sqrt{d^2}\Big),\qquad d^2=\frac{NA^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{\mathcal E}{\sigma^2}.\]

Only the energy-to-noise ratio \(\mathcal E/\sigma^2\) (with \(\mathcal E=NA^2\)) matters. Doubling \(N\) adds 3 dB to \(d^2\).

Design question from the slides: \(P_D=0.99\) at \(P_{FA}=10^{-6}\), \(A^2/\sigma^2=0.1\). How big is N? numeric
\[\sqrt{d^2}=Q^{-1}(10^{-6})-Q^{-1}(0.99)=4.753+2.326=7.08\ \Rightarrow\ d^2\approx 50.1\ (17\text{ dB})\] \[N=\frac{d^2}{A^2/\sigma^2}=\frac{50.1}{0.1}\approx 501\text{ samples.}\]

The ROC

The curve traced by \((P_{FA},P_D)\) as γ sweeps from \(+\infty\) to \(-\infty\), for a fixed data model. Drag the slider to see how one number, \(d^2\), sets the whole curve.

Grey: \(d^2=1,4,9,16\). Blue: your \(d^2\). The dot is the operating point; the ROC's slope there equals the LRT threshold γ.

Properties

  1. Passes through (0,0) and (1,1)
  2. Monotone non-decreasing
  3. On or above the chance line \(P_D=P_{FA}\)
  4. Concave
  5. Perfect detector = corner (0,1)
  6. Slope \(dP_D/dP_{FA}=\gamma\) at the point made by γ

How to use it

  1. The spec fixes α (e.g. 10⁻⁶)
  2. Required \(P_D\) fixes the operating point
  3. Read off \(d^2\) and convert it to SNR

Compare detectors only at the same \(P_{FA}\), or compare whole curves. AUC averages over operating points you may never use.

Why slope = γ derive

With \(T=L\): \(P_D(\gamma)=\int_\gamma^\infty p_L(l;H_1)dl\) and \(P_{FA}(\gamma)=\int_\gamma^\infty p_L(l;H_0)dl\), so \(\frac{dP_D}{dP_{FA}}=\frac{p_L(\gamma;H_1)}{p_L(\gamma;H_0)}\). The ratio of the densities of the likelihood ratio, evaluated at γ, is γ (since \(p_L(l;H_1)=l\,p_L(l;H_0)\)). Concavity follows because γ shrinks as \(P_{FA}\) grows.

06NP examples

Lec 5. Variance change (energy detector), binomial, exponential. These come up as "derive the exact ROC".

Change in variance → energy detector

\(H_0:x[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_0^2)\), \(H_1:x[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2)\), \(\sigma_1^2>\sigma_0^2\), iid.

Detector, exact \(P_D\), N = 2 ROC derive
\[\ln L=-\frac N2\ln\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_0^2}+\frac12\Big(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}\Big)\sum x^2[n]\ \Rightarrow\ T(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x^2[n]\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma'=\frac{2\ln\gamma-N\ln(\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2)}{\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}}.\]

Since \(\frac1{\sigma_0^2}-\frac1{\sigma_1^2}>0\) the direction is preserved. Under \(H_i\), \(T/\sigma_i^2\sim\chi^2_N\):

\[P_{FA}=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_0^2}\right),\qquad P_D=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\gamma'}{\sigma_1^2}\right)=Q_{\chi^2_N}\!\left(\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_1^2}Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_N}(P_{FA})\right).\]

N = 2: \(Q_{\chi^2_2}(y)=e^{-y/2}\), so \(P_{FA}=e^{-\gamma'/(2\sigma_0^2)}\), \(P_D=e^{-\gamma'/(2\sigma_1^2)}\), and

\[\boxed{P_D=P_{FA}^{\,\sigma_0^2/\sigma_1^2}}\]

A bigger ratio \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_0^2\) pushes the curve towards (0,1).

Binomial data (classwork, with numbers)

\(H_0:X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p_0)\), \(H_1:X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p_1)\), \(p_0

General detector, then n = 10, p₀ = 0.5, p₁ = 0.8, α = 0.05 numeric
\[\ln L(x)=x\ln\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}+n\ln\frac{1-p_1}{1-p_0}.\]

The coefficient of \(x\) is positive because \(p_1>p_0\). So \(T=x\gtrless\gamma'\): count the successes. \(T\) is discrete, so randomise.

  1. Try \(\gamma'=8\): \(\Pr\{X\ge8;H_0\}=2^{-10}\big[\binom{10}{8}+\binom{10}{9}+\binom{10}{10}\big]=56/1024=0.0547>0.05\). Too big.
  2. Try \(\gamma'=9\): \(\Pr\{X\ge9;H_0\}=11/1024=0.0107<0.05\). Too small. So the boundary is at \(x=8\), where \(\Pr\{X=8;H_0\}=45/1024=0.0439\).
  3. \(c_0=\dfrac{0.05-0.0107}{0.0439}=0.893\).
  4. Rule: \(x\ge9\) → \(H_1\); \(x=8\) → \(H_1\) with probability 0.893; \(x\le7\) → \(H_0\).
  5. \(P_D=\Pr\{X\ge9;H_1\}+0.893\Pr\{X=8;H_1\}=0.3758+0.893(0.3020)\approx0.646\).

Exponential data

\(p(x;H_i)=\lambda_ie^{-\lambda_ix}\), \(x\ge0\), with \(\lambda_1<\lambda_0\) (larger mean under \(H_1\)).

Detector and exact ROC derive
\[\ln L=N\ln\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}+(\lambda_0-\lambda_1)\sum x[n]\ \Rightarrow\ T=\sum_{n}x[n]\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \gamma'\quad(\lambda_0-\lambda_1>0).\]

N = 1: \(P_{FA}=e^{-\lambda_0\gamma'}\), \(P_D=e^{-\lambda_1\gamma'}\), so \(\boxed{P_D=P_{FA}^{\lambda_1/\lambda_0}}\). It has the same shape as the χ²₂ case, which makes sense because \(\chi^2_2\) is exponential.

General N: \(2\lambda_iT\sim\chi^2_{2N}\) under \(H_i\) (sum of exponentials is Gamma/Erlang), so \(P_D=Q_{\chi^2_{2N}}\big(\tfrac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}Q^{-1}_{\chi^2_{2N}}(P_{FA})\big)\).

07Known signal in WGN: the matched filter

Lec 6. \(H_0:\mathbf x=\mathbf w\), \(H_1:\mathbf x=\mathbf s+\mathbf w\), \(\mathbf s\) fully known (coherent detection), \(\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\). This is Example B with \(\boldsymbol\mu_0=\mathbf 0\), \(\boldsymbol\mu_1=\mathbf s\).

Replica-correlator
\[T(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]s[n]=\mathbf x^T\mathbf s\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \sigma^2\ln\gamma+\frac{\mathcal E}{2},\qquad P_D=Q\Big(Q^{-1}(P_{FA})-\sqrt{\mathcal E/\sigma^2}\Big).\] \(T\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2\mathcal E)\) under \(H_0\) and \(\mathcal N(\mathcal E,\sigma^2\mathcal E)\) under \(H_1\), so \(d^2=\mathcal E/\sigma^2\).

Central result: in WGN, performance depends on the signal only through its energy \(\mathcal E=\mathbf s^T\mathbf s\). A chirp, a sinusoid and a DC level with equal energy detect identically. Shape starts to matter once the noise is coloured.

Correlator = matched filter

FIR filter output \(y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^n h[n-k]x[k]\). Choose \(h[k]=s[N-1-k]\) for \(k=0,\dots,N-1\) (time-reversed signal) and sample at \(n=N-1\): \(y[N-1]=\sum_k s[k]x[k]=\mathbf x^T\mathbf s\). The filter's impulse response is "matched" to the signal. This assumes the signal starts at \(n=0\).

Matched filter maximises output SNR derive

Output at \(N-1\) is \(\mathbf h^T\mathbf x\) (with \(\mathbf h\) the reversed impulse response). SNR \(=\dfrac{(\mathbf h^T\mathbf s)^2}{E[(\mathbf h^T\mathbf w)^2]}=\dfrac{(\mathbf h^T\mathbf s)^2}{\sigma^2\mathbf h^T\mathbf h}\le\dfrac{(\mathbf h^T\mathbf h)(\mathbf s^T\mathbf s)}{\sigma^2\mathbf h^T\mathbf h}=\dfrac{\mathcal E}{\sigma^2}\) by Cauchy–Schwarz, with equality iff \(\mathbf h=c\,\mathbf s\). The maximum SNR equals \(d^2\).

Why sample at N−1? \(y_s[n]=\sum_k s[k]s[k+N-1-n]\) is an autocorrelation \(r_{ss}[N-1-n]\), which peaks at lag 0, i.e. \(n=N-1\).

09Binary & M-ary known signals

Lec 8. \(H_i:x[n]=s_i[n]+w[n]\), energies \(\mathcal E_i\), WGN.

\[T(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^T(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0)\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \sigma^2\ln\gamma+\frac{\mathcal E_1-\mathcal E_0}{2}.\]

For equal priors this is the minimum-distance receiver: decide \(H_1\) if \(\|\mathbf x-\mathbf s_1\|^2<\|\mathbf x-\mathbf s_0\|^2\). The boundary is the perpendicular bisector of \(\mathbf s_0\mathbf s_1\). Unequal priors or costs shift it along \(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\) but never rotate it. Only the projection onto \(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\) matters, so the problem is one-dimensional whatever N is.

Binary error probability (equal priors)
\[P_e=Q\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\|}{2\sigma}\right)=Q\!\left(\tfrac12\sqrt{d^2}\right),\quad d^2=\frac{\|\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\|^2}{\sigma^2}.\] \(P_e\) depends only on the distance between the signals. To signal well, make the waveforms as different as possible for the energy you're allowed to spend.
Derivation of \(P_e\) (equal energies, equal priors) derive

With \(\mathcal E_0=\mathcal E_1\), threshold 0. Let \(\mathbf h=\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\). \(T|H_i\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf h^T\mathbf s_i,\sigma^2\|\mathbf h\|^2)\). \(\mathbf h^T\mathbf s_1-\mathbf h^T\mathbf s_0=\|\mathbf h\|^2\) and, by symmetry, the means sit at \(\pm\|\mathbf h\|^2/2\). So \(P_e=Q\big(\frac{\|\mathbf h\|^2/2}{\sigma\|\mathbf h\|}\big)=Q\big(\frac{\|\mathbf h\|}{2\sigma}\big)\).

Unequal priors \(\pi_0,\pi_1\) (0/1 costs), \(d=\|\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\|/\sigma\):

\[P_e=\pi_0\,Q\!\left(\frac d2+\frac{\ln(\pi_0/\pi_1)}{d}\right)+\pi_1\,Q\!\left(\frac d2-\frac{\ln(\pi_0/\pi_1)}{d}\right).\]

Coloured noise: replace \(\|\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\|^2/\sigma^2\) by \((\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0)^TC^{-1}(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0)\); \(T=(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0)^TC^{-1}\mathbf x\). Whiten, then use minimum distance.

A bank of M correlators, then pick the largest. The decision regions are Voronoi cells (polytopes), so exact \(P_e\) has no closed form in general. Use the union bound:

10Random signals

Lec 9. \(H_0:\mathbf x=\mathbf w\), \(H_1:\mathbf x=\mathbf s+\mathbf w\), \(\mathbf s\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,C_s)\) independent of \(\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\). Examples: fading, fluctuating targets, speech, EEG.

Structural change
Both hypotheses have zero mean. All the information is in the covariance, so the detector is quadratic, not linear.

Setup 1: white signal (\(C_s=\sigma_s^2I\)) → energy detector

\(H_0:\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I)\) vs \(H_1:\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)I)\). This is the variance-change problem: \(T=\sum x^2[n]\), with \(T/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_N\) under \(H_0\) and \(T/(\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2)\sim\chi^2_N\) under \(H_1\). A white signal has no structure to exploit, so total power is all that's left.

Setup 2: general \(C_s\) → estimator-correlator

Derivation derive
\[\ln L=-\tfrac12\mathbf x^T\big[(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}-\tfrac1{\sigma^2}I\big]\mathbf x+\text{const}\ \Rightarrow\ T(\mathbf x)=\sigma^2\mathbf x^T\Big[\tfrac1{\sigma^2}I-(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\Big]\mathbf x.\]

The matrix inversion lemma \((A+BCD)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}B(DA^{-1}B+C^{-1})^{-1}DA^{-1}\) gives \(\tfrac1{\sigma^2}I-(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}=\tfrac1{\sigma^2}C_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\). So

\[\boxed{T(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^T\hat{\mathbf s},\qquad\hat{\mathbf s}=C_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf x}\]

\(\hat{\mathbf s}=E[\mathbf s|\mathbf x]\) is the MMSE (Wiener) estimate of the signal. Since you don't know the signal, estimate it and correlate against the estimate as if it were the replica.

Check: \(C_s=\sigma_s^2I\) gives \(\hat s[n]=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma^2}x[n]\) and \(T\propto\sum x^2[n]\), the energy detector again.

Canonical form

Eigendecompose \(C_s=V\Lambda_sV^T\), \(\Lambda_s=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{N-1})\), and rotate \(\mathbf y=V^T\mathbf x\). Then \(C_s+\sigma^2I=V(\Lambda_s+\sigma^2I)V^T\) and

\[T(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n+\sigma^2}\,y^2[n].\]

Under \(H_0\), \(y[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\); under \(H_1\), \(y[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\lambda_n+\sigma^2)\), independent in both cases. \(T\) is a weighted sum of independent \(\chi^2_1\) variables: no closed-form CDF in general, so compute it numerically. The weights \(\frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n+\sigma^2}\) are Wiener gains: trust high-SNR directions and ignore the rest.

Extensions (Kay §5.6, problems 5.10, 5.11): coloured noise \(C_w\) → prewhiten, then use the MMSE estimator. Non-zero mean \(\mathbf s\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf m_s,C_s)\) → \(T\) has a linear term plus a quadratic term.

11Composite hypotheses: UMP, Bayesian, GLRT

Lec 10. \(H_0:\theta\in\Theta_0\), \(H_1:\theta\in\Theta_1\), with unknown parameters. The LRT depends on θ, so you can't build it directly.

Performance becomes a function: the power function \(\beta(\theta)=\Pr\{\text{decide }H_1;\theta\}\), equal to \(P_{FA}(\theta)\) on \(\Theta_0\) and \(P_D(\theta)\) on \(\Theta_1\). Ideally it is 0 on \(\Theta_0\) and 1 on \(\Theta_1\).

UMP test
Maximise \(P_D(\theta)\) for every \(\theta\in\Theta_1\) subject to \(P_{FA}(\theta)\le\alpha\) for every \(\theta\in\Theta_0\), with the same test and the same threshold. A UMP test doesn't always exist.

One-sided: UMP exists

DC level, \(H_0:A=0\) vs \(H_1:A>0\). The NP test \(\bar x>\gamma'\) uses \(A>0\) only for the direction of the inequality, and \(\gamma'=\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}Q^{-1}(\alpha)\) comes from \(H_0\) alone. So one test is optimal for every \(A>0\), with \(P_D(A)=Q\big(Q^{-1}(\alpha)-\sqrt{NA^2/\sigma^2}\big)\).

Two-sided: no UMP

\(H_1:A\ne0\). For \(A>0\) the NP test is \(\bar x>\gamma'\); for \(A<0\) it is \(\bar x<-\gamma'\). These are two different tests, and each collapses on the other half-line. The clairvoyant detector (which knows A) is an upper bound no realisable test reaches.

α = 0.05, \(\sqrt{N}/\sigma=1\). Orange: \(\bar x>\gamma'\). Teal: \(\bar x<-\gamma'\). Dashed: GLRT \(\bar x^2>\gamma''\). The clairvoyant envelope is the max of the two solid curves.
Rule of thumb
UMP tests tend to exist with a monotone likelihood ratio in a scalar \(T(\mathbf x)\) and a one-sided alternative. They essentially never exist for two-sided alternatives, vector θ, or nuisance parameters (like unknown \(\sigma^2\)) under both hypotheses.

Two ways out

Bayesian: integrate θ out with a prior. \[L_B(\mathbf x)=\frac{\int p(\mathbf x|\theta_1)p(\theta_1)d\theta_1}{\int p(\mathbf x|\theta_0)p(\theta_0)d\theta_0}\] Turns the problem back into simple vs simple; NP-optimal for that prior.
GLRT: plug in MLEs. \[L_G(\mathbf x)=\frac{p(\mathbf x;\hat\theta_1,H_1)}{p(\mathbf x;\hat\theta_0,H_0)}\] No prior needed. Not optimal in general, but asymptotically optimal.

Integration vs maximisation is the only structural difference. Bayesian needs a prior; GLRT needs a computable MLE.

Bayesian: \(A\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_A^2)\), σ² known → \(T=\bar x^2\) derive

\(p(\mathbf x|H_1)=\int p(\mathbf x|A)p(A)dA\). Complete the square in A: \(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\big(NA^2-2AN\bar x\big)+\frac{A^2}{\sigma_A^2}=\frac{1}{v^2}(A-m)^2-\frac{N^2\bar x^2v^2}{\sigma^4}\), where \(\frac1{v^2}=\frac N{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\sigma_A^2}\) and \(m=\frac{N\bar x v^2}{\sigma^2}\). The Gaussian integral in A gives a constant, leaving

\[L_B(\mathbf x)=\frac{v}{\sigma_A}\exp\!\Big(\frac{N^2v^2}{2\sigma^4}\bar x^2\Big)\ \Rightarrow\ T(\mathbf x)=\bar x^2\ \gtrless\ \gamma'.\]

The prior is symmetric in A, so the detector ignores the sign of \(\bar x\) and squares it.

GLRT, A unknown, σ² known → also \(T=\bar x^2\) derive

MLE under \(H_1\): \(\hat A=\bar x\). Then

\[\ln L_G=-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\Big[\sum(x[n]-\bar x)^2-\sum x^2[n]\Big]=\frac{N\bar x^2}{2\sigma^2}\ \Rightarrow\ T=\bar x^2\gtrless\gamma'.\]

Equivalently \(|\bar x|>\sqrt{\gamma'}\). Performance: \(P_{FA}=2Q\big(\sqrt{\gamma'}/\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}\big)\) and \(P_D=Q\big(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma'}-A}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\big)+Q\big(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma'}+A}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/N}}\big)\). It coincides with the Bayesian detector because both are forced by the \(A\to-A\) symmetry.

GLRT, A and σ² both unknown → \(T=\bar x^2/\hat\sigma_1^2\) derive

θ = [A, σ²], and σ² is a nuisance parameter. MLEs: under \(H_1\), \(\hat A=\bar x\), \(\hat\sigma_1^2=\frac1N\sum(x[n]-\bar x)^2\); under \(H_0\), \(\hat\sigma_0^2=\frac1N\sum x^2[n]\). Plugging in, the exponents become \(-N/2\) on both sides:

\[L_G=\left(\frac{\hat\sigma_0^2}{\hat\sigma_1^2}\right)^{N/2},\qquad \hat\sigma_0^2=\hat\sigma_1^2+\bar x^2\ \Rightarrow\ L_G=\Big(1+\frac{\bar x^2}{\hat\sigma_1^2}\Big)^{N/2}.\] \[T(\mathbf x)=\frac{\bar x^2}{\hat\sigma_1^2}\ \gtrless\ \gamma'.\]

The same statistic, normalised by the estimated noise power. This is what makes it CFAR: \(P_{FA}\) no longer depends on the unknown σ². It's essentially a two-sided t-test.

Homework: unknown variance. \(H_0:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2\) vs \(H_1:\sigma^2>\sigma_0^2\): \(T=\sum x^2[n]>\gamma'\), threshold from \(\sigma_0^2\) alone, so UMP exists, with power \(Q_{\chi^2_N}(\gamma'/\sigma^2)\). With \(H_1:\sigma^2\ne\sigma_0^2\), \(\sigma^2<\sigma_0^2\) flips the inequality, so there is no UMP.

Locally most powerful (LMP) tests, optimal for weak signals θ→0, were listed on the agenda but not developed in the slides.

12Sequential detection: Wald's SPRT

Lec 11 (Wald 1947 ch. 2–3; Poor §III-D). Samples cost something. After each one: accept \(H_0\), accept \(H_1\), or take another sample. The sample size N is random, and you want to minimise \(E\{N\}\).

The origin: in 1943, how many shells must be fired (and destroyed) before you accept or reject a lot? It was classified until 1945. Applications today: acceptance sampling, clinical trials, spectrum sensing in cognitive radio, online A/B testing.

Termination

If \(p_0\ne p_1\) (so \(z\) is not identically 0), then \(\Pr\{N<\infty\}=1\). Idea: chop time into blocks of length k. In each block there's a fixed probability \(\delta>0\) that \(|z_{1}+\dots+z_k|>a-b\), which would exit any interval of width \(a-b\). Wald's fundamental inequalities

\(A\le\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}\), \(B\ge\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\) derive

On every sample path that stops accepting \(H_1\), \(p_{1n}\ge A\,p_{0n}\). Summing/integrating over all such paths: \(\Pr_1\{\text{accept }H_1\}\ge A\Pr_0\{\text{accept }H_1\}\), i.e. \(1-\beta\ge A\alpha\).

On paths that accept \(H_0\), \(p_{1n}\le B\,p_{0n}\), so \(\beta\le B(1-\alpha)\).

Equality would need \(\Lambda_N\) to land exactly on A or B (no overshoot). Assuming that gives Wald's approximations:

\[A\approx\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha},\qquad B\approx\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}.\]

The thresholds depend only on (α, β): you don't need the densities or N to set them.

Bound on realised errors: \(\alpha'+\beta'\le\alpha+\beta\) derive

Design with \(A=\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}\), \(B=\frac\beta{1-\alpha}\), and suppose the realised strength is \((\alpha',\beta')\). The inequalities give \(\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}\le\frac{1-\beta'}{\alpha'}\) and \(\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\ge\frac{\beta'}{1-\alpha'}\), i.e.

\[\alpha'(1-\beta)\le\alpha(1-\beta'),\qquad \beta'(1-\alpha)\le\beta(1-\alpha').\]

Add them: \(\alpha'+\beta'-\alpha'\beta-\alpha\beta'\le\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta'-\alpha'\beta\), so \(\alpha'+\beta'\le\alpha+\beta\). Each one individually: \(\alpha'\le\frac{\alpha}{1-\beta}\), \(\beta'\le\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}\).

If one error slips, the other more than compensates. Because of overshoot the test is in practice conservative: the slides' simulation got realised errors of roughly half the nominal ones (nominal 0.05/0.05 → 0.029/0.030), paid for with extra samples.

Average sample number (ASN)

\[E_1\{N\}\approx\frac{(1-\beta)\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}+\beta\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}{D(p_1\|p_0)},\qquad E_0\{N\}\approx\frac{\alpha\ln\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha}+(1-\alpha)\ln\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}}{-D(p_0\|p_1)}.\]

Intuition (Wald's identity \(E[S_N]=E[N]E[z]\)): under \(H_1\), \(S_N\approx a\) w.p. 1−β and ≈ b w.p. β; divide by the drift.

Worked: \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) vs \(\mathcal N(1,1)\), α = β = 0.05 numeric

\(D=\mu^2/2\sigma^2=0.5\) both ways. \(a=\ln19=2.944\), \(b=-2.944\).

\[E_1\{N\}\approx\frac{0.95(2.944)+0.05(-2.944)}{0.5}=\frac{0.9\times2.944}{0.5}\approx5.3.\]

Why not always use the SPRT?
N is unbounded (a single run can be long), you need exact knowledge of both \(p_0\) and \(p_1\), and the theory is thin once the data aren't iid. The SPRT is optimal: among all tests with errors ≤ (α, β), it minimises both \(E_0\{N\}\) and \(E_1\{N\}\) (Wald–Wolfowitz).

\[\ln L(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\ln\frac{p(x[n]-A)}{p(x[n])}=\sum_{n}\underbrace{\ln\frac{1+x^2[n]}{1+(x[n]-A)^2}}_{g(x[n])}\ \underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\ \ln\gamma.\]

For A = 1: \(g(x)=\ln\frac{1+x^2}{1+(x-1)^2}\). It is 0 at \(x=\tfrac12\), positive for \(x>\tfrac12\) and negative for \(x<\tfrac12\). It peaks near \(x\approx1.6\) (value \(\ln\frac{1+\phi^2}{1+(\phi-1)^2}\approx0.96\), with φ the golden ratio), and \(g(x)\to0\) as \(|x|\to\infty\).

Interpretation: a soft limiter that ignores outliers. Cauchy noise produces huge spikes, so a huge sample tells you nothing about A. Compare the Gaussian case, where \(g\) is linear (\(Ax-A^2/2\)), and the Laplacian case, where it is a hard clipper.

DC level: effect of unequal priors and costs (Lec 2 HW)

The statistic stays \(\bar x\); only \(\gamma'=\frac{\sigma^2}{NA}\ln\gamma+\frac A2\) moves, with \(\gamma=\frac{(C_{10}-C_{00})P(H_0)}{(C_{01}-C_{11})P(H_1)}\). Raising \(P(H_0)\) or the false-alarm cost \(C_{10}\) moves γ′ right (fewer false alarms, more misses). The effect fades as \(N\) grows, because \(\sigma^2/(NA)\to0\) and the threshold approaches \(A/2\): with lots of data, the prior matters less.

Three hypotheses \(-A,0,+A\) in WGN, equal priors (Lec 6 HW)

\(\bar x\) is sufficient: \(\bar x\sim\mathcal N(\mu_i,\sigma^2/N)\) with \(\mu_i\in\{-A,0,A\}\). ML = nearest mean on the line:

\[\bar x<-\tfrac A2\Rightarrow H_0,\qquad |\bar x|\le\tfrac A2\Rightarrow H_1,\qquad \bar x>\tfrac A2\Rightarrow H_2.\]

Let \(q=Q\big(\frac{A/2}{\sigma/\sqrt N}\big)=Q\big(\sqrt{NA^2/4\sigma^2}\big)\). The outer hypotheses err with probability q, the middle one with 2q, so

\[P_e=\tfrac13(q+2q+q)=\tfrac43\,Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{NA^2}{4\sigma^2}}\right).\]

With unequal priors/costs, use the general rule \(\arg\min_i C_i\). The boundaries shift but stay at two points on the \(\bar x\) line.

Signal design in correlated noise \(C=\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\) (Lec 7 HW)

Eigenpairs: \(\lambda=1+\rho\) with \(\mathbf v=\frac1{\sqrt2}[1,1]^T\), and \(\lambda=1-\rho\) with \(\mathbf v=\frac1{\sqrt2}[1,-1]^T\).

For \(\rho>0\) the minimum eigenvalue is \(1-\rho\), so use \(\mathbf s=\sqrt{\mathcal E/2}\,[1,-1]^T\) and get \(d^2=\frac{\mathcal E}{1-\rho}\). (For \(\rho<0\), use \([1,1]^T\) and \(d^2=\mathcal E/(1+\rho)\).)

As \(\rho\to1\): \(d^2\to\infty\). The noise samples become identical, so \(x[0]-x[1]\) cancels the noise completely while keeping the signal \(2\sqrt{\mathcal E/2}\). Perfect detection, and a good illustration of why correlated "noise-only" data is not irrelevant.

Detector: \(T=\mathbf x^TC^{-1}\mathbf s\) with \(C^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\rho\\-\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\).

AR(1) noise: whitening filter and what happens as a → 1 (Lec 7 HW)

\(w[n]=a\,w[n-1]+u[n]\), so \(u[n]=w[n]-a\,w[n-1]\) is white. The whitening filter is \(y[n]=x[n]-a\,x[n-1]\) (FIR, \(H(z)=1-az^{-1}\)), scaled by \(1/\sigma_u\). Then correlate with the whitened signal \(s'[n]=s[n]-a\,s[n-1]\). This matches the tridiagonal \(C^{-1}\) on the slide (interior rows \(-a,\ 1+a^2,\ -a\), over \(\sigma_u^2\)).

As \(a\to1\): the noise becomes a slow random walk, with its power concentrated at low frequencies. The whitener becomes a differencer. Signal design: put energy at high frequency (e.g. alternating \(\pm\)), where the noise is weak. A DC signal becomes almost undetectable, because differencing kills it along with the noise.

Random signal with \(C_s=\sigma_s^2\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\): estimator-correlator (Lec 9 HW)

Eigenvalues \(\lambda_0=\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)\), \(\lambda_1=\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)\), with \(y[0]=\frac{x[0]+x[1]}{\sqrt2}\), \(y[1]=\frac{x[0]-x[1]}{\sqrt2}\).

\[T(\mathbf x)=\frac{\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)}{\sigma_s^2(1+\rho)+\sigma^2}\,y^2[0]+\frac{\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)}{\sigma_s^2(1-\rho)+\sigma^2}\,y^2[1],\qquad\hat{\mathbf s}=C_s(C_s+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf x.\]

\(\rho=0\): equal weights, the energy detector. \(\rho\to1\): the second weight goes to 0, so \(T\propto(x[0]+x[1])^2\). The signal lives entirely in the "sum" direction; average the samples, then square.

Bayesian detector and performance for binomial and exponential data (Lec 5 HW)

Binomial: \(x\gtrless\gamma'=\dfrac{\ln\gamma-n\ln\frac{1-p_1}{1-p_0}}{\ln\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}}\) with \(\gamma=P(H_0)/P(H_1)\). Then \(P_e=P(H_0)\sum_{x>\gamma'}\binom nx p_0^x(1-p_0)^{n-x}+P(H_1)\sum_{x\le\gamma'}\binom nx p_1^x(1-p_1)^{n-x}\). For n = 10, 0.5 vs 0.8 with equal priors: \(\gamma'=\frac{10\ln2.5}{\ln4}=6.61\), so decide \(H_1\) for \(x\ge7\), and \(P_e=\tfrac12(0.172+0.121)\approx0.146\).

Exponential (N = 1): \(x\gtrless\gamma'=\frac{\ln\gamma+\ln(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)}{\lambda_0-\lambda_1}\), and \(P_e=P(H_0)e^{-\lambda_0\gamma'}+P(H_1)(1-e^{-\lambda_1\gamma'})\).

Matched filter output peaks at n = N−1 (Lec 6 HW)

Signal part of the output: \(y_s[n]=\sum_k h[n-k]s[k]=\sum_k s[N-1-n+k]s[k]=r_{ss}[N-1-n]\), the deterministic autocorrelation at lag \(N-1-n\). By Cauchy–Schwarz \(|r_{ss}[\ell]|\le r_{ss}[0]=\mathcal E\), so it is maximised at lag 0, i.e. \(n=N-1\).
