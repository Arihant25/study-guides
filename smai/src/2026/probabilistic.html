2.1PCA as minimum reconstruction error

Lecture L08 · Short video: PCA as compression · Short video: Orthogonal line fitting

Keeping the directions of largest variance is the same as throwing away as little of each sample as possible.

On Page 1 PCA was found by asking for the direction along which the data varies most. L08 asks a second question. If each sample is squashed onto one line and then expanded back, which line loses the least? The answer is the same line, and this section shows why.

Writing a sample in a new basis

A basis of \(\R^d\) is a set of \(d\) directions such that every vector is a unique mix of them. The usual basis is \(u_1 = [1,0,\dots,0]\T\), \(u_2 = [0,1,0,\dots,0]\T\) and so on. Here \(\R^d\) means "lists of \(d\) real numbers", and \(\T\) (transpose) turns a row into a column.

If the basis vectors are orthonormal (each has length 1 and each pair is at right angles, so \(u_i\T u_j = 0\) for \(i \ne j\) and \(u_i\T u_i = 1\)), then the amount of \(x\) along \(u_i\) is just the dot product \(\alpha_i = x\T u_i\), and

\[x = \alpha_1 u_1 + \alpha_2 u_2 + \dots + \alpha_d u_d.\]
Reading the formula

Each \(\alpha_i\) is a number, the coordinate of \(x\) along direction \(u_i\). Each \(u_i\) is a direction. The sum rebuilds \(x\) from its pieces.

Example. Take \(d = 2\), \(u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}[1, 1]\T\), \(u_2 = \frac1{\sqrt2}[1, -1]\T\) and \(x = [3, 1]\T\). Then \(\alpha_1 \)\({}= \frac{3 + 1}{\sqrt2} \)\({}= \frac{4}{\sqrt2}\) and \(\alpha_2 \)\({}= \frac{3 - 1}{\sqrt2} \)\({}= \frac{2}{\sqrt2}\). Rebuild the point. \(\frac4{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}[1,1]\T + \frac2{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}[1,-1]\T \)\({}= [2,2]\T + [1,-1]\T \)\({}= [3,1]\T\). It matches \(x\).

If we keep only the first term, we get \([2,2]\T\). The part we threw away is \([1,-1]\T\), whose squared length is 2. That lost part is the reconstruction error.

PCA keeps only the first \(d'\) terms, where \(d' \lt d\). The question is which \(d'\) directions to keep so that the thrown-away part is as small as possible, summed over all samples.

Write this in the exam

Show that the unit vector \(u\) that minimises the total reconstruction error \(\sum_i \|x_i - uu\T x_i\|^2\) is the top eigenvector of \(\Sigma = \sum_i x_i x_i\T\) (data mean-subtracted).

  1. The projection of \(x_i\) onto the line along \(u\) is \(uu\T x_i\), with \(u\T u = 1\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. The amount of \(x_i\) along \(u\) is the number \(u\T x_i\), because \(u\) has length 1.
    2. Going that amount along direction \(u\) gives the point \((u\T x_i)\,u\).
    3. A number can be moved to either side of a vector, so \((u\T x_i)\,u \)\({}= u\,(u\T x_i) \)\({}= uu\T x_i\).
  2. Expand one error term. \(\|x_i - uu\T x_i\|^2 \)\({}= x_i\T x_i - (u\T x_i)^2\).
    Why (5 steps)
    1. For any vector \(v\), \(\|v\|^2 = v\T v\). So the term is \((x_i - uu\T x_i)\T(x_i - uu\T x_i)\).
    2. Multiply out like \((a-b)\T(a-b) \)\({}= a\T a - 2a\T b + b\T b\). This gives \(x_i\T x_i - 2x_i\T uu\T x_i + x_i\T u u\T u u\T x_i\).
    3. In the last term \(u\T u = 1\) sits in the middle, so it becomes \(x_i\T u u\T x_i\).
    4. Combine the middle and last terms. \(-2x_i\T uu\T x_i + x_i\T uu\T x_i \)\({}= -x_i\T uu\T x_i\).
    5. Now \(x_i\T u\) and \(u\T x_i\) are the same number, so \(x_i\T uu\T x_i = (u\T x_i)^2\).
  3. The first term does not depend on \(u\), so minimising the total error is the same as maximising \(\sum_i (u\T x_i)^2\).
  4. Rewrite. \(\sum_i (u\T x_i)^2 \)\({}= \sum_i u\T x_i x_i\T u \)\({}= u\T \Sigma u\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. \((u\T x_i)^2 \)\({}= (u\T x_i)(x_i\T u)\), because a number equals its own transpose.
    2. That is \(u\T (x_i x_i\T) u\).
    3. Add over \(i\) and pull \(u\T\) and \(u\) outside the sum, since they do not depend on \(i\). The sum left inside is \(\Sigma = \sum_i x_ix_i\T\).
  5. Maximise \(u\T\Sigma u\) subject to \(u\T u = 1\). The Lagrangian gives \(\Sigma u = \lambda u\), so \(u\) is an eigenvector.
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Build \(J(u,\lambda) \)\({}= u\T\Sigma u - \lambda(u\T u - 1)\). The number \(\lambda\) (lambda) is a Lagrange multiplier. It enforces the constraint (see basics).
    2. Differentiate with respect to \(u\). For symmetric \(\Sigma\), the derivative of \(u\T\Sigma u\) is \(2\Sigma u\), and of \(u\T u\) is \(2u\).
    3. Set the derivative to zero. \(2\Sigma u - 2\lambda u = 0\), so \(\Sigma u = \lambda u\).
    4. This says \(u\) is an eigenvector of \(\Sigma\) and \(\lambda\) its eigenvalue.
  6. At such a \(u\), the value is \(u\T\Sigma u \)\({}= \lambda u\T u \)\({}= \lambda\). So pick the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue.
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Substitute \(\Sigma u = \lambda u\) into \(u\T\Sigma u\). This gives \(u\T(\lambda u) \)\({}= \lambda\,u\T u\).
    2. Use \(u\T u = 1\). The value is \(\lambda\).
    3. We want the largest value, so we want the largest \(\lambda\). The total error left over is \(\sum_i x_i\T x_i - \lambda_1\).

For \(d'\) directions, the same argument gives the top \(d'\) eigenvectors. The error left is the sum of the eigenvalues that were dropped, \(\lambda_{d'+1} + \dots + \lambda_d\). This is why L06 chose \(d'\) as the smallest number for which the kept eigenvalues make up 95% of the total.

Remember
  • Max variance and min reconstruction error give the same PCA directions, the top eigenvectors of the covariance.
  • Reconstruction error of a \(d'\)-dimensional PCA equals the sum of the dropped eigenvalues (with \(\Sigma = \sum_i x_ix_i\T\)).
  • Subtract the mean first. Otherwise the first direction just points at the mean.

2.2Fisher LDA and other ways to reduce dimension

Lecture L08 · Short video: LDA 1 · Short video: LDA 2

PCA ignores labels. LDA uses them to find the direction on which the two classes are furthest apart compared with their spread.

Why reduce dimension at all

L08 lists the reasons. Fewer numbers per sample means compression, fewer parameters to learn (so less data is needed), less overfitting, faster computation and pictures you can look at (2 or 3 dimensions). It also sorts the methods along two axes.

Unsupervised (no labels)Supervised (uses labels)
Linear, \(x' = Wx\)PCAFisher LDA
Nonlinear, \(x' = f(W, x)\)Autoencoder, t-SNE(the hidden layers of a trained network)

An autoencoder is a neural network trained to output its own input through a narrow middle layer. The middle layer becomes a short code for the input. It comes back on the neural network pages. Manifold methods such as LLE and ISOMAP were named as out of scope.

t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding) is used only for pictures. It places each high-dimensional point in 2-D so that points that were close neighbours stay close neighbours. It does not give a formula \(x' = Wx\) that you could apply to a new point, and distances between far-apart clusters in the picture mean little. Know what it is for, not how it is computed.

The idea of Fisher LDA

LDA stands for linear discriminant analysis. It was proposed by the statistician R. A. Fisher, hence "Fisher linear discriminant". We project each sample onto one direction \(u\), giving a single number \(z = u\T x\). A good \(u\) makes the two class means far apart along \(u\) while each class stays tightly packed along \(u\).

Two matrices measure "far apart" and "tightly packed".

\[S_B = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)(\mu_1-\mu_2)\T\]
\[S_W = S_1 + S_2,\]
\[S_k = \sum_{x\in\omega_k}(x-\mu_k)(x-\mu_k)\T.\]
Reading the formula

\(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\) (omega) name the two classes. \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) (mu) are the class means. \(S_B\) is the between-class scatter, built only from the gap between the means. \(S_W\) is the within-class scatter, the spread of each class around its own mean, added for both classes.

Projected onto \(u\), the gap between the projected means squared is \((u\T\mu_1 - u\T\mu_2)^2 \)\({}= u\T S_B u\). The spread of the projected samples is \(u\T S_W u\). Fisher's criterion is the ratio

\[J(u) = \frac{u\T S_B u}{u\T S_W u}.\]

A large top and a small bottom both make \(J\) large, so we maximise it.

Write this in the exam

Show that the Fisher direction is \(u \propto S_W^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_2)\).

  1. \(J(u)\) does not change if \(u\) is scaled, so fix \(u\T S_W u = 1\) and maximise \(u\T S_B u\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. Replace \(u\) by \(cu\). Top and bottom both get a factor \(c^2\), which cancels.
    2. So only the direction of \(u\) matters. We may choose its length so that the bottom equals 1.
  2. Lagrangian \(L \)\({}= \frac12 u\T S_B u - \frac{\lambda}{2}(u\T S_W u - 1)\). Setting its derivative to zero gives \(S_B u = \lambda S_W u\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Both matrices are symmetric, so the derivative of \(\frac12 u\T S u\) with respect to \(u\) is \(Su\).
    2. The derivative of \(L\) is \(S_B u - \lambda S_W u\).
    3. Set it to zero. \(S_B u = \lambda S_W u\). This is called a generalised eigenvalue problem, because a matrix \(S_W\) appears on the right where an ordinary eigenproblem has \(I\).
  3. \(S_B u \)\({}= (\mu_1-\mu_2)\big((\mu_1-\mu_2)\T u\big)\) is always a multiple of \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. Group the product as \((\mu_1-\mu_2)\,[(\mu_1-\mu_2)\T u]\).
    2. The bracket is a dot product, a single number. Call it \(c\). So \(S_B u = c\,(\mu_1-\mu_2)\).
  4. So \(\lambda S_W u = c(\mu_1-\mu_2)\). Multiply both sides by \(S_W^{-1}\). This gives \(u \)\({}= \frac{c}{\lambda}S_W^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_2)\).
  5. Only the direction matters, so \(u \propto S_W^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2)\). (The symbol \(\propto\) means "is a multiple of".)

Compare with PCA. PCA needed an eigenvector computation. Two-class LDA needs only one matrix inverse, because \(S_B\) has rank 1.

Common mistake

The direction joining the two means, \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\), is not the LDA answer unless \(S_W\) is a multiple of \(I\). When the classes are stretched, \(S_W^{-1}\) turns the direction away from the stretch, so that the spread along \(u\) stays small.

Remember
  • \(J(u) \)\({}= \dfrac{u\T S_B u}{u\T S_W u}\), maximised by \(u \propto S_W^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2)\).
  • PCA is unsupervised and keeps variance. LDA is supervised and keeps class separation.
  • t-SNE is nonlinear and for visualisation only.

2.3Low-rank approximation and matrix completion

Lecture L08 · Short video: SVD · Short video: Rank and recommendations · Read: Stanford CS168 notes on low-rank approximation

Cut the SVD after \(k\) terms and you get the closest rank-\(k\) matrix. Real data matrices are often close to low rank, which is what makes recommendations possible.

Recall the SVD from Page 1. Any \(m\times n\) matrix can be written \(A \)\({}= UDV\T \)\({}= \sum_i d_i\, u_i v_i\T\), where the \(d_i\) are the singular values (non-negative, largest first), the \(u_i\) are orthonormal columns of \(U\) and the \(v_i\) orthonormal columns of \(V\). Each term \(d_iu_iv_i\T\) is a rank-1 matrix.

To get the best rank-\(k\) approximation, keep the first \(k\) terms. \(\hat A \)\({}= \sum_{i=1}^k d_iu_iv_i\T \)\({}= U_kD_kV_k\T\), where \(U_k\) and \(V_k\) are the first \(k\) columns. "Best" means the sum of squared entry differences \(\sum_{i,j}(A_{ij} - \hat A_{ij})^2\) is as small as possible among all rank-\(k\) matrices, and that smallest value equals \(d_{k+1}^2 + d_{k+2}^2 + \dots\).

Where low-rank matrices appear

A user-by-movie rating matrix is mostly empty (sparse), and its filled part is close to low rank, because tastes come in a few kinds. A term-by-document matrix for documents that are either medical or legal is close to rank 2, because there are two topics. A rank-1 matrix has every row a multiple of every other row.

Matrix completion fills in missing entries by assuming the full matrix has low rank. The formulation on the slide is

\[\min_B \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal K} (A_{ij} - B_{ij})^2\]

such that \(\operatorname{rank}(B) = 1\). Here \(\mathcal K\) is the set of known positions.

Reading the formula

The sum runs over the positions \((i,j)\) where \(A\) has a known number. The minimum is over all matrices \(B\) of the same size. So we look for a rank-1 \(B\) that agrees with \(A\) where \(A\) is known. Its other entries are the guesses.

The rank condition is hard to optimise, because rank counts non-zero singular values (an "L0 norm" of the singular values, like the L0 norm of a vector). The usual fix is to penalise the nuclear norm \(\|B\|_* = \sum_i d_i\), the sum of singular values. This is the L1 norm of the singular values, and it relates to rank the way Lasso's L1 penalty relates to counting non-zero weights.

2.4LSI

Lecture L08 · Short video: LSI · Read: latent semantic analysis

Take the SVD of a term-by-document matrix, keep \(k\) dimensions, and compare documents there. Documents about the same topic come out close even when they share no words.

LSI stands for latent semantic indexing. "Latent" means hidden. The hidden things are topics. "Semantic" means to do with meaning. "Indexing" is the search-engine word for organising documents so they can be found.

Start from a term-document matrix \(C\). Row \(t\) is a word, column \(j\) is a document, and the entry is 1 if the word occurs in the document (or a count). Each document is a column vector, and similar documents should be similar vectors. Comparing raw columns fails when two documents use different words for the same thing, such as "ship" and "boat" (synonymy).

Take the SVD \(C = UDV\T\) and keep \(k\) terms. Each document \(d\) (a column of \(C\)) gets a short \(k\)-number code

\[\hat d = d\T U_k D_k^{-1}.\]
Reading the formula

\(d\) is a 5-number column (one per word). \(U_k\) is \(5\times k\), the first \(k\) columns of \(U\). So \(d\T U_k\) is a row of \(k\) numbers, how much of each "topic direction" the document contains. Dividing by \(D_k\) scales each topic by its singular value.

For a document already in the matrix this gives exactly its row of \(V_k\). Since \(C = UDV\T\), we have \(C\T U = VD\), so \(C\T U_kD_k^{-1} = V_k\). That is why a new query mapped the same way can be compared with the rows of \(V_k\).

Example. Document \(d_3\) contains only "ship", so \(d_3 = [1,0,0,0,0]\T\). Then \(d_3\T U_2 = [-0.44, -0.30]\) (the "ship" row of \(U\)). Divide by 2.16 and 1.59. \(\hat d_3 = [-0.204, -0.189]\), which matches column \(d_3\) of \(V\T\) on the slide (\(-0.20\) and \(-0.19\)).

In the original space, \(d_2 = [0,1,1,0,0]\T\) and \(d_3 = [1,0,0,0,0]\T\) have dot product 0, so their cosine similarity is 0. In the 2-D latent space, \(\hat d_2 = [-0.282, -0.528]\) and \(\hat d_3 = [-0.204, -0.189]\), and their cosine similarity is about 0.95. LSI has learned that ship, boat and ocean go together, because they occur together across the collection.

Remember
  • LSI is a truncated SVD of the term-document matrix. Documents become rows of \(V_k\). A query \(q\) becomes \(q\T U_kD_k^{-1}\).
  • Compare in the latent space with cosine similarity, \(\cos(a,b) \)\({}= \dfrac{a\T b}{\|a\|\,\|b\|}\).
  • It helps with synonymy (different words, same meaning) and with clustering documents by topic.

2.5Bayesian decision

Lectures L09, L10, Tutorial 2 · Short video: Geometry of Gaussians · Short video: Data from multivariate distribution

Pick the class with the larger posterior probability. No classifier makes fewer mistakes on average, and with Gaussian classes this rule turns into nearest-mean, linear or quadratic boundaries.

The basics page has Bayes' theorem. Here it is in classification language. There are classes \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \dots\) (omega). A sample has measured features \(x\). Then

\[P(\omega_i\mid x) = \frac{p(x\mid\omega_i)\,P(\omega_i)}{p(x)}\]
\[p(x) = \sum_j p(x\mid\omega_j)P(\omega_j)\]
Reading the formula

\(P(\omega_i)\) is the prior, how common class \(i\) is before looking at \(x\). \(p(x\mid\omega_i)\) is the likelihood, how typical \(x\) is for class \(i\). \(P(\omega_i\mid x)\) is the posterior, the probability of class \(i\) after seeing \(x\). The bottom, \(p(x)\), is the evidence. It adds the top over all classes so that the posteriors add up to 1. In words, posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence.

A small \(p\) is used for a density (continuous \(x\)) and a capital \(P\) for a probability. The vertical bar \(\mid\) is read "given".

Example. Two classes with priors 0.9 and 0.1. A sample has likelihoods 0.2 under class 1 and 0.6 under class 2. Tops are \(0.2\times0.9 = 0.18\) and \(0.6\times 0.1 = 0.06\). Evidence \(= 0.24\). Posteriors are \(0.18/0.24 = 0.75\) and \(0.06/0.24 = 0.25\). They add to 1, and class 1 wins although its likelihood was smaller, because its prior is much larger.

The Bayes decision rule is to decide \(\omega_1\) if \(P(\omega_1\mid x) > P(\omega_2\mid x)\), otherwise \(\omega_2\). Since both posteriors have the same bottom \(p(x)\), the rule is the same as comparing \(p(x\mid\omega_1)P(\omega_1)\) with \(p(x\mid\omega_2)P(\omega_2)\). The "naive decision" from L09 that looks only at priors ("decide \(\omega_1\) if \(P(\omega_1) > P(\omega_2)\)") ignores \(x\) entirely. It is a near-blind guess.

Why the Bayes rule makes the fewest mistakes

Write this in the exam

Show that the Bayes decision rule minimises the probability of error.

  1. For a given \(x\), if we decide \(\omega_1\), the chance of being wrong is \(P(\omega_2\mid x)\). If we decide \(\omega_2\), it is \(P(\omega_1\mid x)\).
  2. The total error is \(P(\text{error}) \)\({}= \int P(\text{error}\mid x)\,p(x)\,dx\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. This is the sum rule (total probability) with a continuous \(x\). Average the error at each \(x\), weighted by how often that \(x\) occurs.
    2. For a discrete \(x\), the integral is a sum \(\sum_x P(\text{error}\mid x)P(x)\).
  3. \(p(x) \ge 0\) everywhere, so the integral is smallest when \(P(\text{error}\mid x)\) is smallest at every single \(x\).
  4. At each \(x\), the smaller of the two error chances is \(\min\{P(\omega_1\mid x), P(\omega_2\mid x)\}\), reached by deciding the class with the larger posterior. That is the Bayes rule.

This is why the course calls the Bayes classifier "optimal minimum error classification". In practice we do not know the true \(p(x\mid\omega_i)\) and must estimate it from data, which is what MLE (section 2.6) is for.

MAP and ML decisions, and risk

Choosing the class with the largest posterior is called MAP, maximum a posteriori ("a posteriori" is Latin for "from what comes after", meaning after seeing \(x\)). If the priors are equal, they cancel, and the rule becomes "pick the class with the largest likelihood \(p(x\mid\omega_i)\)". That is the ML (maximum likelihood) decision.

Some mistakes cost more than others. Missing a disease is worse than a false alarm. L10 adds weights \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\) (the cost of getting each class wrong) and the rule becomes to decide \(\omega_1\) if \(\lambda_1P(\omega_1\mid x) \ge \lambda_2 P(\omega_2\mid x)\). A large \(\lambda_1\) makes us call \(\omega_1\) more often, because missing it is expensive.

Gaussian classes give simple boundaries

Suppose each class is Gaussian (normal). The 1-D density with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) (sigma squared) is

\[p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).\]

Here \(\exp(a)\) means \(e^a\). The \(d\)-dimensional version with mean vector \(\mu\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) (capital sigma) is

\[p(x\mid\mu,\Sigma) = \frac{e^{-\Delta^2/2}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\]
\[\Delta^2 = (x-\mu)\T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\]
Reading the formula

\(|\Sigma|\) is the determinant of \(\Sigma\). The front fraction only makes the total probability 1. All the shape is in the exponent \(-\Delta^2/2\). The quantity \(\Delta^2\) (capital delta squared) is the squared Mahalanobis distance from \(x\) to \(\mu\). It is the ordinary squared distance when \(\Sigma = I\), and otherwise it counts distance in units of the spread along each direction.

Points with equal density lie on ellipses whose axes are the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\). A positive off-diagonal entry tilts the cloud up to the right, a negative one down to the right, and a diagonal \(\Sigma\) gives an ellipse lined up with the axes.

Take logs of prior × likelihood. Since log is increasing, comparing logs gives the same decision. For class \(i\) define the discriminant

\[g_i(x) = -\tfrac12\Delta_i^2 - \tfrac12\ln|\Sigma_i| + \ln P(\omega_i)\]
\[\Delta_i^2 = (x-\mu_i)\T\Sigma_i^{-1}(x-\mu_i)\]

(dropping the \(-\tfrac d2\ln 2\pi\) that every class shares). It is the log of prior × likelihood. Decide the class with the largest \(g_i\). The four cases follow.

  1. Same variance, 1-D, equal priors. Decide the nearer mean. The boundary is the midpoint \(\frac{\mu_1+\mu_2}{2}\), and the rule is \(\operatorname{sign}\left(x - \frac{\mu_1+\mu_2}2\right)\). This is "nearest mean" or template matching.
  2. \(\Sigma_i = \sigma^2I\) for both. \(g_i\) becomes \(-\frac{\|x-\mu_i\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\) plus a constant. With equal priors that is nearest mean in ordinary distance, and the boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining the means.
  3. Same \(\Sigma\) for both. Expanding \(g_i\), the term \(x\T\Sigma^{-1}x\) is the same for both classes and cancels in \(g_1 - g_2\). What is left is linear in \(x\), so the boundary is a straight line (a hyperplane). This connects Bayes with the linear classifier \(\operatorname{sign}(w\T x + b)\) of Page 1.
  4. Different \(\Sigma_i\). The \(x\T\Sigma_i^{-1}x\) terms no longer cancel. The boundary is quadratic (a curve such as an ellipse or a parabola).
Write this in the exam

Two 1-D Gaussian classes with equal variance \(\sigma^2\), means \(\mu_1 \lt \mu_2\) and priors \(P_1, P_2\). Find the decision threshold.

  1. Decide \(\omega_1\) when \(P_1 e^{-(x-\mu_1)^2/2\sigma^2} > P_2 e^{-(x-\mu_2)^2/2\sigma^2}\). The factors \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\) cancel.
  2. Take logs. \(\ln P_1 - \frac{(x-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma^2} > \ln P_2 - \frac{(x-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma^2}\).
  3. Collect the squares. \((x-\mu_2)^2 - (x-\mu_1)^2 > 2\sigma^2\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}\).
  4. Expand. \(2x(\mu_1 - \mu_2) + \mu_2^2 - \mu_1^2 > 2\sigma^2\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. \((x-\mu_2)^2 \)\({}= x^2 - 2x\mu_2 + \mu_2^2\).
    2. \((x-\mu_1)^2 \)\({}= x^2 - 2x\mu_1 + \mu_1^2\).
    3. Subtract. The \(x^2\) terms cancel and we get \(-2x\mu_2 + 2x\mu_1 + \mu_2^2 - \mu_1^2\).
  5. Solve for \(x\). The result is decide \(\omega_1\) when \(x \lt x^*\), with \[x^* = \frac{\mu_1+\mu_2}{2} + \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu_2-\mu_1}\ln\frac{P_1}{P_2}.\]
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Move \(\mu_2^2 - \mu_1^2\) to the right. \[2x(\mu_1-\mu_2) > 2\sigma^2\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} - (\mu_2^2 - \mu_1^2).\]
    2. Divide by \(2(\mu_1 - \mu_2)\), which is negative, so the inequality flips. \(x \lt \frac{\mu_2^2-\mu_1^2}{2(\mu_2-\mu_1)} - \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu_2-\mu_1}\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}\).
    3. Use \(\mu_2^2 - \mu_1^2 \)\({}= (\mu_2-\mu_1)(\mu_2+\mu_1)\). The first fraction is \(\frac{\mu_1+\mu_2}{2}\).
    4. Use \(-\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} \)\({}= \ln\frac{P_1}{P_2}\). This gives the boxed formula.
  6. With equal priors, \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(x^* = \frac{\mu_1+\mu_2}{2}\), the midpoint. A larger \(P_1\) moves \(x^*\) towards \(\mu_2\), so class 1 gets more of the line.
Remember
  • The Bayes rule picks the largest \(p(x\mid\omega_i)P(\omega_i)\). It has the least probability of error.
  • MAP uses priors. ML ignores them (same as MAP with equal priors).
  • With Gaussian classes, a shared \(\sigma^2 I\) gives the perpendicular bisector, shared \(\Sigma\) gives a line, different \(\Sigma_i\) give a quadratic curve.
  • The equal-variance 1-D threshold is \(x^* \)\({}= \frac{\mu_1+\mu_2}{2} + \frac{\sigma^2}{\mu_2-\mu_1}\ln\frac{P_1}{P_2}\).

2.6Maximum likelihood

Lectures L09, L10 · Short video: MLE · Short video: MSE as MLE

Choose the parameters under which the data you actually saw is most probable. For Gaussians this gives the sample mean and variance, and for linear regression with Gaussian noise it gives least squares.

A model here is a family of distributions with knobs, the parameters, written \(\theta\) (theta). A coin has one parameter, its head probability \(p\). A Gaussian has two, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\).

The likelihood of \(\theta\) is the probability of the observed data computed as if \(\theta\) were true, \(L(\theta) \)\({}= P(\text{data}\mid\theta)\). It is read as a function of \(\theta\) with the data held fixed. If the samples are independent, it is a product.

\[L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^N p(x_i\mid\theta)\]
\[\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \argmax_\theta L(\theta)\]
Reading the formula

\(\prod\) (capital pi) multiplies terms, the way \(\sum\) adds them. So \(\prod_{i=1}^3 p(x_i\mid\theta) \)\({}= p(x_1\mid\theta)\,p(x_2\mid\theta)\,p(x_3\mid\theta)\). The \(\argmax\) returns the \(\theta\) at which the function is largest, not the largest value itself.

Example. A coin gives H, T, T, H, T. With head probability \(p\), \(L(p) \)\({}= p\cdot(1-p)(1-p)\cdot p\cdot(1-p) \)\({}= p^2(1-p)^3\). At \(p = 0.3\), \(L = 0.09\times 0.343 = 0.0309\). At \(p = 0.4\), \(L = 0.16\times 0.216 = 0.0346\). At \(p = 0.5\), \(L = 0.03125\). The best of these is 0.4, which is the fraction of heads, \(\frac25\). L10's other example is \(L(0.5\mid\text{HH}) \)\({}= 0.5^2 \)\({}= 0.25\).

We maximise the log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta) \)\({}= \ln L(\theta) \)\({}= \sum_i\ln p(x_i\mid\theta)\) instead. Three reasons. The log turns the product into a sum, which is easier to differentiate. A product of many small probabilities underflows to 0 on a computer, and a sum of logs does not. And \(\ln\) is increasing, so the \(\theta\) that maximises \(\ln L\) also maximises \(L\). Minimising the negative log-likelihood (NLL) is the same thing, and that is how a likelihood becomes a loss function.

Write this in the exam

Coin: \(h\) heads in \(N\) tosses. Show that \(\hat p = h/N\).

  1. \(L(p) = p^h(1-p)^{N-h}\), so \(\ell(p) \)\({}= h\ln p + (N-h)\ln(1-p)\).
  2. \(\frac{d\ell}{dp} \)\({}= \frac hp - \frac{N-h}{1-p} \)\({}= 0\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. \(\frac{d}{dp}\ln p = \frac1p\).
    2. \(\frac{d}{dp}\ln(1-p) \)\({}= \frac{-1}{1-p}\) by the chain rule (the inside \(1-p\) has derivative \(-1\)).
  3. Multiply by \(p(1-p)\). \(h(1-p) = (N-h)p\), so \(h = Np\) and \(\hat p = \frac hN\).
Write this in the exam

Samples \(x_1,\dots,x_N\) from \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). Find the MLE of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\).

  1. \(\ell(\mu,\sigma^2) \)\({}= \sum_{i=1}^N\left[-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Take the log of one Gaussian density. \(\ln\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \)\({}= -\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)\).
    2. The log of \(\exp(a)\) is \(a\), so the exponent comes down as \(-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\).
    3. Add over the \(N\) samples (the log of a product is the sum of logs).
  2. \(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} \)\({}= \sum_i\frac{x_i-\mu}{\sigma^2} \)\({}= 0\) gives \(\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_i x_i\), the sample mean.
    Why (4 steps)
    1. The first term has no \(\mu\), so its derivative is 0.
    2. \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(x_i-\mu)^2 \)\({}= -2(x_i-\mu)\), so \(-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\) has derivative \(\frac{x_i-\mu}{\sigma^2}\).
    3. Set the sum to 0 and multiply by \(\sigma^2\). \(\sum_i x_i - N\mu = 0\).
    4. Divide by \(N\). \(\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_ix_i\).
  3. Write \(v = \sigma^2\). \(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v} \)\({}= -\frac{N}{2v} + \frac{1}{2v^2}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2 \)\({}= 0\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. \(-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi v)\) has derivative \(-\frac{1}{2v}\), and there are \(N\) copies.
    2. \(-\frac{a}{2v}\) has derivative \(+\frac{a}{2v^2}\), with \(a = (x_i-\mu)^2\).
  4. Multiply by \(2v^2\). \(-Nv + \sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2 = 0\), so \(\hat\sigma^2 \)\({}= \frac1N\sum_i(x_i-\hat\mu)^2\).

The MLE variance divides by \(N\), not \(N-1\). Statistics courses often use \(N-1\) to remove a small bias. In this course the MLE answer with \(N\) is the one to write.

The same steps in \(d\) dimensions give \(\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_ix_i\) and \(\hat\Sigma \)\({}= \frac1N\sum_i(x_i-\hat\mu)(x_i-\hat\mu)\T\) (L10). The mean also has a direct meaning from L09. It is the point \(y\) that minimises \(\sum_i\|x_i - y\|^2\), the total squared distance to all the samples.

Least squares is maximum likelihood

Write this in the exam

Assume \(y_i = w\T x_i + \epsilon_i\) with \(\epsilon_i\) independent \(N(0,\sigma^2)\). Show that the MLE of \(w\) minimises the MSE.

  1. Then \(y_i\) given \(x_i\) is \(N(w\T x_i, \sigma^2)\). So \(p(y_i\mid x_i, w) \)\({}= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y_i - w\T x_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. \(\epsilon_i\) (epsilon) is the noise. Adding the fixed number \(w\T x_i\) to a \(N(0,\sigma^2)\) variable shifts its mean to \(w\T x_i\) and keeps the variance.
    2. So the density of \(y_i\) is the Gaussian formula with \(\mu = w\T x_i\).
  2. Take logs and add over samples. Write \(K = -\frac N2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)\), which does not depend on \(w\). \[\ell(w) = K - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i - w\T x_i)^2.\]
  3. The factor \(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) does not depend on \(w\) either. Write it as \(c > 0\), so \(\ell(w) \)\({}= K - c\sum_i(y_i - w\T x_i)^2\).
  4. Maximising \(\ell\) is therefore minimising \(\sum_i(y_i - w\T x_i)^2\), the squared error. MSE is the NLL up to a scale and a constant.

Streaming mean and variance

Sometimes samples arrive one at a time and we cannot keep them all ("streaming data"). Keep the count \(N\), the mean \(\mu_N\) and the mean of squares \(m_N = \frac1N\sum x_i^2\). When \(x_{N+1}\) arrives,

\[\mu_{N+1} = \frac{N\mu_N + x_{N+1}}{N+1}\]
\[m_{N+1} = \frac{N m_N + x_{N+1}^2}{N+1}\]
\[\sigma^2_{N+1} = m_{N+1} - \mu_{N+1}^2\]
Reading the formula

\(N\mu_N\) is the old total. Add the new sample and divide by the new count. The variance uses the identity \(\sigma^2 \)\({}= \text{mean of squares} - (\text{mean})^2\), which follows from expanding \(\frac1N\sum(x_i-\mu)^2\). Substitute \(m_N = \sigma_N^2 + \mu_N^2\) to update from \(\sigma^2_N\) directly.

Example. Samples 4, 8, 6, 10. After 4 and 8, \(\mu_2 = 6\) and \(m_2 = (16+64)/2 = 40\), so \(\sigma_2^2 = 40 - 36 = 4\). Add 6: \(\mu_3 = (12 + 6)/3 = 6\), \(m_3 = (80 + 36)/3 = 38.67\), \(\sigma_3^2 = 38.67 - 36 = 2.67\). Add 10. \(\mu_4 = (18+10)/4 = 7\), \(m_4 = (116 + 100)/4 = 54\), \(\sigma^2_4 = 54 - 49 = 5\). Check directly. The deviations from 7 are \(-3, 1, -1, 3\), squares sum to 20, and \(20/4 = 5\).

This is needed in batch normalisation in neural networks, where running means and variances are kept as batches stream past.

2.7GMMs and EM

Lectures L10, L11, Tutorial 2 · Read: EM explained · Read: EM for GMMs

A GMM models data as a weighted mix of a few Gaussians. EM fits it by repeating two steps. First guess which Gaussian each point came from, then refit each Gaussian to the points it was given.

One Gaussian has one peak. Real data often has several clusters (it is multimodal). A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) adds \(K\) Gaussians with weights.

\[p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\,N(x\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\]
\[0\le\pi_k\le 1, \quad \sum_k\pi_k = 1\]
Reading the formula

Here \(\pi_k\) (pi) is a weight, not 3.14. It is the mixing coefficient, the fraction of data that comes from component \(k\). Think of making one sample in two stages. First pick a component \(k\) with probability \(\pi_k\). Then draw \(x\) from that component's Gaussian. The component picked is hidden from us. A hidden variable like this is called a latent variable.

The weights add to 1 so that \(p(x)\) is still a density with total probability 1.

Example. \(K = 2\) in 1-D with \(\pi = (0.5, 0.5)\), means 1 and 6, both variances 1. At \(x = 2\) the first component gives \(0.242\) and the second \(0.0001\) (the Gaussian formula with \(\sigma = 1\)). So \(p(2) \)\({}= 0.5(0.242) + 0.5(0.0001) \)\({}= 0.121\).

Why plain MLE gets stuck

The log-likelihood is \(\sum_i\ln\sum_k\pi_kN(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\). The sum inside the log does not split, so setting derivatives to zero gives equations with no closed-form answer. L10 notes the way round it. If we knew which component each point came from, fitting would be easy (the MLE of section 2.6 on each group). If we knew the components, we could work out which one each point probably came from. EM alternates between these two easy problems.

The probability that component \(k\) produced point \(x\) is a posterior, by Bayes with \(\pi_k\) as the prior. It is called the responsibility.

\[\gamma_k(x) = P(k\mid x) = \frac{\pi_k\,N(x\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j\,N(x\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}.\]
Write this in the exam

The EM algorithm for a GMM.

  1. Initialise \(\mu_k\), \(\Sigma_k\), \(\pi_k\) for every \(k\), and compute the log-likelihood.
  2. E-step (expectation). For every point \(x_i\) and component \(k\), compute \(\gamma_k(x_i)\) with the formula above.
    Why (2 steps)
    1. The top is prior × likelihood for component \(k\). The bottom adds this over all components.
    2. So for each point, the \(K\) responsibilities add up to 1. They are a soft assignment, so point \(i\) belongs 0.9 to one component and 0.1 to another, say.
  3. M-step (maximisation). Let \(N_k = \sum_i\gamma_k(x_i)\). Update \(\mu_k \)\({}= \frac1{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_k(x_i)\,x_i\), \(\Sigma_k \)\({}= \frac1{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_k(x_i)(x_i-\mu_k)(x_i-\mu_k)\T\) and \(\pi_k = \frac{N_k}N\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. \(N_k\) is the effective number of points in component \(k\), each point counted by the fraction it belongs.
    2. The new mean and covariance are the ordinary MLE formulas of section 2.6, but each point is weighted by its responsibility.
    3. The new weight is the fraction of all \(N\) points that component \(k\) is responsible for.
  4. Recompute the log-likelihood. If it has stopped changing, stop. Otherwise go back to line 2.

Each EM round never decreases the log-likelihood, so EM converges. But it converges to a local optimum, which depends on the initialisation.

This is the known way a GMM breaks. It is a bad answer that happens to have a huge likelihood. Real implementations keep a small floor under every variance, or restart from another initialisation.

GMMs are used whenever data has several modes, for example speech and image signals where two sources are mixed, and background subtraction in video (each pixel's colour over time is modelled as a mix of "background" and "moving object").

L10 slideWhy does setting the derivative of the GMM log-likelihood to zero not give a closed-form answer, as it did for one Gaussian?›

For one Gaussian, \(\ln N(x_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2)\) is a simple quadratic in \(\mu\), and the derivatives give linear equations. For a GMM the log is taken of a sum \(\sum_k\pi_kN(\cdot)\). EM handles this by freezing the responsibilities (E-step), solving the now simple weighted MLE (M-step), and repeating.

2.8k-means

Lectures L10, L11

k-means is EM with hard assignments. Put each point in the cluster with the nearest mean, recompute the means, repeat.

Clustering splits a set into groups that do not overlap and together cover everything ("mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive"). In set notation, \(A \)\({}= C_1\cup C_2\cup\dots\cup C_K\) with \(C_i\cap C_j = \emptyset\) for \(i\ne j\). L11 mentions two families. Agglomerative methods start with many small clusters and merge them (single linkage can be done with a minimum spanning tree). Divisive methods start with one cluster and split it. k-means is neither. It fixes \(K\) and improves a partition.

k-means minimises

\[J = \sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{x_i\in C_k}\|x_i - \mu_k\|^2,\]

the total squared distance from each point to the mean of its own cluster.

  1. Start with \(K\) means (or a random partition, then compute its means).
  2. Assignment step. Put each point in the cluster whose mean is nearest.
  3. Update step. Recompute each mean as the average of its cluster.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until nothing changes.
Write this in the exam

Show that each k-means step can only lower (or keep) \(J\), so the algorithm stops.

  1. In the assignment step the means are fixed. Moving \(x_i\) to its nearest mean replaces its term \(\|x_i-\mu_{\text{old}}\|^2\) by a value no larger. So \(J\) cannot rise.
  2. In the update step the clusters are fixed. The point that minimises \(\sum_{x\in C_k}\|x - y\|^2\) over \(y\) is the mean of \(C_k\). So replacing \(\mu_k\) by the mean cannot raise \(J\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. L09 showed this. Differentiate \(\sum_{x\in C_k}(x-y)\T(x-y)\) with respect to \(y\). You get \(\sum -2(x - y)\).
    2. Set it to zero. \(y \)\({}= \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{x\in C_k}x\), the mean.
  3. \(J \ge 0\) and there are only finitely many ways to split \(N\) points into \(K\) groups. A sequence that never rises over finitely many states must stop changing.

Comparison with GMMs. k-means is what EM for a GMM becomes when every component has the same tiny variance \(\sigma^2 I\) and equal weights. As \(\sigma\to 0\), the responsibilities become 1 for the nearest mean and 0 for the others (hard assignment), and the M-step for the means becomes the plain average. So k-means is "hard EM".

The answer depends on the starting means, as the second example below shows. In practice, run it from several starts and keep the lowest \(J\). Choosing \(K\) is also open. \(J\) always falls as \(K\) grows (with \(K = N\) every point is its own cluster and \(J = 0\)), so the smallest \(J\) is not a way to choose. A common method is to plot \(J\) against \(K\) and pick the "elbow" where the drop slows down.

Remember
  • Objective \(J \)\({}= \sum_k\sum_{x\in C_k}\|x-\mu_k\|^2\). Assign to nearest mean, recompute means, repeat.
  • Each step lowers \(J\) or keeps it, so it stops, but only at a local minimum that depends on the start.
  • k-means is hard-assignment EM for a GMM with equal spherical covariances.

2.9Naive Bayes

Lecture L11, Tutorial 2 · Short video: Naive Bayes

Assume the features are independent once the class is known. Then the class likelihood is a product of one-feature probabilities, which can be estimated from small data.

The Bayes rule needs \(P(x\mid y)\) for a whole feature vector \(x = (x_1,\dots,x_d)\). With \(d\) binary features there are \(2^d\) possible vectors. With \(d = 30\) that is about a billion, far more than any dataset, so most combinations are never seen and \(P(x\mid y)\) cannot be estimated by counting.

Features are conditionally independent given the class if

\[P(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_d\mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^d P(x_j\mid y).\]

This is weaker than full independence. Words such as "free" and "offer" are related overall, because both are common in spam. The assumption says that once we know the email is spam, seeing "free" tells us nothing more about "offer". That is usually false, which is why the method is called naive. But now we only need \(d\) one-feature tables per class, and it works well in practice, especially for text.

\[\hat y = \argmax_y\; P(y)\prod_{j=1}^dP(x_j\mid y).\]

In practice we add logs, \(\ln P(y) + \sum_j\ln P(x_j\mid y)\), to avoid underflow. If a word never appeared with a class in training, its count is 0 and the whole product becomes 0. The usual fix is to add 1 to every count (Laplace smoothing).

Remember
  • Conditional independence given the class turns \(P(x\mid y)\) into \(\prod_jP(x_j\mid y)\).
  • It reduces the data needed from about \(2^d\) combinations to \(d\) small tables.
  • Absent features count too, with \(P(\bar x_j\mid y) \)\({}= 1 - P(x_j\mid y)\).

2.10Logistic regression

Lecture L11 · Short video: Loss functions 1

A linear classifier that outputs a probability. Squash \(w\T x\) through the sigmoid, and train by maximum likelihood, which gives the cross-entropy loss.

Despite the name, logistic regression is a classifier. It is called regression because it fits a real-valued function (a probability) and then thresholds it. The logistic function, also called the sigmoid (S-shaped), is

\[g(z) = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.\]
Reading the formula

For large positive \(z\), \(e^{-z}\) is near 0, so \(g(z)\) is near 1. For large negative \(z\), \(e^{-z}\) is huge, so \(g(z)\) is near 0. At \(z = 0\), \(g = \frac1{1+1} = 0.5\). So \(g\) maps any real number into \((0,1)\), which makes it usable as a probability.

Example. \(g(-2) \)\({}= \frac1{1+e^2} \)\({}= \frac1{8.39} \)\({}= 0.119\). \(g(0) = 0.5\). \(g(2) \)\({}= \frac{1}{1+0.135} \)\({}= 0.881\). Note \(g(-2) + g(2) = 1\). In general \(g(-z) = 1 - g(z)\).

The model says \(p(y=1\mid x) = g(w\T x)\) and \(p(y \)\({}=0\mid x) \)\({}= 1 - g(w\T x) \)\({}= g(-w\T x)\). These add to 1. The decision rule is to output 1 when \(g(w\T x) \ge 0.5\), which happens exactly when \(w\T x\ge 0\). So the boundary is the hyperplane \(w\T x = 0\), the same as the linear classifier of Page 1. What is new is the confidence, and the way \(w\) is trained.

Write this in the exam

With labels \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\), derive the loss of logistic regression from maximum likelihood, and its gradient.

  1. One sample. \(p(y\mid x) \)\({}= g(z)^y(1-g(z))^{1-y}\) with \(z = w\T x\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. If \(y = 1\), the formula is \(g(z)^1(1-g(z))^0 = g(z)\), which is \(p(y=1\mid x)\).
    2. If \(y = 0\), it is \(g(z)^0(1-g(z))^1 = 1 - g(z)\), which is \(p(y = 0\mid x)\). The powers just pick the right case.
  2. Independent samples. \(\ln L(w) = \sum_i\ell_i\), where \[\ell_i = y_i\ln g(z_i) + (1-y_i)\ln(1-g(z_i)).\]
  3. The loss is the negative log-likelihood, \(L(w) = -\sum_i\ell_i\). It is called the binary cross-entropy.
  4. Use \(g'(z) = g(z)(1-g(z))\). Then \(\nabla_w L \)\({}= \sum_i (g(z_i) - y_i)\,x_i\).
    Why (6 steps)
    1. Work on one sample and drop \(i\). Write \(g = g(z)\).
    2. \(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln g \)\({}= \frac{g'}{g} \)\({}= \frac{g(1-g)}{g} \)\({}= 1 - g\).
    3. \(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\ln(1-g) \)\({}= \frac{-g'}{1-g} \)\({}= \frac{-g(1-g)}{1-g} \)\({}= -g\).
    4. The loss of one sample is \(-y\ln g - (1-y)\ln(1-g)\). By steps 2 and 3, its derivative with respect to \(z\) is \(-y(1-g) + (1-y)g\).
    5. Multiply out. \(-y + yg + g - yg = g - y\).
    6. Chain rule. \(z = w\T x\) has \(\frac{\partial z}{\partial w} \)\({}= x\), so the gradient is \((g - y)x\). Add over samples.
  5. Gradient descent. \(w \leftarrow w - \eta\sum_i(g(w\T x_i) - y_i)x_i\), with step size \(\eta\) (eta).

The gradient has the same shape as for linear regression, (prediction minus target) times input. There is no closed form here, so we use gradient descent. The loss is convex in \(w\), so gradient descent reaches the global minimum.

The ±1 form

With labels \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\), \(p(y=+1\mid x) = g(w\T x)\) and \(p(y=-1\mid x) = g(-w\T x)\). Both are \(g(y\,w\T x)\). So \(p(y_i\mid x_i) \)\({}= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-y_iw\T x_i}}\), and the NLL is

\[L(w) = \sum_i\ln\left(1 + e^{-y_iw\T x_i}\right).\]

The product \(y_i\,w\T x_i\) is called the margin of sample \(i\). It is positive when the sample is on the correct side. This form is compared with the SVM's hinge loss in section 2.11.

Softmax for more than two classes

Rewrite \(p(y \)\({}=1\mid x) \)\({}= \frac{e^{w\T x}}{1 + e^{w\T x}}\) (multiply top and bottom of \(g\) by \(e^{w\T x}\)). The top is a positive score for the class, and the bottom adds the scores of both classes (class 0 having score \(e^0 = 1\)). With \(K\) classes, give each class its own \(w_c\).

\[p(y = c\mid x) = \frac{e^{w_c\T x}}{\sum_{k=1}^Ke^{w_k\T x}}.\]
Reading the formula

Each class gets the score \(w_c\T x\). Exponentiating makes every score positive. Dividing by the total makes the \(K\) numbers add up to 1. This function is the softmax, a smooth version of "pick the largest". Classify into the class with the largest probability.

Example. Scores \(2, 1, 0\). Exponentials \(7.39, 2.72, 1\), total \(11.11\). Probabilities \(0.665, 0.245, 0.090\), which add to 1.

Softmax is the output layer of almost every modern classifier network.

Why not use squared error

One could try \(J(w) \)\({}= \frac1{2N}\sum_i(g(w\T x_i) - y_i)^2\). L11 says this is non-convex and asks for a proof. Here is a short one with a single sample \(x = 1\), \(y = 1\), so \(J(w) = (g(w) - 1)^2\). As \(w\to -\infty\), \(g\to 0\) and \(J\to 1\). As \(w\to\infty\), \(J\to 0\). So \(J\) is bounded above by 1 on the whole real line, and it is not constant. A convex function on the whole real line that is bounded above must be constant. Therefore \(J\) is not convex. In practice the flat region far to the left traps gradient descent, because the gradient there is almost zero even though the prediction is badly wrong. Cross-entropy has no such flat region for wrong predictions.

Remember
  • \(p(y \)\({}=1\mid x) \)\({}= \frac1{1+e^{-w\T x}}\). Decide 1 when \(w\T x \ge 0\).
  • The loss is the binary cross-entropy \(-\sum[y\ln g + (1-y)\ln(1-g)]\), and its gradient is \(\sum(g - y)x\).
  • With ±1 labels the loss is \(\sum\ln(1 + e^{-yw\T x})\). More than two classes use softmax.
  • \(g' = g(1-g)\), \(\tanh(z) = 2g(2z) - 1\), \(\tanh' = 1 - \tanh^2\).

2.11SVMs

Lecture L12 · Video: SVM 1 (Prof. Anoop) · Video: SVM 2

Among all lines that separate two classes, pick the one with the widest empty band around it. Only the points on the edge of the band, the support vectors, decide where it goes.

A perceptron (on the neural page) stops at any separating line. Logistic regression and SVMs both pick a best one. The SVM's idea of best is the maximum margin, the widest gap between the line and the nearest points. A wide gap tolerates small changes in new data, which is why it generalises well.

Setting up the constraints

Training points \(x_i\) have labels \(y_i = +1\) (class 1) or \(-1\) (class 2). The classifier is \(f(x) = w\T x + b\), deciding by its sign. Since \(w\) and \(b\) can be scaled freely without moving the line, we fix the scale by asking for

\[w\T x_i + b \ge +1 \ \text{ if } y_i = +1\]
\[w\T x_i + b\le -1 \ \text{ if } y_i = -1\]

which combine into one line, \(y_i(w\T x_i + b) \ge 1\) for every \(i\). The points where equality holds lie on the two planes \(H_1\colon w\T x + b = 1\) and \(H_2\colon w\T x + b = -1\). These points are the support vectors, so named because they "support" (hold in place) the two edge planes.

Write this in the exam

Show that the margin (the distance between \(H_1\) and \(H_2\)) is \(\frac{2}{\|w\|}\), and write the SVM problem.

  1. The distance from the origin to the plane \(w\T x + b = c\) is \(\frac{|c - b|}{\|w\|}\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. The plane's normal direction is \(w\). The nearest point of the plane to the origin is \(tw\) for some number \(t\).
    2. It lies on the plane, so \(w\T(tw) + b = c\), giving \(t\|w\|^2 = c - b\) and \(t = \frac{c-b}{\|w\|^2}\).
    3. Its distance from the origin is \(|t|\,\|w\| \)\({}= \frac{|c - b|}{\|w\|}\).
  2. \(H_1\) has \(c = 1\) and \(H_2\) has \(c = -1\). They are parallel, so the gap between them is the difference of signed distances, \(\frac{(1 - b) - (-1-b)}{\|w\|} \)\({}= \frac{2}{\|w\|}\).
  3. Maximising \(\frac2{\|w\|}\) is the same as minimising \(\|w\|\), and the same as minimising \(\frac12 w\T w\) (squaring and halving do not move the minimum and make the derivative tidy).
  4. Without constraints the minimum is \(w = 0\), which classifies nothing. So keep every point correctly outside the band. \[\min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12 w\T w\] subject to \(y_i(w\T x_i + b)\ge 1\) for all \(i\).

This is the primal problem. It is a quadratic objective with linear constraints (a quadratic program), so it has one global minimum and no bad local minima. That is one of the advantages listed on the slides.

The dual, at the level the course needs

L12 marks the Lagrange-multiplier derivation as out of scope, but uses its results. Giving each constraint a multiplier \(\alpha_i\ge 0\) (alpha) and eliminating \(w\) and \(b\) gives the dual problem.

\[J_d(\alpha) = \sum_i\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_i\sum_j\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,x_i\T x_j\]
\[J_d(\alpha) = \sum_i\alpha_i - \tfrac12 Q\] \[Q = \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,x_i\T x_j\]

Maximise \(J_d(\alpha)\) subject to \(\alpha_i\ge 0\) and \(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i = 0\).

Reading the formula

There is one \(\alpha_i\) per training point. Solving the dual gives the \(\alpha\)s. Then

\[w = \sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\]
\[b = \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}(y_s - w\T x_s)\]

where \(S\) is the set of support vectors and \(|S|\) its size. Only the support vectors have \(\alpha_i > 0\). Every other point has \(\alpha_i = 0\) and drops out of \(w\). So moving or deleting a non-support point does not change the answer.

The data appears in the dual only through dot products \(x_i\T x_j\). Section 2.12 uses this.

The \(b\) formula comes from \(y_s(w\T x_s + b) = 1\) for a support vector. Multiply by \(y_s\) (and \(y_s^2 = 1\)) to get \(b = y_s - w\T x_s\). Averaging over all support vectors reduces rounding error.

Training solves for \(\alpha\) (the support vectors and their weights). Testing computes \(\operatorname{sign}(w\T x + b)\), or in dual form \(\operatorname{sign}\left(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\,x_i\T x + b\right)\).

Soft margin, when no line separates the classes

Give each point a slack \(\xi_i\ge 0\) (xi) that lets it violate the margin. The constraint becomes \(y_i(w\T x_i + b)\ge 1 - \xi_i\). A point with \(0\lt\xi_i\lt 1\) is inside the band but on the right side. A point with \(\xi_i > 1\) is misclassified. The objective pays for slack.

The L1 SVM minimises the first of these and the L2 SVM the second.

\[\tfrac12w\T w + C\sum_i\xi_i\]
\[\tfrac12w\T w + \tfrac C2\sum_i\xi_i^2\]

\(C\) is a hyperparameter. A large \(C\) makes violations expensive, giving a narrow margin that fits the training data hard. A small \(C\) allows more violations and a wider margin. The L1 SVM's dual is the same as before, except that each \(\alpha_i\) is now capped, \(0\le\alpha_i\le C\).

\(\alpha_i\)What it means
\(\alpha_i = 0\)\(\xi_i = 0\). Correctly classified, outside the band. Not a support vector.
\(0\lt\alpha_i\lt C\)\(\xi_i = 0\) and \(y_i(w\T x_i+b) = 1\). On the edge plane. An unbounded support vector.
\(\alpha_i = C\)\(\xi_i\ge 0\). A bounded support vector inside the band. Correct if \(\xi_i \lt 1\), misclassified if \(\xi_i > 1\).

Hinge loss compared with logistic loss

The soft-margin SVM is the same as minimising \(C\sum_i\max(0, 1 - y_if(x_i)) + \|w\|^2\) (up to the factor ½). The term \(\max(0, 1 - y_if(x_i))\) is the hinge loss. Logistic regression minimises \(\sum_i\ln(1 + e^{-y_if(x_i)}) + \lambda\|w\|^2\). On the L11 plot both are drawn against the margin \(y_if(x_i)\), together with the 0-1 loss (1 for a mistake, 0 otherwise). Both approximate the 0-1 loss from above and behave alike for very wrong points. The differences are that the hinge has a corner at 1 and is exactly 0 beyond it, while the logistic loss is smooth and never quite 0. Because the hinge is exactly 0 for points safely outside the margin, those points get \(\alpha_i = 0\), and the SVM solution is sparse (it depends on few points).

The slides list pros and cons. On the plus side, maximising the margin aims directly at generalisation, the problem is convex (no local minima), and \(C\) controls outliers. On the minus side, more than two classes needs extra schemes (one class against the rest, or one against one), training on very large data is slow because the dual has one variable per point, and the kernel, its parameters and \(C\) must be chosen.

  • The dual is \(\max\sum\alpha_i - \frac12\sum\sum\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jx_i\T x_j\), \(\alpha_i\ge 0\), \(\sum\alpha_iy_i = 0\). Then \(w = \sum\alpha_iy_ix_i\).
  • Support vectors are the points with \(\alpha_i > 0\). Soft margin adds slack \(\xi_i\) and caps \(\alpha_i\le C\).

L12 slideWhat makes a point a support vector, and why do only support vectors matter?›

Support vectors satisfy \(y_i(w\T x_i + b) = 1\) (on the edge planes), or in the soft-margin case lie inside the band. They are the points closest to the boundary and the hardest to classify. In the dual, only they have \(\alpha_i > 0\), and \(w = \sum\alpha_iy_ix_i\), so \(w\) and \(b\) are built from them alone. Removing any other point and retraining gives the same classifier.

L12 slideThe slides say a perceptron finds "a valid solution" but LR and SVM find "an optimal solution". What is the difference?›

The perceptron stops at the first line that separates the training data, and which line that is depends on the order of samples and the start. Logistic regression and the SVM each minimise a convex loss (cross-entropy, or hinge plus \(\|w\|^2\)). Each has a single best answer, the one with the most confident probabilities or the widest margin.

2.12Kernels

Lecture L12

Map the data into a bigger feature space where it becomes linearly separable. Because the SVM only uses dot products, a kernel function gives those dot products without ever building the big vectors.

XOR (exclusive or) is the classic data set that no line can separate. Use ±1 coding. Points \((1,1)\) and \((-1,-1)\) are class A, and \((1,-1)\) and \((-1,1)\) are class B. Any line that puts both A points on one side puts at least one B point there too.

Add the feature \(x_1x_2\). For the A points it is \(+1\), and for the B points it is \(-1\). In the new feature, a threshold at 0 separates them. A feature map \(\phi\) (phi) turns \(x\) into a longer vector \(\phi(x)\), and a linear classifier on \(\phi(x)\) is a curved classifier on \(x\).

The dual SVM and its test rule use only \(x_i\T x_j\). Replace each dot product by \(\phi(x_i)\T\phi(x_j)\). A kernel is a function that computes this directly.

\[K(p, q) = \phi(p)\T\phi(q)\]
\[J_d(\alpha) = \sum_i\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jK(x_i,x_j)\]
\[J_d(\alpha) = \sum_i\alpha_i - \tfrac12 Q\] \[Q = \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jK(x_i,x_j)\]
\[f(x) = \sum_i\alpha_iy_iK(x_i, x) + b\]

This is the kernel trick. We never write \(\phi(x)\) down. The kernel matrix (Gram matrix) has entries \(K_{ij} = K(x_i, x_j)\), and in matrix form the dual is \(\max_\alpha\ \alpha\T\mathbf 1 - \frac12\alpha\T\operatorname{diag}(y)K\operatorname{diag}(y)\alpha\) with \(\alpha\T y = 0\), \(0\le\alpha\le C\).

Write this in the exam

In 2-D, find \(\phi\) for \(K(p,q) = (p\T q)^2\) and for \(K(p,q) = (p\T q + 1)^2\).

  1. \((p\T q)^2 \)\({}= (p_1q_1 + p_2q_2)^2 \)\({}= p_1^2q_1^2 + 2p_1p_2q_1q_2 + p_2^2q_2^2\).
  2. Split each term into a \(p\) part times a \(q\) part. So \(\phi(p) \)\({}= (p_1^2,\ \sqrt2\,p_1p_2,\ p_2^2)\), a 3-D map.
    Why (2 steps)
    1. \(p_1^2q_1^2 = (p_1^2)(q_1^2)\) and \(p_2^2q_2^2 = (p_2^2)(q_2^2)\).
    2. \(2p_1p_2q_1q_2 \)\({}= (\sqrt2p_1p_2)(\sqrt2q_1q_2)\). The \(\sqrt2\) is split evenly so both vectors use the same map.
  3. \((p\T q + 1)^2 \)\({}= (p\T q)^2 + 2p\T q + 1\). Expand each piece with line 1, and \(2p\T q = 2p_1q_1 + 2p_2q_2\).
  4. So \(\phi(p) \)\({}= (1,\ \sqrt2p_1,\ \sqrt2p_2,\ p_1^2,\ \sqrt2p_1p_2,\ p_2^2)\), a 6-D map.
Example

Take \(p = (1,2)\) and \(q = (3,1)\). Then \(p\T q = 3 + 2 = 5\).

First kernel. \(K = 25\). Via the map, \(\phi(p) = (1, 2\sqrt2, 4)\) and \(\phi(q) = (9, 3\sqrt2, 1)\). Dot product \(9 + 2\sqrt2\cdot3\sqrt2 + 4 \)\({}= 9 + 12 + 4 \)\({}= 25\). The same.

Second kernel. \(K = 36\). Via the map, \(\phi(p) \)\({}= (1, \sqrt2, 2\sqrt2, 1, 2\sqrt2, 4)\) and \(\phi(q) \)\({}= (1, 3\sqrt2, \sqrt2, 9, 3\sqrt2, 1)\). Dot product \(1 + 6 + 4 + 9 + 12 + 4 = 36\). The same.

The kernel took one dot product and one square. The map needed 6 products. For degree-10 polynomials in 100 dimensions the map would have billions of entries, and the kernel still costs one dot product.

The second kernel's map contains \(\sqrt2p_1p_2\), the XOR feature, so an SVM with \(K(p,q) = (p\T q + 1)^2\) separates XOR.

Remember
  • A kernel is \(K(p,q) = \phi(p)\T\phi(q)\). Replace every \(x_i\T x_j\) in the dual and the test rule by \(K\).
  • \((p\T q)^2\) in 2-D gives \((p_1^2, \sqrt2p_1p_2, p_2^2)\). \((p\T q+1)^2\) gives the 6-D map with 1, linear and quadratic terms.
  • XOR becomes separable once \(x_1x_2\) is a feature.

2.13Principles of learning

Lecture L13 · Short video: Generalisation and overfitting · Short video: Occam's razor · Short video: Validation · Short video: Bias and variance

The goal is good predictions on new data, not on the training data. Overfitting, cross-validation, regularisation and the bias–variance trade-off are the tools for that.

No free lunch and the ugly duckling

The no free lunch theorem says that, averaged over every possible problem, every learning algorithm does equally well. An algorithm only looks strong on problems that match its built-in assumptions (its bias). The name is from the saying "there is no such thing as a free lunch".

The ugly duckling theorem says that if every possible property counts equally, any two objects share the same number of properties, so all pairs are equally similar. (The name is from the fairy tale. Without a chosen set of features, an ugly duckling is as similar to a swan as two swans are.) Similarity only means something once we choose which features matter.

Together, no classifier is best for everything, and no representation is best for everything. We do well in practice because we design for a specific problem.

Fitting the training data is not the goal

With enough parameters we can make the training error zero. A lookup table or a Karnaugh map memorises every training input exactly, yet says nothing useful about an input it has not seen. That is not learning. Generalisation is doing well on new data from the same source.

Overfitting is fitting the training data so closely that the noise is fitted too, so test error is much larger than training error. Underfitting is a model too simple to capture the real pattern, so both errors are high.

Occam's razor (after the 14th-century philosopher William of Ockham) says to choose the simplest hypothesis that explains the data. In learning, minimise fit error plus a measure of complexity, for example the degree of a polynomial. Regularisation (Page 1) is exactly this. The ways to avoid overfitting listed in L13 are cross-validation, more training data, fewer features, early stopping, regularisation and ensembles (averaging several models).

Train, validation, test, and cross-validation

  • Training set. Used to fit the parameters.
  • Validation set. An internal test set used to choose hyperparameters (and to stop early). We retrain with different settings and compare validation error.
  • Test set. Touched once, at the end, to report the final model's error.

k-fold cross-validation splits the training data into \(k\) parts. Train on \(k-1\) parts, measure error on the one left out, and do this \(k\) times so each part is left out once. The cross-validation error is the average. With very little data, use \(k = N\), leaving one sample out each time. That is leave-one-out (LOO). Another option is to split randomly into \(x\%\) train and \((100 - x)\%\) evaluation many times and average.

Early stopping watches the validation error during iterative training and stops at its minimum, the dashed line on the slide above.

Bias and variance

Bias is error from assumptions that are too simple, such as fitting a straight line to curved data. The model cannot represent the true pattern however much data it gets. Variance is error from sensitivity to the particular training set. Train on a slightly different sample and a high-variance model changes a lot, as a very deep decision tree does. The expected prediction error is bias (squared) plus variance plus irreducible noise.

Learning is choosing the right complexity, between the two.

Parameters and hyperparameters

Parameters are learned from data during training. Examples are regression weights and bias, the logistic regression \(w\), neural network weights, a Gaussian's mean and variance, decision tree split thresholds. Hyperparameters are set before training and control how learning happens. Examples are the learning rate, \(K\) in k-means, the maximum depth of a tree, the regularisation strength \(\lambda\), the number of hidden layers. Hyperparameters are chosen on the validation set.

Remember
  • Overfit when training error is much smaller than test error.
  • The fixes are cross-validation, more data, fewer features, early stopping, regularisation, ensembles.
  • k-fold CV averages \(k\) held-out errors. LOO is \(k = N\).
  • Expected error is bias² + variance + noise. Small \(k\) in k-NN is low bias and high variance.
  • Parameters are learned. Hyperparameters are chosen on validation data.
