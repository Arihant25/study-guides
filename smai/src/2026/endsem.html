End-sem 2026, solved

The final exam of the Spring 2026 course, rebuilt from the TAs' answer key and grading rubric. It has 18 questions over three parts and is worth 180 marks. Sit it as a three-hour timed paper first, then spend about three hours on the answers. Budget roughly 6 hours in total.

E.1Read this first

The end-sem was held in late April 2026, a few days after the last lecture (L26, 23 April). It lasted three hours and was worth 180 marks, which the final grading scheme scaled to 21% of the course. It examined the whole course, from k-NN and regression to residual networks and attention.

What this page is based on. A friend shared the TAs' answer key with its grading rubric. The key restates each question in a line or two and then gives the answer, so the questions below are rebuilt from it with every number kept. For multiple-correct questions the key lists only the correct options and one reason per option, so the wording of each option here is rebuilt from those reasons. The real paper may have phrased them differently.

A muddle in the key. The key's own summary sheet at the end lists the questions in a different order from the body, and its notes column has slipped by a row or two. For example, it labels Part II Q4 as "Backprop on single neuron" and Part III Q1 as "Eigen decomposition + SVD". The body of the key is complete and consistent, so this page follows the body. Every question that the summary mentions (multi-head attention, 1-D k-means, the SLP and MLP parameter count) does appear in the body.

A second muddle. Part II Q1 is headed 20 marks but its parts add up to 3 + 10 + 8 = 21. Treat it as 20.

How multiple-correct questions were marked

The key states one strict rule for every multiple-correct question. You get full marks only if you tick all the correct options and nothing else. You get half marks if you tick at least half of the correct options (rounded up) and nothing wrong. Ticking even one wrong option gives zero. So for three correct options you need at least two of them, with no wrong one, to score anything.

True-or-false parts are marked separately and are not covered by this rule. A correct T or F gets full marks even without a reason, unless a reason is asked for.

E.2How to use this paper

  1. Print or copy out the questions only. Set a timer for 60 minutes per part and do not open any answer.
  2. In Parts II and III, spend the first two minutes choosing your three questions. Pick the ones where you can write the setup at once. In Part II, Q2 (Bayes) and Q4 (k-means) are pure arithmetic and the safest. In Part III, Q2 (kernel matrix) and Q3 (residual block) are short proofs that the course practised, so most people would drop Q1 or Q4.
  3. Mark your work with the "Marks" row under each answer. It says where the TAs gave and took away marks.
  4. For every question you lost marks on, follow the "Where it comes from" link and redo the questions in that section.

E.3Part I · Objective questions (60 marks)

Ten questions of 6 marks. Aim for six minutes each.

Q1 · IPL wicket prediction (2 + 2 + 2)

A classifier predicts whether the next ball in an IPL match will take a wicket. On a test set of 1000 balls there were 15 actual wickets. The model flagged 20 balls as "wicket-likely", and 12 of those really were wickets.

  1. Write the confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN).
  2. Find the precision and the recall.
  3. Which of these are valid criticisms of judging this model by accuracy alone? (i) Wickets are rare, so a model that never predicts a wicket already scores 98.5% accuracy. (ii) Accuracy hides how many wickets are missed, which recall shows. (iii) Accuracy hides how many flagged balls are false alarms, which precision shows. (iv) A confusion matrix is not useful when the classes are imbalanced.
End-sem 2026Part I Q1 · confusion matrix, precision and recall for the wicket predictor›
Formula
\(\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}\), \(\text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}\).
Where it comes from
(a) 1.5, box "Find precision and recall from scores and a threshold", line 2. (b) the same box, lines 3 and 4. (c) 1.5, the Common mistake card on accuracy with a rare class.
Working
  1. A true positive is a flagged ball that was a wicket. \(TP = 12\).
  2. A false positive is a flagged ball that was not a wicket. \(FP = 20 - 12 = 8\).
  3. A false negative is a wicket the model did not flag. \(FN = 15 - 12 = 3\).
  4. Everything else is a true negative. \(TN = 1000 - 12 - 8 - 3 = 977\).
  5. Precision is \(\frac{12}{12 + 8} = \frac{12}{20} = 0.6\).
  6. Recall is \(\frac{12}{12 + 3} = \frac{12}{15} = 0.8\).
  7. For (c), option (i) is true. Predicting "no wicket" every time gets \(985/1000 = 98.5\%\) right. Options (ii) and (iii) are true for the reasons they give. Option (iv) is false, because the confusion matrix is exactly the tool that shows what accuracy hides. The answer is (i), (ii), (iii).
  8. Check. \(12 + 8 + 3 + 977 = 1000\), so every ball is counted once.
Marks
(a) half a mark for each cell, full marks if any three are right because the fourth follows. Swapping FP and FN still earned 1 mark. (b) 1 mark each, with carry-forward if (a) was wrong. (c) 2 marks for all three correct options, 1 mark for exactly two of them, 0 if (iv) is ticked.

Q2 · Linear regression for strike rate (2 + 2 + 2)

A linear model \(Xw \approx y\) predicts a batter's strike rate from features in the rows of \(X\).

  1. True or false? The closed form \(w = (X\T X)^{-1}X\T y\) can always be used.
  2. Write the gradient of \(J(w) = \frac1N\|Xw - y\|^2\).
  3. Which are true? (i) Ridge regression has the closed form \(w = (X\T X + \lambda I)^{-1}X\T y\). (ii) Ridge regression sets irrelevant weights exactly to zero. (iii) Adding \(\lambda I\) makes the matrix better conditioned and always invertible for \(\lambda > 0\). (iv) Ridge gives sparser weights than lasso.
End-sem 2026Part I Q2 · when the closed form fails, the MSE gradient, ridge versus lasso›
Formula
\(\nabla J = \frac2N X\T(Xw - y)\). Ridge gives \(w = (X\T X + \lambda I)^{-1}X\T y\).
Where it comes from
(a) 1.6, box "Show that \(\mathbf w^* = (\mathbf X\T\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X\T\mathbf y\)", line 5, and the Reading the formula card after the box (when the inverse fails). (b) the same box, line 3. (c) 1.8, box "Show that ridge regression gives \(\mathbf w^* = (\mathbf X\T\mathbf X + \lambda\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf X\T\mathbf y\)", line 4, and the lasso paragraph after it.
Working
  1. (a) False. The formula needs \(X\T X\) to be invertible. It is singular when two features are copies or combinations of each other, or when there are fewer samples than features. Then there are many least-squares solutions, and you would use the pseudo-inverse or ridge.
  2. (b) Expand \(J = \frac1N(w\T X\T Xw - 2w\T X\T y + y\T y)\).
  3. Use \(\nabla(w\T Aw) = 2Aw\) for symmetric \(A\), and \(\nabla(w\T a) = a\). This gives \(\nabla J = \frac1N(2X\T Xw - 2X\T y)\).
  4. Take out \(2X\T\). So \(\nabla J \)\({}= \frac2N X\T(Xw - y)\).
  5. (c) (i) is true, it is the ridge formula. (ii) is false, because it is lasso (the L1 penalty) that sets weights exactly to zero. (iii) is true, because \(X\T X + \lambda I\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_i + \lambda > 0\). (iv) is false, lasso is the sparser one. The answer is (i), (iii).
  6. Check (b) in one dimension. With one feature, \(J = \frac1N\sum(wx_i - y_i)^2\), and \(\frac{dJ}{dw} = \frac2N\sum x_i(wx_i - y_i)\), which matches.
Marks
(a) 2 for False. Answering True but explaining the pseudo-inverse earned 1. (b) any of \(\frac2N X\T(Xw-y)\), \(X\T(Xw-y)\), \(\frac1N X\T(Xw-y)\) or \(-\frac2N X\T(y-Xw)\) got full marks. Right shape with a wrong constant got 1, only the right sign got half. (c) 2 for both (i) and (iii) with nothing wrong, 1 for one of them.

Q3 · PCA and eigenfaces (2 + 2 + 2)

There are \(N = 400\) face images of size \(100\times100\), flattened into the rows of a mean-subtracted data matrix \(X\).

  1. What is the size of the covariance matrix \(\Sigma = \frac1N X\T X\), and what is the largest its rank can be?
  2. True or false? The non-zero eigenvalues of \(X\T X\) and \(XX\T\) are the same, so you can find the eigenfaces from the smaller matrix.
  3. Which are true? (i) PCA is unsupervised and linear. (ii) Maximising the variance kept is the same as minimising the reconstruction error. (iii) PCA uses the class labels, like LDA. (iv) The covariance matrix is symmetric and positive semi-definite.
End-sem 2026Part I Q3 · covariance size and rank, the small-matrix trick, facts about PCA›
Formula
\(\operatorname{rank}(X\T X) = \operatorname{rank}(X)\). If \(XX\T u = \lambda u\) then \(X\T X(X\T u) = \lambda(X\T u)\).
Where it comes from
(a) 1.13, box "Get the eigenvectors of \(\mathbf X\mathbf X\T\) from those of \(\mathbf X\T\mathbf X\)", line 4, and the Remember box in 1.13. (b) the same box, lines 1 to 4. (c) (i) and (iii) 1.12, the opening paragraph (linear and unsupervised). (ii) 1.12, box "Show that the line through the mean that minimises the sum of squared perpendicular distances points along the top eigenvector", lines 2 and 3. (iv) B.3, box "Show that \(A = X\T X\) is symmetric and positive semi-definite".
Working
  1. Each image has \(100\cdot100 = 10000\) pixels, so \(X\) is \(400\times10000\).
  2. \(\Sigma = \frac1N X\T X\) is \((10000\times400)(400\times10000)\), which is \(10000\times10000\).
  3. Its rank equals the rank of \(X\), which is at most \(\min(400, 10000) = 400\).
  4. Subtracting the mean makes the 400 rows add up to the zero vector. So one row is minus the sum of the others, and the rank is at most \(400 - 1 = 399\).
  5. (b) True. Multiply \(XX\T u = \lambda u\) on the left by \(X\T\). That gives \(X\T X(X\T u) \)\({}= \lambda(X\T u)\), so \(X\T u\) is an eigenvector of the big matrix with the same \(\lambda\). The \(400\times400\) problem is far cheaper.
  6. (c) (i) true. (ii) true, this is the reconstruction view of PCA. (iii) false, PCA never sees labels. (iv) true, \(z\T\Sigma z = \frac1N\|Xz\|^2 \ge 0\). The answer is (i), (ii), (iv).
Marks
(a) 1 for the size, 1 for the rank. Both 399 and 400 were accepted. (b) full marks for True, even with no reason. (c) 2 for all of (i), (ii), (iv), 1 for exactly two, 0 if (iii) is ticked.

Q4 · Bayesian decision in a classroom (4 + 2)

Two classes have priors \(P(\omega_1) = 0.7\) and \(P(\omega_2) = 0.3\). For an observed \(x\) the likelihoods are \(p(x\mid\omega_1) = 0.4\) and \(p(x\mid\omega_2) = 0.6\).

  1. Find \(P(\omega_1\mid x)\).
  2. True or false? Choosing the class with the larger posterior gives the smallest possible probability of error.
End-sem 2026Part I Q4 · one posterior with Bayes' rule›
Formula
\(P(\omega_1\mid x) = \frac{p(x\mid\omega_1)P(\omega_1)}{p(x\mid\omega_1)P(\omega_1) + p(x\mid\omega_2)P(\omega_2)}\)
Where it comes from
(a) 2.5, Reading the formula for Bayes' theorem. (b) 2.5, box "Show that the Bayes decision rule minimises the probability of error", lines 3 and 4
Working
  1. The top is \(0.4\times0.7 = 0.28\).
  2. The second term of the bottom is \(0.6\times0.3 = 0.18\).
  3. The bottom is \(0.28 + 0.18 = 0.46\).
  4. So \(P(\omega_1\mid x) = \frac{0.28}{0.46} = \frac{14}{23} \approx 0.609\).
  5. (b) True. This is why the Bayes classifier is called optimal.
  6. Check. \(P(\omega_2\mid x) = 0.18/0.46 \approx 0.391\), and the two add up to 1. Class 1 still wins even though its likelihood is smaller, because its prior is much larger.
Marks
(a) 2 for the formula, 1 for the set-up, 1 for the number. Anything from 0.60 to 0.61 was accepted. Right formula with wrong arithmetic got 3. (b) 2 for True.

Q5 · Naive Bayes and logistic regression (2 + 2 + 2)

  1. True or false? Naive Bayes assumes the features are conditionally independent given the class.
  2. Write \(p(y = 1\mid x)\) for logistic regression and name the loss usually used to train it.
  3. Which are true? (i) Training logistic regression with the squared error \((\sigma(w\T x) - y)^2\) gives a non-convex loss. (ii) With the squared error, a saturated sigmoid makes the gradient vanish. (iii) Cross-entropy is the maximum likelihood loss for a Bernoulli label. (iv) Logistic regression always violates Bayes optimality.
End-sem 2026Part I Q5 · the naive assumption, the logistic model and its loss›
Formula
\(p(y = 1\mid x) = \sigma(w\T x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-w\T x}}\). \(L = -\frac1N\sum_i[y_i\ln\sigma(w\T x_i) + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - \sigma(w\T x_i))]\).
Where it comes from
(a) 2.9, the conditional independence equation. (b) 2.10, box "With labels \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\), derive the loss of logistic regression …", lines 1 to 3. (c) (i) and (ii) 2.10, "Why not use squared error". (iii) the same box, lines 2 and 3
Working
  1. (a) True. That one assumption is what makes the method "naive".
  2. (b) The model is the sigmoid of a linear score, as in the formula row. The loss is the binary cross-entropy, also called the log-loss or the negative log-likelihood.
  3. (c) (i) true, the L11 slides ask you to check this. (ii) true, the squared-error gradient carries a factor \(\sigma'(z)\), which is near 0 when \(|z|\) is large. (iii) true, page 2 derives it. (iv) false, "always" is too strong. If the true posterior really is a sigmoid of a linear score, logistic regression is Bayes optimal. The answer is (i), (ii), (iii).
Marks
(a) 2 for True. (b) 1 for the sigmoid, 1 for any correct name of the loss. (c) 2 for all three correct options, 1 for exactly two, 0 if (iv) is ticked.

Q6 · SVMs and kernels (3 + 3)

  1. Which are true? (i) In the dual solution only the support vectors have \(\alpha_i > 0\). (ii) The margin is \(\frac{2}{\|w\|}\). (iii) The dual uses the data only through dot products \(x_i\T x_j\), which is what allows kernels. (iv) Replacing the constraints by a hinge loss alone, with no \(C\) and no slack variables, gives exactly the soft-margin SVM.
  2. True or false? A larger \(C\) gives a narrower margin.
End-sem 2026Part I Q6 · support vectors, margin, kernels, the effect of C›
Formula
The soft-margin SVM solves \(\min\frac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i\xi_i\) such that \(y_i(w\T x_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i\), \(\xi_i \ge 0\).
Where it comes from
(a) (i) 2.11, Reading the formula for the dual. (ii) 2.11, box "Show that the margin … is \(\frac{2}{\|w\|}\) …", line 2. (iii) 2.12, the boxed kernel formulas. (iv) 2.11, "Hinge loss compared with logistic loss". (b) 2.11, the soft-margin paragraph on \(C\)
Working
  1. (i) True. A point strictly outside the margin has \(\alpha_i = 0\), so it does not affect \(w = \sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\).
  2. (ii) True. The two planes \(w\T x + b = \pm1\) are \(\frac{2}{\|w\|}\) apart.
  3. (iii) True. Replace each \(x_i\T x_j\) by \(K(x_i, x_j)\) and nothing else changes.
  4. (iv) The key marks this false. The soft-margin primal is \(\frac12\|w\|^2 \)\({}+ C\sum_i\max(0, 1 - y_i(w\T x_i + b))\), so the hinge loss needs the \(C\) and the \(\|w\|^2\) term with it. The key admits that some books treat the two as the same.
  5. (b) True. \(C\) is the price of each violation. A larger price forces fewer violations, which means a narrower margin, closer to the hard-margin SVM.
Marks
(a) 3 for (i), (ii), (iii), 1.5 for exactly two of them. Because (iv) is genuinely ambiguous, ticking all four also got 3. This was the only exception to the strict rule in the paper. (b) 3 for True, at most 1 off for a wrong explanation.

Q7 · From SLP to MLP (3 + 3)

  1. Which are true? (i) The perceptron rule finds a separating line in finitely many steps if the data are linearly separable. (ii) A single layer perceptron can learn XOR. (iii) The delta rule converges, with a small enough learning rate, even when the data are noisy and not separable. (iv) A single layer perceptron is a universal approximator.
  2. An MLP has 3 inputs, one hidden layer of 2 sigmoid neurons and 1 linear output. Every neuron has a bias. How many learnable parameters does it have?
End-sem 2026Part I Q7 · perceptron facts and a parameter count›
Formula
A dense layer has (inputs + 1) × outputs parameters.
Where it comes from
(a) 3.2, "Does it always stop?", claims 1 and 2 for (i) and (ii), 3.3, the Remember box and the comparison table for (iii), and 3.4, "The universal approximation theorem" for (iv). (b) 3.4, box "Three inputs, one hidden layer of two sigmoid neurons", lines 1 to 3
Working
  1. (i) true, this is the perceptron convergence theorem. (ii) false, XOR is not linearly separable. (iii) true, the delta rule is gradient descent on a convex squared error, so it settles at the least-squares weights. (iv) false, it is an MLP with a hidden layer that is a universal approximator. The answer is (i), (iii).
  2. (b) Input to hidden. Each of the 2 hidden neurons has 3 weights and 1 bias, so \(2\times(3 + 1) = 8\).
  3. Hidden to output. The output neuron has 2 weights and 1 bias, so \(1\times(2 + 1) = 3\).
  4. The total is \(8 + 3 = 11\).
Marks
(a) 3 for both (i) and (iii), 1.5 for one of them, 0 if (ii) or (iv) is ticked. (b) 3 for 11. Answers of 8 or 10 (a bias forgotten) got 2, and 6 (all biases forgotten) got 1.

Q8 · Optimisers, BatchNorm and dropout (2 + 2 + 2)

  1. True or false? Adam combines momentum with a separate adaptive learning rate for each parameter.
  2. Which help against vanishing gradients? (i) ReLU activations. (ii) Skip connections. (iii) Batch normalisation. (iv) Initialising all weights to zero.
  3. What did the original BatchNorm paper say it was for? (i) Reducing internal covariate shift. (ii) Reducing the number of parameters. (iii) Replacing dropout. (iv) Making the loss convex.
End-sem 2026Part I Q8 · Adam, fixes for vanishing gradients, why BatchNorm›
Formula
Adam keeps \(m_t = \beta_1m_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)g_t\) and \(v_t = \beta_2v_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)g_t^2\), corrects them by \(1 - \beta^t\), and steps by \(\eta\,\hat m_t/(\sqrt{\hat v_t} + \epsilon)\).
Where it comes from
(a) 3.8, Reading the formula for Adam. (b) 3.5, the card "Why ReLU helps with vanishing gradients", 4.6, box "Show why a skip connection helps the gradient reach early layers", lines 1 to 4, 3.6, "Vanishing and exploding gradients, and zero initialisation", and its Remember box for (iv). (c) 3.7, Reading the formula for batch normalisation
Working
  1. (a) True. \(m_t\) is the momentum part and \(v_t\) scales the step for each weight separately.
  2. (b) (i) helps, because ReLU's slope is 1 wherever it is active. (ii) helps, because the skip adds a path with derivative 1. (iii) helps, because it keeps pre-activations in the range where sigmoid and tanh are not flat. (iv) does not, zero weights make every hidden unit get the same gradient so they never become different. The answer is (i), (ii), (iii).
  3. (c) Only (i). Ioffe and Szegedy (2015) introduced BatchNorm to reduce internal covariate shift, meaning the change in each layer's input distribution while earlier layers train.
Marks
(a) 2 for True. (b) 2 for all three, 1 for exactly two, 0 if (iv) is ticked. (c) single correct option, so 2 or 0.

Q9 · 2-D convolution arithmetic (2 + 2 + 2)

The input is \(224\times224\times1\). A convolution layer has 32 filters of size \(7\times7\), stride 2 and padding 3.

  1. What is the output size?
  2. How many learnable parameters does the layer have?
  3. A \(2\times2\) max-pool with stride 2 follows. What is its output size?
End-sem 2026Part I Q9 · output size, parameters and pooling for a 7×7 conv layer›
Formula
Output side \(\lfloor(n + 2p - k)/s\rfloor + 1\). Parameters \((k\cdot k\cdot D + 1)\cdot K\).
Where it comes from
(a) 4.3, box "Show that a filter of width k with stride s ... gives floor((n+2p-k)/s) + 1 outputs", line 4 (line 5 for the same-padding check). (b) 4.4, box "Count the parameters of a conv layer", line 4. (c) 4.3, the same box, line 4 with k = s = 2
Working
  1. Put in \(n = 224\), \(p = 3\), \(k = 7\), \(s = 2\). The top is \(224 + 6 - 7 = 223\).
  2. \(223/2 = 111.5\), and the floor is 111.
  3. Add 1 to get 112. Each filter gives one channel, so the output is \(112\times112\times32\).
  4. (b) Each filter has \(7\cdot7\cdot1 = 49\) weights and 1 bias, which is 50.
  5. For 32 filters that is \(32\times50 = 1600\).
  6. (c) Pooling halves each side, \(112/2 = 56\), and keeps the channels. The output is \(56\times56\times32\).
  7. Check (a). Padding 3 with a \(7\times7\) filter is "same" padding, \((7-1)/2 = 3\). With stride 1 the output would be 224, and stride 2 halves it to 112.
Marks
(a) 1.5 for \(112\times112\), 0.5 for 32 channels. Forgetting the padding gives 109, which earned half a mark for method. (b) 2 for 1600, 1.5 for 1568 (biases forgotten). (c) 1 for \(56\times56\), 1 for keeping 32 channels.

Q10 · Multi-head self-attention (2 + 2 + 2)

A multi-head self-attention layer has 8 heads. The input and output dimension is \(d = 100\), there are \(N = 1000\) tokens, and each head's queries, keys and values have dimension 50.

  1. How many weight matrices does the layer have?
  2. What is the size of each?
  3. How many learnable parameters are there, ignoring biases?
End-sem 2026Part I Q10 · counting the matrices and parameters of multi-head attention›
Formula
Each head \(i\) has \(W_Q^{(i)}, W_K^{(i)}, W_V^{(i)}\) of size \(d\times d_k\). The output projection \(W_O\) is \((h\cdot d_k)\times d\).
Where it comes from
(a) 4.11, box "Count the weights of multi-head attention", lines 2 and 3. (b) the same box, lines 1 and 3. (c) the same box, line 4
Working
  1. (a) Each head has 3 matrices, so the heads have \(8\times3 = 24\). The output projection adds 1. The total is 25.
  2. (b) Each query, key and value matrix maps 100 numbers to 50, so it is \(100\times50\).
  3. Concatenating the 8 heads gives \(8\times50 = 400\) numbers, and \(W_O\) maps them back to 100. So \(W_O\) is \(400\times100\).
  4. (c) The 24 head matrices hold \(24\times100\times50 = 120\,000\) weights.
  5. \(W_O\) holds \(400\times100 = 40\,000\).
  6. The total is \(120\,000 + 40\,000 = 160\,000\).
  7. Note that \(N = 1000\) never appears. The number of tokens changes the cost of running the layer but not the number of weights, because the same matrices are applied to every token.
Marks
(a) 2 for 25, 1 for 24 (\(W_O\) forgotten). Writing 4 because you stacked each of Q, K and V across heads into one matrix was also accepted. (b) 1 for the head matrices, 1 for \(W_O\), transposed shapes accepted. (c) 2 for 160 000, 1 for 120 000.

E.4Part II · Three out of four (60 marks)

Numerical questions of 20 marks. Choose three. Aim for 20 minutes each.

Q1 · Gradient descent on a single neuron (3 + 10 + 8)

One neuron computes \(o = \sigma(w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b)\) with \(\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\), and the loss is \(L = \frac12(o - y)^2\). Start from \(w_1 = 1\), \(w_2 = -1\), \(b = 0\). The sample is \(x = [1, 1]\) with \(y = 1\), and the learning rate is \(\eta = 1\).

  1. Show that \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\).
  2. Do the forward pass and find \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}\), \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}\) and \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\).
  3. Do one gradient descent step.
Write this in the exam

(a) Show that \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\).

  1. \(\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}\), so \(\sigma'(z) \)\({}= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Write \(\sigma\) as a power, \(\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}\).
    2. The chain rule gives \(-1\cdot(1 + e^{-z})^{-2}\) times the derivative of the inside.
    3. The derivative of \(1 + e^{-z}\) is \(-e^{-z}\). The two minus signs cancel, leaving \(\frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}\).
  2. \(\sigma'(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}\).
  3. \(\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} = 1 - \sigma(z)\), so \(\sigma'(z) \)\({}= \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\).
    Why (covers lines 2 and 3, 3 steps)
    1. Split the square in the bottom into two copies of \(1 + e^{-z}\) and give one to each factor.
    2. The first factor is \(\sigma(z)\) by definition.

  1. (a) \(T(x\mid\mu) = 1 - |x - \mu|\) when \(|x - \mu| \le 1\), and 0 otherwise. The support is \([\mu - 1, \mu + 1]\). (Under the text's reading it is \(\max(0, 2 - 4|x - \mu|)\) on \([\mu - 0.5, \mu + 0.5]\).)
  2. (b) GMM. Start with guesses for \(\pi_k\), \(\mu_k\) and \(\sigma_k^2\).
  3. E-step. \(\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\sigma_j^2)}\), the responsibility of component \(k\) for point \(i\).
  4. M-step. \(N_k = \sum_i\gamma_{ik}\), \(\pi_k = N_k/N\), \(\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i\), \(\sigma_k^2 = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)^2\).
  5. Repeat until the log-likelihood stops changing by more than a small tolerance.
  6. (c) TMM. Start with guesses for \(\pi_k\) and \(\mu_k\). There is no width to learn, because the triangle's shape is fixed.
  7. E-step. Use the triangle in place of the Gaussian, \(\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_kT(x_i\mid\mu_k)}{\sum_j\pi_jT(x_i\mid\mu_j)}\).
  8. If a point lies outside every triangle, the bottom is 0. Handle it by adding a small constant to \(T\) or by moving a mean towards that point.
  9. M-step. \(N_k = \sum_i\gamma_{ik}\), \(\pi_k = N_k/N\), \(\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i\). The weighted mean is the natural copy of the GMM step. The key notes that the exact maximum-likelihood update for a triangle is not a simple formula, and the weighted mean works well in practice.
  10. Repeat until the means and the log-likelihood stop changing.
  11. (d) For the TMM, it is cheaper (no exponentials or square roots), a component ignores points outside its base so there are no long tails, and each component has fewer parameters.
  12. Against the TMM, the density has a kink at the peak so the likelihood is not smooth, a point outside every base breaks the E-step, and a fixed width cannot fit clusters of different spreads.
  13. Check (a). The area is \(\int_{\mu-1}^{\mu+1}(1 - |x - \mu|)\,dx\). Each half is a triangle of base 1 and height 1, area \(\frac12\), so the total is 1.
Marks
(a) 2 for using \(|x - \mu|\), 2 for the straight-line shape with area 1, 2 for the support. Forgetting "zero outside" got 4. (b) 1 for the E-step, 2 for the M-step, 1 for the stopping rule. (c) 2 for the E-step with the triangle, 2 for the M-step, 1 for noting the fixed width, 1 for convergence. Copying GMM with the triangle put in got full marks. (d) 2 for two advantages and 2 for two disadvantages. Any sensible points were accepted.

E.6What the marking rewarded

  • Multiple-correct questions were strict. One wrong tick gave zero. Leaving out an option you are unsure of costs at most half the marks, and a wrong tick costs all of them.
  • True or false needs no reason, unless one is asked for. Do not add a qualification you are not sure of, because a wrong one could cost half a mark.
  • Derivations and numbers were marked generously. The key says to give credit for a correct set-up and chain rule even when the arithmetic slips, and to carry a wrong number forward without punishing it twice. So always write the formula before you put numbers in.
  • Either sign convention was fine (for example \(o - y\) or \(y - o\)), as long as you used it consistently.
  • Both \(\frac1N\) and \(\frac1{2N}\) versions of the MSE were accepted, and so were transposed matrix shapes.
  • Ambiguous questions were read kindly. The triangle question accepted either reading of the ratio, and the paper told students to make reasonable assumptions. State your assumption in one line and carry on.
  • Units and shapes earn marks. Several questions gave a mark just for saying the answer is a vector in \(\R^p\), or for keeping the 32 channels after pooling.
