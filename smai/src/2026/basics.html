B.1Vectors

Used from L01 onward · Short video: Data as matrix · Short video: Distances and similarities · Read: Mathematics for Machine Learning, chapter 2

A vector is a list of numbers. In this course every data point (an email, an image, a day's weather) is turned into a vector before any algorithm sees it.

A vector with \(d\) entries is written as a column:

\[\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_d \end{bmatrix}.\]

Bold lower-case letters (\(\mathbf x, \mathbf w\)) are vectors. Plain letters with a subscript (\(x_1, x_2\)) are the numbers inside. The number of entries \(d\) is called the dimension. The slides often drop the bold and write \(x\) for a vector too, so read the context.

The statement \(\mathbf x \in \R^d\) is read "x is in R d" and means "x is a list of \(d\) real numbers". The symbol \(\in\) means "is a member of", and \(\R\) is the set of real numbers.

The transpose, written with a superscript \(\mathsf T\), turns a column into a row, so \(\mathbf x\T \)\({}= [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d]\). The slides write vectors inline as \([1, 2]^{\mathsf T}\) to save space, which means the column with entries 1 and 2.

Adding and scaling

Vectors of the same dimension add entry by entry, so \([1,2]\T + [3,5]\T = [4,7]\T\). Multiplying by a number (a scalar) multiplies every entry, so \(3\,[1,2]\T = [3,6]\T\).

The dot product

\[\mathbf w\T\mathbf x = \sum_{i=1}^{d} w_i x_i.\]
Reading the formula

The symbol \(\sum\) (capital sigma) means "add up". The \(i=1\) underneath and the \(d\) on top say that \(i\) runs through \(1, 2, \dots, d\). For each \(i\) we write the term \(w_ix_i\), and then we add all the terms.

Written out, it is \(w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_dx_d\). So the dot product multiplies matching entries and adds the results. The answer is a single number, not a vector.

Example. \(\mathbf w = [1,1,1,1]\T\) and \(\mathbf x = [0,1,2,3]\T\). The products are \(1\cdot0, 1\cdot1, 1\cdot2, 1\cdot3\), that is 0, 1, 2, 3. Their sum is \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf x = 6\).

The linear classifier of L01 decides the class from the sign of \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf x\), so you will compute this number all the time.

Length and angle

The length of a vector comes from Pythagoras' theorem, which gives \(\|\mathbf x\| \)\({}= \sqrt{x_1^2 + \dots + x_d^2} \)\({}= \sqrt{\mathbf x\T\mathbf x}\). The double bars \(\|\cdot\|\) mean "length" (more on this in B.2).

The dot product also measures the angle \(\theta\) (theta) between two vectors:

\[\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf a\T\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf a\|\,\|\mathbf b\|}.\]
Reading the formula

The top is the dot product. The bottom is the product of the two lengths. Dividing by the lengths removes the effect of how long the vectors are, so only the direction matters. The result always lies between \(-1\) and \(1\).

  • \(\cos\theta = 1\) means the vectors point the same way (\(\theta = 0°\)).
  • \(\cos\theta = 0\) means they are at right angles (\(\theta = 90°\)). Such vectors are called orthogonal.
  • \(\cos\theta = -1\) means they point in opposite directions.

Example. \(\mathbf a = [3,4]\T\), \(\mathbf b = [4,3]\T\). The dot product is \(12 + 12 = 24\). Both lengths are \(\sqrt{9+16} = 5\). So \(\cos\theta \)\({}= \frac{24}{25} \)\({}= 0.96\), and \(\theta \approx 16°\).

This quantity is called cosine similarity. Latent semantic indexing (L08) uses it to compare documents.

Projection onto a direction

A unit vector has length 1. If \(\mathbf u\) is a unit vector, the number \(\mathbf u\T\mathbf x\) is how far \(\mathbf x\) reaches along the direction of \(\mathbf u\). The vector \((\mathbf u\T\mathbf x)\,\mathbf u\) is the projection of \(\mathbf x\) onto that direction, the closest point to \(\mathbf x\) on the line through the origin along \(\mathbf u\).

Example

\(\mathbf u \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt2}[1,1]\T\) (length \(\sqrt{\frac12+\frac12} = 1\)) and \(\mathbf x = [3,1]\T\).

  1. \(\mathbf u\T\mathbf x \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt2}(3 + 1) \)\({}= \frac4{\sqrt2} \)\({}= 2\sqrt2\).
  2. The projection is \(2\sqrt2\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}[1,1]\T \)\({}= [2,2]\T\).
  3. The leftover \(\mathbf x - [2,2]\T = [1,-1]\T\) is at right angles to \(\mathbf u\), because \([1,1]\cdot[1,-1] = 1 - 1 = 0\). This always happens. The leftover of a projection is orthogonal to the direction.

PCA (page 1) keeps the projections onto a few chosen directions and throws away the leftovers.

The augmented vector, and why the bias disappears

A line in the plane that passes through the origin can be written \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf x = 0\). A general line needs an extra number \(b\), called the bias, giving \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf x + b = 0\). L01 shows a trick to hide \(b\). Add a 1 to the end of \(\mathbf x\) and put \(b\) at the end of \(\mathbf w\).

\[\begin{bmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \\ b\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ 1\end{bmatrix} = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b.\]

The longer \(\mathbf x\) is called the augmented vector. From now on, when the slides write \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf x\), assume \(\mathbf x\) has been augmented and the bias is inside \(\mathbf w\) unless the slide says otherwise.

Remember
  • \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf x \)\({}= \sum_i w_ix_i\), a single number.
  • \(\|\mathbf x\| \)\({}= \sqrt{\mathbf x\T\mathbf x}\), and \(\cos\theta \)\({}= \frac{\mathbf a\T\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|}\).
  • Orthogonal means dot product 0.
  • Projection of \(\mathbf x\) onto unit \(\mathbf u\) is \((\mathbf u\T\mathbf x)\mathbf u\).
  • Appending a 1 to \(\mathbf x\) absorbs the bias into \(\mathbf w\).

B.2Norms

Lecture L04 · Quiz 1 question 1 in all four sets

A norm is a way to measure the size of a vector. Different norms give different sizes, and the course uses four of them.

The general Lp norm (read "L p norm") of a vector \(\mathbf x \in \R^d\) is

\[\|\mathbf x\|_p = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^d |x_i|^p\Big)^{1/p}.\]
Reading the formula

\(|x_i|\) is the absolute value of the entry (drop its minus sign). Raise each one to the power \(p\), add them up, then take the \(p\)-th root. The \(p\)-th root undoes the power so the answer has the same units as the entries.

  • \(p = 2\) gives the L2 norm, the ordinary length, \(\|\mathbf x\|_2 \)\({}= \sqrt{x_1^2 + \dots + x_d^2}\).
  • \(p = 1\) gives the L1 norm, the sum of absolute values, \(\|\mathbf x\|_1 \)\({}= |x_1| + \dots + |x_d|\). It is also called the Manhattan or city-block distance when applied to \(\mathbf x - \mathbf y\), because it is how far you walk along a street grid.
  • The L∞ norm (L infinity, the max norm) is the largest absolute entry. As \(p\) grows, the biggest entry dominates the sum, which is why the limit is the maximum.
  • The L0 "norm" counts the non-zero entries. It is not a true norm (scaling \(\mathbf x\) by 2 does not double it), but the slides call it a norm.

Example. \(\mathbf x = [3, -4, 0, 12]\T\).

  • L0: three entries are non-zero, so 3.
  • L1: \(3 + 4 + 0 + 12 = 19\).
  • L2: \(\sqrt{9 + 16 + 0 + 144} \)\({}= \sqrt{169} \)\({}= 13\).
  • L∞: the largest absolute entry is 12.

Ridge regression (page 1) adds \(\|\mathbf w\|_2^2\) to the loss, and lasso adds \(\|\mathbf w\|_1\). L04 explains that the L1 penalty pushes some weights to exactly zero, which is what the L0 count really wants but cannot be optimised directly.

A norm of a difference, \(\|\mathbf x - \mathbf y\|\), is a distance. L02 lists the four properties a distance function (a metric) must have. The distance from a point to itself is 0 (identity), distances are never negative, the distance from \(\mathbf x\) to \(\mathbf y\) equals that from \(\mathbf y\) to \(\mathbf x\) (symmetry), and going via a third point is never shorter (the triangle inequality).

Remember
  • L0 counts non-zeros, L1 adds absolute values, L2 is the usual length, L∞ is the largest absolute entry.
  • For any vector, \(\|\mathbf x\|_\infty \le \|\mathbf x\|_2 \le \|\mathbf x\|_1\). Use this to check your answers.

B.3Matrices

Lectures L03 to L05 · Short video: Data as matrix · Reference: Minka, Old and new matrix algebra useful for statistics

A matrix is a table of numbers. In SMAI the most important one is the data matrix, with one data point per row.

An \(m \times n\) matrix has \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns. The entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is written \(A_{ij}\). If you have \(N\) data points, each a vector in \(\R^d\), stacking them as rows gives the \(N \times d\) data matrix \(X\). Some slides stack them as columns instead (a \(d\times N\) matrix), so always check which convention a question uses.

Multiplying matrices

The product \(AB\) exists when the number of columns of \(A\) equals the number of rows of \(B\). If \(A\) is \(m\times n\) and \(B\) is \(n\times p\), then \(AB\) is \(m\times p\), and

\[(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n A_{ik}B_{kj}.\]
Reading the formula

Entry \((i,j)\) of the product is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\). The index \(k\) walks along that row and down that column at the same time.

Example.

\[\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5\\6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\cdot5+2\cdot6\\3\cdot5+4\cdot6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}17\\39\end{bmatrix}.\]

A \(2\times2\) matrix times a \(2\times1\) vector gives a \(2\times1\) vector. Order matters, because \(AB\) is usually not equal to \(BA\).

A matrix times a vector, \(\mathbf x' = W\mathbf x\), is the linear feature transformation of L02. If \(W\) is \(k\times d\) with \(k \lt d\), the new vector is shorter, which is dimensionality reduction.

Special matrices

  • The transpose \(A\T\) swaps rows and columns, so \((A\T)_{ij} = A_{ji}\). A useful rule is \((AB)\T = B\T A\T\) (the order reverses).
  • The identity matrix \(I\) has 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. \(I\mathbf x = \mathbf x\) for every \(\mathbf x\), so it is the matrix version of the number 1.
  • A diagonal matrix has non-zero entries only on the diagonal. The slides write \(\operatorname{Diag}(\alpha_i)\) for the diagonal matrix with \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots\) on the diagonal.
  • A symmetric matrix equals its transpose, \(A = A\T\). Covariance matrices are symmetric.
  • An upper triangular matrix has only zeros below the diagonal.
  • The inverse \(A^{-1}\) of a square matrix satisfies \(AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I\). It exists only when the determinant (below) is not zero. Such a matrix is called invertible or non-singular. One without an inverse is singular.
  • An orthogonal matrix \(Q\) has orthonormal columns (unit length, pairwise orthogonal), so \(Q\T Q = I\) and \(Q^{-1} = Q\T\).

Determinant and trace of a 2×2 matrix

\[A = \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\]
\[\det A = ad - bc,\qquad \operatorname{tr}A = a + d\]
\[A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}\]
Reading the formula

The determinant \(\det A\) is one number that says how much the matrix stretches areas. If it is 0, the matrix squashes the plane onto a line, information is lost, and no inverse can undo it. That is why \(\det A\) sits in the bottom of the inverse formula.

The trace \(\operatorname{tr}A\) is the sum of the diagonal entries.

Example. \(A \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\). \(\det A = 4 - 1 = 3\), \(\operatorname{tr} A = 4\), and \(A^{-1} \)\({}= \frac13\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\). To check one entry of \(AA^{-1}\), note that row 1 times column 1 is \(\frac13(2\cdot2 + 1\cdot(-1)) \)\({}= \frac33 \)\({}= 1\). Good.

The closed-form regression of page 1 needs \((X\T X)^{-1}\), and for two parameters that is exactly this \(2\times2\) inverse.

Positive semi-definite matrices

A symmetric matrix \(A\) is positive semi-definite (PSD) if \(\mathbf z\T A\mathbf z \ge 0\) for every vector \(\mathbf z\). The expression \(\mathbf z\T A\mathbf z\) is a single number (a row times a matrix times a column), called a quadratic form. Covariance matrices and every matrix of the form \(X\T X\) are PSD. The derivation below is a question the slides asked twice (L05 and L06).

Write this in the exam

Show that \(A = X\T X\) is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

  1. \(A\T \)\({}= (X\T X)\T \)\({}= X\T (X\T)\T \)\({}= X\T X \)\({}= A\), so \(A\) is symmetric.
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Use the rule \((PQ)\T = Q\T P\T\) with \(P = X\T\) and \(Q = X\). This gives \((X\T X)\T = X\T (X\T)\T\).
    2. Transposing twice gives back the original matrix, so \((X\T)\T = X\).
    3. So \(A\T = X\T X = A\). A matrix equal to its transpose is symmetric by definition.
  2. For any \(\mathbf z\), \(\mathbf z\T A\mathbf z \)\({}= \mathbf z\T X\T X\mathbf z \)\({}= (X\mathbf z)\T(X\mathbf z) \)\({}= \|X\mathbf z\|^2 \ge 0\), so \(A\) is PSD.
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Substitute \(A = X\T X\). This gives \(\mathbf z\T X\T X\mathbf z\).
    2. By the same transpose rule, \(\mathbf z\T X\T \)\({}= (X\mathbf z)\T\). So the expression is \((X\mathbf z)\T(X\mathbf z)\).
    3. \(X\mathbf z\) is a vector, and a vector dotted with itself is its squared length (B.1). So the expression equals \(\|X\mathbf z\|^2\).
    4. A squared length is never negative. This holds for every \(\mathbf z\), which is the definition of PSD.
Remember
  • \((AB)_{ij}\) = row \(i\) of \(A\) dotted with column \(j\) of \(B\). The sizes follow \((m\times n)(n\times p) \)\({}= m\times p\).
  • \((AB)\T = B\T A\T\) and \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\).
  • \(2\times2\): \(\det = ad - bc\), inverse swaps \(a,d\), negates \(b,c\), divides by \(\det\).
  • \(X\T X\) is always symmetric and PSD.

B.4Rank

Lecture L05 (the whole lecture is about the rank of the data matrix)

The rank of a matrix is the number of genuinely different directions among its rows. Real data matrices usually have far lower rank than their size suggests, and the course builds on that fact.

A set of vectors is linearly dependent if one of them can be written as a combination of the others (numbers times vectors, added). Otherwise they are linearly independent. The rank of a matrix is the largest number of linearly independent rows. It always equals the largest number of linearly independent columns too, so an \(N\times d\) matrix has rank at most \(\min(N, d)\). A square matrix with rank equal to its size is full rank, and that is the same as having a non-zero determinant.

Example

Why is the rank of \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\\7&8&9\end{bmatrix}\) equal to 2?

  1. Write \(\mathbf r = [1,2,3]\) and \(\mathbf 1 = [1,1,1]\).
  2. Row 1 is \(\mathbf r\). Row 2 is \(\mathbf r + 3\cdot\mathbf 1\). Row 3 is \(\mathbf r + 6\cdot\mathbf 1\).
  3. So every row is a combination of the two vectors \(\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf 1\). The rank is at most 2.
  4. Rows 1 and 2 are not multiples of each other (\(4/1 \ne 5/2\)), so the rank is at least 2.
  5. So the rank is 2. Its determinant is 0, and one of its eigenvalues is 0 (B.5).

L05 uses this idea on data. Suppose 300 documents are each turned into a 300-word histogram. The data matrix is \(300\times300\), but if the documents really come from only 3 topics, the rows are close to combinations of 3 "topic" rows, and the matrix behaves as if it had rank close to 3. That is the low-rank idea behind PCA, eigenfaces, LSI and recommendation systems.

With measured data, noise makes almost every matrix full rank in the strict sense. What matters is the effective rank, the number of eigenvalues (or singular values) that are large. The rest are tiny and come from noise.

Remember
  • Rank = number of independent rows = number of independent columns \(\le \min(N,d)\).
  • For a square matrix, three statements go together. It is full rank, its determinant is non-zero, and it is invertible. If one holds, all three hold.
  • Duplicate or near-duplicate data points do not raise the rank.

  4. Rank 2. The same argument shows that any \(n\times n\) matrix of consecutive numbers (with \(n \ge 2\)) has rank 2.

B.5Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Lectures L04 to L06 · Short video: Eigen decomposition · Short video: Optimization and eigen vectors

An eigenvector of a matrix is a direction that the matrix only stretches, without turning. The stretch factor is the eigenvalue. PCA, LDA and eigenfaces are all "find the eigenvectors" problems.

\[A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v,\qquad \mathbf v \ne \mathbf 0.\]
Reading the formula

\(A\) is a square \(n\times n\) matrix. \(\mathbf v\) is a non-zero vector, and \(\lambda\) (lambda) is a number. The equation says that multiplying \(\mathbf v\) by \(A\) gives back \(\mathbf v\) scaled by \(\lambda\). The direction does not change.

The name comes from the German "eigen", meaning "own". These are the matrix's own special directions.

Example. \(A \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf v = [1,1]\T\). Then \(A\mathbf v \)\({}= [2+1, 1+2]\T \)\({}= [3,3]\T \)\({}= 3\mathbf v\). So \(\mathbf v\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3. Try \([1,-1]\T\) instead. \(A[1,-1]\T = [1,-1]\T\), so that is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1. Most vectors, such as \([1,0]\T \mapsto [2,1]\T\), change direction and are not eigenvectors.

Any multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector, so we usually scale it to length 1. The slides also sort the eigenvalues from largest to smallest.

Finding them by hand for a 2×2 matrix

Write this in the exam

Find the eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors of \(A \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\).

  1. Eigenvalues solve \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\).
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Start from \(A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\). Write \(\lambda\mathbf v\) as \(\lambda I\mathbf v\), which is allowed because \(I\mathbf v = \mathbf v\).
    2. Move everything to one side. \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf v \)\({}= \mathbf 0\).
    3. If \(A - \lambda I\) had an inverse, multiplying both sides by it would give \(\mathbf v = \mathbf 0\). But an eigenvector must be non-zero.
    4. So \(A - \lambda I\) has no inverse, which means its determinant is 0 (B.3).
  2. \(\det\begin{bmatrix}2-\lambda&1\\1&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix} \)\({}= (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 \)\({}= 0\), so \(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Subtracting \(\lambda I\) takes \(\lambda\) off each diagonal entry.
    2. Apply \(\det = ad - bc\). \((2-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - 1\cdot1\).
    3. Expand. \(4 - 4\lambda + \lambda^2 - 1 \)\({}= \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3\).
  3. \((\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1) = 0\), so \(\lambda_1 = 3\) and \(\lambda_2 = 1\).
  4. For \(\lambda_1 = 3\), the equation \((A - 3I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0\) gives \(-v_1 + v_2 = 0\), so \(\mathbf v_1 \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt2}[1,1]\T\).
    Why (4 steps)
    1. \(A - 3I \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\).
    2. Its first row gives the equation \(-v_1 + v_2 = 0\), so \(v_2 = v_1\). The second row gives the same equation, as it must, since the determinant is 0.
    3. So any vector \([t, t]\T\) works. Take \(t = 1\), which gives \([1,1]\T\).
    4. Divide by its length \(\sqrt2\) to make it a unit vector.
  5. For \(\lambda_2 = 1\), the equation \((A - I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0\) gives \(v_1 + v_2 = 0\), so \(\mathbf v_2 \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt2}[1,-1]\T\).
  6. Check. \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \)\({}= 4 \)\({}= \operatorname{tr}A\) and \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 \)\({}= 3 \)\({}= \det A\).

The check in the last line is a general fact, and it gives a shortcut. For any \(2\times2\) matrix, line 2 always comes out as \(\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A \)\({}= 0\). (Expand \((a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) - bc\) to see it.) A quadratic \(\lambda^2 - S\lambda + P\) has roots that add to \(S\) and multiply to \(P\).

Properties the course uses

  • The sum of the eigenvalues is the trace. The product of the eigenvalues is the determinant. So a matrix with a zero eigenvalue has determinant 0 and is singular.
  • If \(A\) is symmetric, all its eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal. The slide states orthogonality without this condition, but it needs symmetry. Covariance matrices are symmetric, so it always holds where the course uses it.
  • For a symmetric matrix, the number of non-zero eigenvalues equals the rank. (This can fail for non-symmetric matrices, such as \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\), which has rank 1 but both eigenvalues 0.)
  • A symmetric matrix is PSD if and only if all its eigenvalues are \(\ge 0\). So covariance matrices have non-negative eigenvalues.
  • \(A\T\) has the same eigenvalues as \(A\), because \(\det(A\T - \lambda I) \)\({}= \det(A - \lambda I)\). Its eigenvectors are generally different unless \(A\) is symmetric.
  • \(cA\) (every entry times the number \(c\)) has eigenvalues \(c\lambda_i\) and the same eigenvectors, because \(cA\mathbf v \)\({}= c\lambda\mathbf v\).
  • \(A^{-1}\) has eigenvalues \(1/\lambda_i\) and the same eigenvectors.

Eigen decomposition

A symmetric matrix can be rebuilt from its eigenvalues and unit eigenvectors:

\[A = Q\Lambda Q\T = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\,\mathbf v_i\mathbf v_i\T.\]
Reading the formula

\(Q\) has the unit eigenvectors as its columns, so it is orthogonal. \(\Lambda\) (capital lambda) is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. The second form writes \(A\) as a sum of simple pieces. Each \(\mathbf v_i\mathbf v_i\T\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix of rank 1 (a column times a row), weighted by \(\lambda_i\).

Example. With the matrix above, \(\mathbf v_1\mathbf v_1\T \)\({}= \frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf v_2\mathbf v_2\T \)\({}= \frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}\). Then \(3\cdot\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix} + 1\cdot\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix} \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\). That is \(A\).

If some \(\lambda_i\) are tiny, dropping their terms changes \(A\) very little. That is the whole idea of PCA, on page 1.

Remember
  • Solve \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\), then \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf v \)\({}= \mathbf 0\).
  • Sum of eigenvalues = trace, product = determinant.
  • Symmetric: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors, \(A \)\({}= \sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf v_i\mathbf v_i\T\), non-zero eigenvalue count = rank.
  • \(cA \to c\lambda\), \(A\T \to\) same \(\lambda\), \(A^{-1} \to 1/\lambda\).

B.6A first look at the SVD

Lectures L05, L07, L08 · Short video: SVD · Full treatment on page 1, SVD and low-rank approximation

Eigenvectors only work for square matrices. The singular value decomposition (SVD) does the same job for any matrix, including a rectangular data matrix.

\[M = UDV\T = \sum_i d_i\,\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i\T.\]
Reading the formula

\(M\) is any \(m\times n\) matrix (take \(m \ge n\)).

  • \(V\) is \(n\times n\) and orthogonal. Its columns \(\mathbf v_i\) are the right singular vectors.
  • \(D\) is \(n\times n\) and diagonal, with entries \(d_1 \ge d_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\), the singular values.
  • \(U\) is \(m\times n\) with orthonormal columns \(\mathbf u_i\), the left singular vectors, so \(U\T U = I_n\).

They come from two facts, \(M\T M = VD^2V\T\) and \(MM\T = UD^2U\T\). So the \(\mathbf v_i\) are eigenvectors of \(M\T M\), the \(\mathbf u_i\) are eigenvectors of \(MM\T\), and each \(d_i\) is the square root of an eigenvalue of \(M\T M\). The number of non-zero singular values is the rank of \(M\).

Example. \(M \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}\). \(M\T M \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{bmatrix}\), with trace 50 and determinant \(625 - 400 = 225\). The eigenvalues solve \(\lambda^2 - 50\lambda + 225 \)\({}= 0\), giving 45 and 5. So \(d_1 \)\({}= \sqrt{45} \)\({}= 3\sqrt5 \approx 6.71\) and \(d_2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.24\). The eigenvectors of \(M\T M\) are \(\mathbf v_1 \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt2}[1,1]\T\) and \(\mathbf v_2 \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt2}[1,-1]\T\). Each \(\mathbf u_i = M\mathbf v_i/d_i\), which gives \(\mathbf u_1 \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt{10}}[1,3]\T\) and \(\mathbf u_2 \)\({}= \frac1{\sqrt{10}}[3,-1]\T\).

Remember
  • \(M = UDV\T\): \(V\) from eigenvectors of \(M\T M\), \(U\) from eigenvectors of \(MM\T\), \(d_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(M\T M)}\).
  • Number of non-zero singular values = rank.
  • For a symmetric PSD matrix, the SVD and the eigen decomposition are the same thing.

B.7Probability, expectation and covariance

Lecture L09 "Probability: Revise" · Short video: Data from multivariate distribution

Probability gives a number between 0 and 1 to how likely something is. The course treats each data point as a random draw from some distribution and reasons about the classes with these rules.

Events and random variables

An event is something that may or may not happen, like "the email is spam". \(P(A)\) is its probability. A random variable is a quantity whose value is random, like the temperature tomorrow.

  • A discrete random variable takes separate values (a coin shows H or T). Its probabilities \(P(X = x)\) add up to 1. For example, a Bernoulli variable is 1 with probability \(p\) and 0 with probability \(1-p\).
  • A continuous random variable takes any value in a range. It has a probability density \(p(x)\), whose area under the curve over an interval gives the probability of landing there. The total area is 1. A density can be larger than 1 at a point, because only areas are probabilities.

Joint, conditional and the two rules

\(P(A, B)\) is the joint probability that both happen. \(P(A \mid B)\) is the conditional probability of \(A\) given that \(B\) happened. The bar \(\mid\) is read "given". (Some slides write it with a slash, \(P(A/B)\). It means the same.)

The product rule and the sum rule are

\[P(A, B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B),\]
\[P(A) = \sum_{B} P(A, B).\]
Reading the formula

The product rule says that for both to happen, \(B\) must happen (probability \(P(B)\)), and then \(A\) must happen given \(B\). Dividing both sides by \(P(B)\) gives the definition \(P(A\mid B) \)\({}= \frac{P(A,B)}{P(B)}\).

The sum rule says that to get \(P(A)\) on its own, add the joint probability over every possible value of \(B\). Combining the two gives the law of total probability:

\[P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i),\]

where \(B_1, B_2, \dots\) are cases that do not overlap and cover everything.

Example. A basket has 90 mangoes and 10 pears (L10). 5 of the mangoes and 8 of the pears are yellow. What is \(P(\text{yellow})\)?

  1. The two cases are mango and pear, with \(P(\text{mango}) = 0.9\) and \(P(\text{pear}) = 0.1\).
  2. \(P(\text{yellow}\mid\text{mango}) = \frac5{90}\) and \(P(\text{yellow}\mid\text{pear}) = \frac8{10}\).
  3. The mango term is \(\frac5{90}\times0.9 = 0.05\). The pear term is \(\frac8{10}\times0.1 = 0.08\).
  4. Add them. \(P(\text{yellow}) = 0.05 + 0.08 = 0.13\).
  5. Check by counting: 13 yellow fruits out of 100.

Two events are independent if \(P(A, B) = P(A)P(B)\), which is the same as \(P(A\mid B) = P(A)\). Knowing \(B\) tells you nothing about \(A\). Naive Bayes (page 2) assumes the features are independent once the class is known.

Expectation and variance

The expectation (mean) \(E[X]\) is the probability-weighted average value, \(E[X] = \sum_x x\,P(X = x)\), or \(\int x\,p(x)\,dx\) for a continuous variable. It is often written \(\mu\) (mu). The variance \(\operatorname{Var}(X) \)\({}= E[(X - \mu)^2]\), written \(\sigma^2\) (sigma squared), is the average squared distance from the mean. Its square root \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation.

From a sample \(x_1, \dots, x_N\), the course estimates these as

\[\mu = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N x_i,\qquad \sigma^2 = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2.\]

(Statistics courses often divide by \(N-1\) for the variance. SMAI uses \(1/N\), which is what maximum likelihood gives on page 2.)

Covariance and the covariance matrix

For two features, the covariance measures whether they move together. It is \(\operatorname{cov}(X, Y) \)\({}= E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]\). It is positive if they tend to be large together, negative if one is large when the other is small, and 0 if there is no linear relation.

For data points \(\mathbf x_i \in \R^d\), the covariance matrix collects all the variances and covariances:

\[\Sigma = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N (\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu)\T.\]
Reading the formula

\(\boldsymbol\mu\) is the mean vector. Each \((\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu)\) is a \(d\times1\) column, and a column times a row gives a \(d\times d\) matrix. Averaging these matrices gives \(\Sigma\) (capital sigma, not the summation sign here).

The diagonal entry \(\Sigma_{jj}\) is the variance of feature \(j\). The off-diagonal \(\Sigma_{jk}\) is the covariance of features \(j\) and \(k\). Since \(\Sigma_{jk} = \Sigma_{kj}\), the matrix is symmetric. It is also PSD, by the same argument as \(X\T X\) in B.3. If \(X\) is the mean-subtracted \(N\times d\) data matrix, \(\Sigma = \frac1N X\T X\).

Example. Points \((1,1), (2,3), (3,2)\).

  1. Mean: \(\boldsymbol\mu \)\({}= [\frac{1+2+3}3, \frac{1+3+2}3]\T \)\({}= [2,2]\T\).
  2. Subtract it. \((-1,-1), (0,1), (1,0)\).
  3. The variance of \(x\) is \(\frac{1 + 0 + 1}3 = \frac23\). The variance of \(y\) is \(\frac{1 + 1 + 0}3 = \frac23\).
  4. Covariance: \(\frac{(-1)(-1) + 0\cdot1 + 1\cdot0}3 \)\({}= \frac13\).
  5. So \(\Sigma \)\({}= \begin{bmatrix}2/3&1/3\\1/3&2/3\end{bmatrix}\). It is symmetric, and its eigenvalues are 1 and \(\frac13\), both \(\ge 0\).
Remember
  • \(P(A,B) = P(A\mid B)P(B)\) and \(P(A) \)\({}= \sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)\).
  • \(\mu = \frac1N\sum x_i\), \(\sigma^2 \)\({}= \frac1N\sum(x_i - \mu)^2\).
  • \(\Sigma \)\({}= \frac1N\sum(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu)\T\) is \(d\times d\), symmetric and PSD. It is diagonal when the features are uncorrelated.

B.8Bayes' theorem

Lectures L06, L09, L10 · Tutorial 2 (probability and GMMs)

Bayes' theorem turns "how likely is the evidence if the class is true" into "how likely is the class now that I have seen the evidence". Every probabilistic classifier in the course rests on it.

\[P(\omega_i \mid \mathbf x) = \frac{p(\mathbf x \mid \omega_i)\,P(\omega_i)}{p(\mathbf x)},\]
\[p(\mathbf x) = \sum_j p(\mathbf x\mid\omega_j)\,P(\omega_j).\]
Reading the formula

\(\omega_i\) (omega i) is class \(i\), and \(\mathbf x\) is what we observed.

  • \(P(\omega_i)\) is the prior, how common the class is before we look at \(\mathbf x\).
  • \(p(\mathbf x\mid\omega_i)\) is the likelihood, how probable this observation is if the class is \(\omega_i\).
  • \(p(\mathbf x)\) is the evidence, the overall probability of the observation. The second line computes it with the law of total probability.
  • \(P(\omega_i\mid\mathbf x)\) is the posterior, the probability of the class after seeing \(\mathbf x\).

In words, the posterior is the likelihood times the prior, divided by the evidence. The bottom is the same for every class, so it only rescales the posteriors to add up to 1.

It follows from the product rule, which gives \(P(\omega, \mathbf x) \)\({}= P(\omega\mid\mathbf x)p(\mathbf x)\) and also \(P(\omega, \mathbf x) \)\({}= p(\mathbf x\mid\omega)P(\omega)\). Set them equal and divide by \(p(\mathbf x)\).

Example (L10). Mangoes 90, pears 10. 5 mangoes and 8 pears are yellow. You see a yellow fruit. \(P(\text{mango}\mid\text{yellow}) \)\({}= \frac{(5/90)(90/100)}{0.13} \)\({}= \frac{0.05}{0.13} \approx 0.385\), and \(P(\text{pear}\mid\text{yellow}) \)\({}= \frac{0.08}{0.13} \approx 0.615\). The prior said mango (0.9), but the evidence makes pear the better guess.

When a question gives "true positive rate" and "false positive rate" for a test, translate them first. The true positive rate is \(P(+\mid\text{sick})\). The false positive rate is \(P(+\mid\text{healthy})\).

Common mistake

\(P(+\mid\text{sick})\) is not \(P(\text{sick}\mid+)\). A test that catches 90% of sick people can still be wrong most of the time it says "positive" when the disease is rare. Always write down which conditional you are given and which one you want.

Remember
  • Posterior = likelihood × prior ÷ evidence, with evidence \(= \sum_j\) likelihood\(_j\) × prior\(_j\).
  • Write the four numbers (two priors, two likelihoods) before computing.
  • A rare class needs strong evidence to become the most probable.

B.9The Gaussian (normal) distribution

Lectures L09, L10 · Short video: Geometry of Gaussians

The Gaussian is the bell-shaped distribution. The course uses it to model each class, the noise in regression and the clusters of a GMM.

\[p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}.\]
Reading the formula

This is the density of the univariate (one-variable) normal distribution, written \(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\). \(\mu\) is the mean (the centre of the bell) and \(\sigma^2\) is the variance (how wide it is). \(\exp(z)\) means \(e^z\).

  • The exponent \(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\) is 0 at \(x = \mu\) and becomes more negative as \(x\) moves away, so the density is highest at the mean and falls off on both sides. Dividing by \(\sigma^2\) measures the distance in units of the spread.
  • The front factor \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\) is chosen so the total area is 1. A wider bell (bigger \(\sigma\)) must be lower.

Example. For \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), at \(x = 0\) the density is \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.399\). At \(x = 1\) it is \(0.399\times e^{-1/2} \approx 0.399\times0.607 \approx 0.242\).

About 68% of the probability lies within one \(\sigma\) of the mean, 95% within two and 99.7% within three.

For a vector \(\mathbf x \in \R^d\), the multivariate normal \(\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)\) has density

\[p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\,|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\,e^{-\Delta^2/2},\]
\[\Delta^2 = (\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)\T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu).\]
Reading the formula

Each piece is the vector version of the 1-D formula.

  • \(\boldsymbol\mu \in \R^d\) is the mean vector, the centre.
  • \(\Sigma\) is the \(d\times d\) covariance matrix (B.7). It plays the role of \(\sigma^2\).
  • \(\Delta^2\) (delta squared) replaces \(\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^2}\). It is a single number, the squared Mahalanobis distance from \(\mathbf x\) to the centre, measured in units of the spread in each direction.
  • \(|\Sigma|\) here means the determinant of \(\Sigma\), not an absolute value. It replaces \(\sigma^2\) in the normalising factor, and \((2\pi)^{d/2}\) replaces \(\sqrt{2\pi}\).

Example. If \(\Sigma = \sigma^2 I\) (same variance in every direction, no correlation), then \(\Sigma^{-1} \)\({}= \frac1{\sigma^2}I\) and the exponent becomes \(-\frac{\|\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\). The density depends only on the ordinary distance from the centre, and its contours are circles. If \(\Sigma\) is diagonal with different entries, the contours are ellipses lined up with the axes. A general \(\Sigma\) gives tilted ellipses whose axes are the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\).

Page 2 shows that when two classes are Gaussian with the same \(\Sigma\), the Bayes decision boundary is a straight line, and with different \(\Sigma\) it is curved.

Remember
  • In 1-D the density is \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\).
  • In \(d\) dimensions it is \(\frac1{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}e^{-\Delta^2/2}\) with \(\Delta^2 = (\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\).
  • \(\Sigma = \sigma^2I\) gives circular contours, diagonal \(\Sigma\) gives axis-aligned ellipses.
  • Taking the log turns the product of exponentials into sums of squares, which is how MLE and MSE meet on page 2.

B.10Derivatives and minima

Lectures L03, L04 · Short video: MSE using GD

Training a model means finding the parameters that make a loss as small as possible. Derivatives tell us which way is downhill.

The derivative \(f'(x)\), also written \(\frac{df}{dx}\), is the slope of \(f\) at \(x\). It says how much \(f\) changes per unit change in \(x\). The rules you need are \(\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}\), \(\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac1x\), and that the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives.

At a minimum or maximum of a smooth function, the slope is 0. So to find the minimum, set \(f'(x) = 0\) and solve. The second derivative \(f''(x)\) tells you which it is. If \(f'' > 0\), the curve bends upward and the point is a minimum.

A function whose second derivative is never negative is convex (bowl-shaped). A convex function has no separate local minima. Any point with zero slope is the global minimum. Linear regression's loss is convex, and a neural network's is not, which is why L03 and L15 ask about "global versus local optima".

Example

Minimise \(f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 4\) (an L03 problem).

  1. \(f'(x) = 2x - 2\).
  2. Set it to 0. \(2x - 2 = 0\), so \(x = 1\).
  3. \(f''(x) = 2 > 0\), so this is a minimum.
  4. The minimum value is \(f(1) = 1 - 2 + 4 = 3\). On the slide's options that is (c).
  5. Check by completing the square. \(x^2 - 2x + 4 = (x - 1)^2 + 3\), which is at least 3 and equals 3 at \(x = 1\).

When setting the derivative to zero cannot be solved by hand, we walk downhill instead. That is gradient descent, \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta f'(x_k)\), where \(\eta\) (eta) is a small step size called the learning rate. Page 1 covers it in detail. The minus sign moves against the slope, which is downhill.

Remember
  • Minimum: \(f'(x) = 0\) and \(f''(x) > 0\).
  • Convex means any stationary point is the global minimum.
  • Gradient descent: \(x \leftarrow x - \eta f'(x)\).

B.11Gradients of vector expressions

Lectures L03, L04, L06, L09 · Reference: The Matrix Cookbook

When the parameter is a vector \(\mathbf w\), the derivative becomes a vector of slopes called the gradient. Two identities do almost all the work in this course.

A partial derivative \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_k}\) (the curly \(\partial\) is read "partial") is the derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(w_k\), treating every other variable as a constant. The gradient \(\nabla f\) (nabla f), also written \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf w}\), stacks them into a vector:

\[\nabla f(\mathbf w) = \Big[\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_d}\Big]\T.\]

The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. Gradient descent steps the other way, \(\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\nabla f(\mathbf w)\). At a minimum of a smooth function, every partial derivative is 0, so \(\nabla f = \mathbf 0\).

Example

\(f(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 + 3w_1w_2\). Treating \(w_2\) as a constant, \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_1} \)\({}= 2w_1 + 3w_2\). Treating \(w_1\) as a constant, \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_2} \)\({}= 3w_1\). So \(\nabla f \)\({}= [2w_1 + 3w_2,\ 3w_1]\T\). At \(\mathbf w = [1,2]\T\) it is \([8, 3]\T\).

The two identities

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf w}(\mathbf w\T\mathbf a) = \mathbf a,\]
\[\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf w}(\mathbf w\T A\mathbf w) = (A + A\T)\mathbf w.\]

If \(A\) is symmetric, \(A + A\T = 2A\), so the second gradient is \(2A\mathbf w\).

Write this in the exam

Derive both identities.

  1. \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf a \)\({}= \sum_i w_ia_i\), so \(\frac{\partial}{\partial w_k} \)\({}= a_k\), and the gradient is \(\mathbf a\).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Write the dot product as a sum. \(w_1a_1 + w_2a_2 + \dots + w_da_d\).
    2. Differentiate with respect to one entry \(w_k\). Every term without \(w_k\) is a constant and gives 0. The term \(w_ka_k\) gives \(a_k\).
    3. Stack the \(d\) partial derivatives. \([a_1, \dots, a_d]\T \)\({}= \mathbf a\).
  2. \(\mathbf w\T A\mathbf w \)\({}= \sum_i\sum_j w_iA_{ij}w_j\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. \(A\mathbf w\) is the vector whose entry \(i\) is \(\sum_jA_{ij}w_j\).
    2. Dotting with \(\mathbf w\) multiplies entry \(i\) by \(w_i\) and adds over \(i\).
  3. \(\frac{\partial}{\partial w_k} \)\({}= \sum_j A_{kj}w_j + \sum_i w_iA_{ik} \)\({}= (A\mathbf w)_k + (A\T\mathbf w)_k\).
    Why (4 steps)
    1. \(w_k\) appears in the double sum in two ways, as \(w_i\) with \(i = k\) and as \(w_j\) with \(j = k\).
    2. Terms with \(i = k\) are \(w_k\sum_jA_{kj}w_j\). Differentiating gives \(\sum_jA_{kj}w_j\), which is entry \(k\) of \(A\mathbf w\).
    3. Terms with \(j = k\) are \(w_k\sum_iw_iA_{ik}\). Differentiating gives \(\sum_iA_{ik}w_i\). Since \(A_{ik} = (A\T)_{ki}\), this is entry \(k\) of \(A\T\mathbf w\).
    4. The term \(i = j = k\) is \(A_{kk}w_k^2\), with derivative \(2A_{kk}w_k\). It is counted once in each of the two sums above, so nothing is missed or doubled.
  4. So the gradient is \((A + A\T)\mathbf w\), which is \(2A\mathbf w\) when \(A = A\T\).
  5. Check in 1-D. With \(A = a\), \(\mathbf w\T A\mathbf w = aw^2\) and its derivative is \(2aw\). It matches.

These two identities give the closed-form regression \(\mathbf w \)\({}= (X\T X)^{-1}X\T\mathbf y\) on page 1, the mean as the best representative point, the eigenvector condition of PCA (B.13) and Fisher's LDA.

Example

Gradient of \(J(\mathbf w) \)\({}= \|\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\|^2\), the regression loss.

  1. Expand. \((\mathbf y - X\mathbf w)\T(\mathbf y - X\mathbf w) \)\({}= \mathbf y\T\mathbf y - 2\mathbf w\T X\T\mathbf y + \mathbf w\T X\T X\mathbf w\). (The two cross terms \(\mathbf y\T X\mathbf w\) and \(\mathbf w\T X\T\mathbf y\) are the same number, so they combine.)
  2. \(\mathbf y\T\mathbf y\) has no \(\mathbf w\), so its gradient is \(\mathbf 0\).
  3. \(-2\mathbf w\T(X\T\mathbf y)\) has gradient \(-2X\T\mathbf y\), by the first identity with \(\mathbf a = X\T\mathbf y\).
  4. \(\mathbf w\T(X\T X)\mathbf w\) has gradient \(2X\T X\mathbf w\), by the second identity, since \(X\T X\) is symmetric.
  5. Total: \(\nabla J \)\({}= 2X\T X\mathbf w - 2X\T\mathbf y\). Page 1 sets this to zero.

The Hessian is the matrix of second partial derivatives, \(H_{jk} \)\({}= \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial w_j\partial w_k}\). It is the vector version of \(f''\). If it is PSD everywhere, \(f\) is convex. For \(J\) above, \(H = 2X\T X\), which is PSD (B.3), so the regression loss is convex. Newton's method (L18) uses the Hessian to take better steps than plain gradient descent.

Remember
  • \(\nabla(\mathbf w\T\mathbf a) \)\({}= \mathbf a\), \(\nabla(\mathbf w\T A\mathbf w) \)\({}= (A + A\T)\mathbf w\).
  • \(\nabla\|\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\|^2 \)\({}= 2X\T X\mathbf w - 2X\T\mathbf y\).
  • Hessian PSD means convex.

B.12The chain rule

Lectures L11, L15, L16 · Short video: Neural networks basics

A neural network is a long chain of simple functions. The chain rule says how to differentiate a chain, and backpropagation is the chain rule applied carefully.

If \(y = f(u)\) and \(u = g(x)\), then

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}.\]
Reading the formula

A small change in \(x\) changes \(u\) by \(\frac{du}{dx}\) times as much, and that change in \(u\) changes \(y\) by \(\frac{dy}{du}\) times as much. So the overall rate is the product. Longer chains just multiply more factors, as in \(\frac{dL}{dw} \)\({}= \frac{dL}{do}\cdot\frac{do}{ds}\cdot\frac{ds}{dw}\).

Example. \(L = (wx - y)^2\) with \(w = 2\), \(x = 3\), \(y = 4\). Let \(o = wx = 6\). Then \(\frac{dL}{do} = 2(o - y) = 4\) and \(\frac{do}{dw} = x = 3\). So \(\frac{dL}{dw} = 4\times3 = 12\). To check, expand \(L = (3w - 4)^2\), so \(\frac{dL}{dw} \)\({}= 2(3w - 4)\cdot3 \)\({}= 6(6 - 4) \)\({}= 12\).

The sigmoid and its derivative

The sigmoid (or logistic) function \(\sigma(z) = \frac1{1 + e^{-z}}\) squashes any number into the range 0 to 1. Logistic regression (page 2) and early neural networks (page 3) use it. (The slides also write it \(g(z)\) or \(\phi(z)\). This \(\sigma\) is not a standard deviation.)

Write this in the exam

Show that \(\sigma'(z) \)\({}= \sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big)\).

  1. \(\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}\), so \(\sigma'(z) \)\({}= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}\).
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Let \(u = 1 + e^{-z}\), so \(\sigma = u^{-1}\).
    2. \(\frac{d\sigma}{du} = -u^{-2}\).
    3. \(\frac{du}{dz} = -e^{-z}\), using the chain rule on \(e^{-z}\) (outer \(e^v\), inner \(v = -z\)).
    4. Multiply. \(\sigma'(z) \)\({}= (-u^{-2})(-e^{-z}) \)\({}= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}\).
  2. Split. \(\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} \)\({}= \frac1{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}\).
  3. \(\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} \)\({}= 1 - \frac1{1+e^{-z}} \)\({}= 1 - \sigma(z)\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. Write the top as \((1 + e^{-z}) - 1\).
    2. Divide each part by \(1 + e^{-z}\). \(1 - \frac1{1+e^{-z}}\).
  4. So \(\sigma'(z) \)\({}= \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))\).
  5. Check at \(z = 0\). \(\sigma(0) = \frac12\), so \(\sigma'(0) = \frac14\). From line 1, \(\frac{1}{(1+1)^2} = \frac14\). It matches.

The largest value of \(\sigma'\) is \(\frac14\), at \(z = 0\). Far from 0 it is nearly 0. A chain of many sigmoids multiplies many numbers below \(\frac14\), so the gradient shrinks towards zero. That is the vanishing gradient problem of L16, and it is one reason ReLU is used instead.

The hyperbolic tangent \(\tanh(z) \)\({}= \frac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}}\) squashes into \(-1\) to 1. Its derivative is \(\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z)\).

Remember
  • Chain rule: multiply the local derivatives along the chain.
  • \(\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)\), at most \(\frac14\). \(\tanh' = 1 - \tanh^2\), at most 1.
  • \(\frac{\partial(wx)}{\partial w} \)\({}= x\), \(\frac{\partial(wx)}{\partial x} \)\({}= w\), \(\frac{\partial(o - y)^2}{\partial o} \)\({}= 2(o - y)\).

B.13Lagrange multipliers

Lectures L04, L06, L12 · Short video: Optimization and eigen vectors

Some problems ask for the best \(\mathbf w\) subject to a condition, such as "\(\mathbf w\) has length 1". A Lagrange multiplier turns that into an ordinary "set the gradient to zero" problem.

To maximise (or minimise) \(f(\mathbf w)\) subject to the constraint \(g(\mathbf w) = c\), form the Lagrangian

\[\mathcal L(\mathbf w, \lambda) = f(\mathbf w) - \lambda\big(g(\mathbf w) - c\big)\]

and set its partial derivatives with respect to \(\mathbf w\) and \(\lambda\) to zero. The new variable \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier, named after the mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange.

Reading the formula

Setting \(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda} \)\({}= 0\) gives back \(g(\mathbf w) = c\), so the constraint is enforced. Setting \(\nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathcal L \)\({}= \mathbf 0\) gives \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\). This says that at the best point, the gradient of \(f\) is parallel to the gradient of the constraint. If it were not, you could slide along the constraint and still increase \(f\).

Example. Maximise \(f = xy\) subject to \(x + y = 10\). \(\mathcal L \)\({}= xy - \lambda(x + y - 10)\). The partial derivatives are \(y - \lambda = 0\) and \(x - \lambda = 0\), so \(x = y = \lambda\). The constraint gives \(2\lambda = 10\), so \(x = y = 5\) and \(f = 25\). Check: \(x = 4, y = 6\) gives 24, which is smaller.

The slides call the theory of Lagrange multipliers out of scope, but the following result is used for PCA (L06), for LDA (L08) and in the SVM dual (L12). Learn the derivation.

Write this in the exam

Show that the maximum of \(\mathbf w\T A\mathbf w\) subject to \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf w = 1\), for symmetric \(A\), is the largest eigenvalue, reached at its eigenvector.

  1. \(\mathcal L(\mathbf w,\lambda) \)\({}= \mathbf w\T A\mathbf w - \lambda(\mathbf w\T\mathbf w - 1)\).
  2. \(\nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathcal L \)\({}= 2A\mathbf w - 2\lambda\mathbf w \)\({}= \mathbf 0\), so \(A\mathbf w = \lambda\mathbf w\).
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Gradient of \(\mathbf w\T A\mathbf w\) is \(2A\mathbf w\), by B.11 with symmetric \(A\).
    2. Gradient of \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf w\) is \(2\mathbf w\), by B.11 with \(A = I\). The constant \(-1\) has gradient \(\mathbf 0\).
    3. So \(\nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathcal L \)\({}= 2A\mathbf w - 2\lambda\mathbf w\). Set it to \(\mathbf 0\).
    4. Divide by 2 and move the second term across. \(A\mathbf w = \lambda\mathbf w\). This is the eigenvector equation, so \(\mathbf w\) must be an eigenvector of \(A\) and \(\lambda\) its eigenvalue.
  3. At such a \(\mathbf w\), the objective is \(\mathbf w\T A\mathbf w \)\({}= \lambda\,\mathbf w\T\mathbf w \)\({}= \lambda\).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. Replace \(A\mathbf w\) by \(\lambda\mathbf w\) from line 2. \(\mathbf w\T(\lambda\mathbf w) \)\({}= \lambda\,\mathbf w\T\mathbf w\).
    2. The constraint says \(\mathbf w\T\mathbf w = 1\), so the value is \(\lambda\).
  4. So the maximum is the largest eigenvalue, at its unit eigenvector. The minimum is the smallest eigenvalue, at its unit eigenvector.

  • \(\max \mathbf w\T A\mathbf w\) with \(\|\mathbf w\| = 1\) gives the top eigenvector, and the value is the top eigenvalue. Minimising gives the bottom one.
