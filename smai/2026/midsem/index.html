Mid-sem 2026, solved

The mid-sem from early March 2026, all 16 questions, each solved step by step with the marks the TAs gave for it. Do it as a timed paper first (90 minutes), then read the answers. With review, plan on about 3 hours.

Read this first

The mid-sem was held in early March 2026, after L14. It covered L01 to L13, so everything on the maths basics page, Page 1 and Page 2. It was worth 12% of the grade, raised to 14% at the end of the semester.

The paper had three parts, each meant to take 30 minutes.

PartKind of questionQuestionsPoints
Q-IShort answers, such as a formula, a matrix, true or false, or a multiple-choice pick10 of 3 points30
Q-IIPrecise answers, such as a short proof, a calculation or a short program4 of 7.5 points30
Q-IIIDetailed answers, here a long hand calculation and a full Python pipeline2 of 15 points30

The class average, announced on 3 April, was 44.98. The announcement did not give the maximum. If it was out of 90, the average was about half marks.

Where this page comes from. The question paper itself was not posted. This page is built from the TAs' answer key and grading rubric, which a student shared after the course. The key restates each question briefly, so the questions below are rebuilt from it with every number kept. Where the key gives options for a multiple-choice question but not their exact wording, this page says so. Every answer was checked again independently, and the three places where the key needs a correction are marked.

What the marking rewards

The rubric shows a few patterns that are worth knowing before the exam.

  • Define every symbol. Writing μ in the covariance formula without saying it is the mean cost 0.5 of 3 points.
  • Write the full formula, not a shortcut. For gradient descent, writing only 𝐰k+1=𝐰k−η∇ℒ without working out ∇ℒ earned 1 of 3 points.
  • Get the constants right. A missing factor of 2 or a missing 1N each cost marks, and a wrong sign cost more than either.
  • Python is expected. Two questions asked for numpy code. Pseudocode lost marks, and using scikit-learn was explicitly not allowed (at most 1 of 5.5 points).
  • Show intermediate steps. A correct final answer with no working was capped (4 of 7.5 in the Bayes question).
  • Multiple-choice questions give little partial credit. A wrong pick usually scores 0. A few "nearly right" picks score 0.5 or 1.

How to use this page

  1. Take a pen, paper and a timer. Read only the question lines (the grey rows) of Part 1 and answer them in 30 minutes without opening any answer.
  2. Do the same for Part 2 and then Part 3, 30 minutes each.
  3. Now open each answer. Mark yourself with the "Marks" row, which gives the TAs' rubric.
  4. For every question you lost marks on, follow the link in "Where it comes from" and reread that section before trying the question again.

Each answer has four rows. "Formula" is what to use. "Where it comes from" links to the section of the guide that teaches it. "Working" does it step by step and ends with a check. "Marks" summarises how the TAs graded it.

M.1Part 1, short answers

Ten questions of 3 points each. Each needs one formula, one matrix or one choice, so aim for 3 minutes each.

Mid-sem 2026Q1.1 (3 points). A set 𝒳={𝐱i} holds N samples 𝐱i∈ℝd drawn from a multivariate Gaussian. Write the formula for its covariance matrix Σ.›
Formula
𝝁=1N∑i=1N𝐱i and Σ=1N∑i=1N(𝐱i−𝝁)(𝐱i−𝝁)𝖳.
Where it comes from
B.7, Reading the formula for the covariance matrix (the mean is defined just above it under "Expectation and variance"). 2.6 shows this is the maximum likelihood estimate.
Working
  1. First define the mean, because the covariance uses it. 𝝁=1N∑i=1N𝐱i, a vector in ℝd.
  2. Subtract the mean from each sample. 𝐱i−𝝁 is a d×1 column.
  3. Multiply each centred column by its own transpose. (𝐱i−𝝁)(𝐱i−𝝁)𝖳 is (d×1)(1×d)=d×d. This is called an outer product.
  4. Average the N outer products. Σ=1N∑i=1N(𝐱i−𝝁)(𝐱i−𝝁)𝖳.
  5. The key also accepts 1N−1 in place of 1N. That version is the unbiased estimate (Bessel's correction).
  6. Check the size. Σ must be d×d, one row and one column per feature, and it is.
Marks
3 for the full expression with the mean subtracted and the outer product. 2.5 if 𝝁 is used but not defined as the mean. 1.5 if the transpose is missing, so the product is not an outer product. 1 for 1N∑𝐱i𝐱i𝖳 without subtracting the mean. 0.5 for only the scalar variance formula.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.2 (3 points). A transformation 𝐱′=𝐖𝐱 of 𝐱=[x1,x2]𝖳 gives x′1=x1+x2 and x′2=x1−x2. Write 𝐖.›
Formula
Row i of 𝐖 holds the coefficients of the inputs in output x′i.
Where it comes from
1.4, Reading the formula for 𝐱′=𝐖𝐱 (row r of 𝐖 gives new feature r).
Working
  1. The superscripts here are component numbers, not powers. x1 is the first feature and x2 the second.
  2. x′1=1⋅x1+1⋅x2, so row 1 is [1,1].
  3. x′2=1⋅x1+(−1)⋅x2, so row 2 is [1,−1].
  4. So 𝐖=[111−1].
  5. Check with 𝐱=[3,1]𝖳. 𝐖𝐱=[3+1,3−1]𝖳=[4,2]𝖳, which matches x1+x2=4 and x1−x2=2.
Marks
3 for the correct matrix. 1.5 for one sign wrong. 1 for the transpose. Here the transpose happens to be the same matrix, because 𝐖 is symmetric, so this deduction cannot arise for this 𝐖.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.3 (3 points). Data in ℝ2 has mean [1,1]𝖳. Write a Python snippet that makes every sample have unit L2 norm and makes the mean zero.›
Formula
Centre with 𝐱←𝐱−𝝁, then normalise with 𝐱←𝐱/‖𝐱‖2.
Where it comes from
1.4, box "Mean-centre a data set, then scale it", lines 2 and 5.
Working
import numpy as np
# X has shape (N, 2), one sample per row
X = X - np.mean(X, axis=0)                      # centre: subtract the column means, shape stays (N, 2)
norms = np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)  # length of each row, shape (N, 1)
X = X / norms                                    # divide each row by its own length
  1. np.mean(X, axis=0) averages down the rows and gives the mean vector, shape (2,). Subtracting it from X subtracts it from every row. Since the mean is given as [1,1], writing X - np.array([1, 1]) also earned full marks.
  2. np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1) gives one length per row. keepdims=True keeps the shape as (N, 1) so that the division in the next line divides each row by its own length.
  3. Check one row. If a centred row is [3,4], its norm is 5 and it becomes [0.6,0.8], whose length is 0.36+0.64=1.
The two requests conflict

After the rows are scaled to length 1, the mean is usually no longer exactly zero, because each row is divided by a different number. For example, take the centred data [2,0], [−1,0], [−1,0]. Its mean is zero. After normalising, the rows are [1,0], [−1,0], [−1,0], with mean [−13,0]. The key says so and accepts either order of the two steps.

Marks
3 for both steps with the right axis and keepdims. 2 if both steps are there but the axis is wrong. 1.5 for only the centring. 1 for only the normalisation. 0.5 for scikit-learn's StandardScaler, which scales each column to unit variance and does not make rows unit length.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.4 (3 points). True or false? "In k-NN, if we increase K, the decision boundary becomes more and more smooth."›
Formula
k-NN predicts the majority label of the K nearest training points.
Where it comes from
1.2, box "Classify a test point with k-NN and find the accuracy", lines 3 and 4, and the paragraph "Choosing K" after it. The bias and variance view is in 2.13.
Working
  1. With K=1, the label at a point is the label of the single nearest training point. One noisy training point creates its own small island, so the boundary is jagged.
  2. With a larger K, each prediction is a vote over many points. A single noisy point is outvoted, so the small islands disappear and the boundary becomes smoother.
  3. Answer: true.
  4. Check with the extreme. With K=N, every point gets the majority label of the whole training set, so there is no boundary at all, which is the smoothest possible. In bias–variance terms, a small K has low bias and high variance, and a large K has high bias and low variance.
Marks
3 for true, 0 for false. No partial credit.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.5 (3 points). The MSE loss is ℒ=1N∑i=1N(yi−𝐰𝖳𝐱i)2. Write the gradient descent update for 𝐰k+1.›
Formula
𝐰k+1=𝐰k−η∇ℒ(𝐰k), with ∇ℒ=−2N∑i(yi−𝐰𝖳𝐱i)𝐱i.
Where it comes from
1.7, box "Derive the gradient descent update", lines 2 and 3.
Working
  1. Differentiate one term. By the chain rule, ∇𝐰(yi−𝐰𝖳𝐱i)2=2(yi−𝐰𝖳𝐱i)⋅(−𝐱i). The −𝐱i is the gradient of the inside, yi−𝐰𝖳𝐱i.
  2. Average over the N terms. ∇ℒ=−2N∑i=1N(yi−𝐰𝖳𝐱i)𝐱i.
  3. Step against the gradient with learning rate η. The two minus signs combine into a plus. 𝐰k+1=𝐰k+2ηN∑i=1N(yi−(𝐰k)𝖳𝐱i)𝐱i.
  4. In matrix form, with X of size N×d and 𝐲 of size N×1, this is 𝐰k+1=𝐰k+2ηNX𝖳(𝐲−X𝐰k).
  5. Check the sign with one sample in 1-D. Take x=1, y=3, w=0. The prediction 0 is too small, and the update adds 2η1(3−0)(1)=6η>0, which moves w up toward 3, as it should.
Marks
3 for the full update with the right sign, the factor 2 and the 1N. 2.5 without the factor 2. 2 without the 1N. 1.5 for the right gradient without writing the update. 1 for the wrong sign (that is gradient ascent). 1 for writing only 𝐰−η∇ℒ without working out the gradient.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.6 (3 points). The best solution of an overdetermined homogeneous system 𝐀𝐱=𝟎, with 𝐀 of size m×n and m>n, is the eigenvector corresponding to (a) the largest eigenvalue of 𝐀𝐀𝖳 (b) the largest eigenvalue of 𝐀𝖳𝐀 (c) the smallest eigenvalue of 𝐀𝐀𝖳 (d) the smallest eigenvalue of 𝐀𝖳𝐀.›
Formula
The minimum of 𝐱𝖳𝐌𝐱 over unit vectors 𝐱 is the smallest eigenvalue of 𝐌, reached at its eigenvector.
Where it comes from
1.9, box "Maximise 𝐰𝖳𝐀𝐰 subject to 𝐰𝖳𝐰=1", lines 3 and 4, applied to ‖𝐀𝐱‖2=𝐱𝖳𝐀𝖳𝐀𝐱.
Working
  1. With m>n there are more equations than unknowns, so usually only 𝐱=𝟎 solves them exactly. That answer is useless, so ask for the unit vector that makes ‖𝐀𝐱‖ as small as possible.
  2. Square the norm. ‖𝐀𝐱‖2=(𝐀𝐱)𝖳(𝐀𝐱)=𝐱𝖳(𝐀𝖳𝐀)𝐱.
  3. So the matrix in the middle is 𝐀𝖳𝐀, which is n×n, matching 𝐱∈ℝn.
  4. It is a minimisation, so the answer is the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue. Answer: (d).
  5. Check (c). 𝐀𝐀𝖳 is m×m, so its eigenvectors have m entries. They cannot even be multiplied by 𝐀, so (c) cannot be right.
Marks
3 for (d). 1 for (c), the right idea with the wrong matrix. 0.5 for (b), the right matrix with the wrong end of the spectrum. 0 for (a).
Mid-sem 2026Q1.7 (3 points). A=aI is a diagonal matrix with every diagonal entry equal to a. What is its SVD UDV𝖳?›
Formula
A=UDV𝖳 with U and V orthogonal and D diagonal with entries ≥0.
Where it comes from
1.11, box "Find the SVD by hand", lines 1 to 4, and the Remember box in 1.11 (singular values are never negative).
Working
  1. If a>0, the matrix is already in the required form. Take U=I, D=aI, V=I. Then UDV𝖳=I⋅aI⋅I=aI.
  2. If a<0, D=aI is not allowed, because singular values cannot be negative. Use D=|a|I and move the sign into V. Take U=I, D=|a|I, V=−I. Then UDV𝖳=|a|I⋅(−I)=−|a|I=aI.
  3. So in general U=I, D=|a|I, V=sign⁡(a)I. All the singular values equal |a|.
  4. If a=0, then D=0 and any orthogonal U and V work. The key's V=sign⁡(0)I=0 is not orthogonal in this case, which is a small gap in the key.
  5. The SVD is not unique here. Any orthogonal Q gives A=Q(|a|I)(sign⁡(a)Q)𝖳, because QQ𝖳=I. The key accepts any valid pair.
Marks
3 for U=V=I and D=aI (assuming a>0) or any valid answer with D=|a|I. Not discussing the sign was accepted. 1.5 for only saying "the singular values are all a". 1.5 for U=V=I with D wrong.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.8 (3 points). The 1-D data is (+1,+), (+2,+), (+3,−), (+4,−). Give the SVM decision rule and the support vectors.›
Formula
A hard-margin SVM needs yi(wxi+b)≥1 for every sample, with equality at the support vectors, and margin 2/|w|.
Where it comes from
2.11, box "Show that the margin … is 2‖w‖ …", lines 2 and 4
Working
  1. The positives are 1 and 2. The negatives are 3 and 4. The two classes are separated, and the closest pair across the gap is 2 (positive) and 3 (negative). These are the support vectors.
  2. The widest gap is obtained with the boundary halfway between them, at x=2.5.
  3. Find w and b from the support vectors, where the constraint holds with equality. For x=2, y=+1, the condition is 2w+b=1.
  4. For x=3, y=−1, the condition is −(3w+b)=1, so 3w+b=−1.
  5. Subtract the first equation from the second. w=−2. Then b=1−2w=5.
  6. The rule is f(x)=sign⁡(−2x+5), the same as sign⁡(−x+2.5). Predict + when x<2.5 and − when x>2.5.
  7. Check the other points. x=1 gives −2+5=3≥1. x=4 gives −8+5=−3, and y(wx+b)=3≥1. Both satisfy the constraint without touching it, so they are not support vectors. The margin is 2/|w|=1, the gap from 2 to 3.
Marks
3 for support vectors 2 and 3 and the boundary at 2.5. 2 if the boundary is missing or slightly off. 1.5 for the boundary without naming the support vectors. 1 for calling all four points support vectors.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.9 (3 points). 100 samples each come from 𝒩(20,σ2) and 𝒩(30,2σ2). Where is the optimal Bayesian threshold? The key's answer is "(a) closer to 20". The other options are not listed in the key, but its notes imply they were about the threshold being closer to 30, exactly at 25, or beyond 30.›
Formula
With equal priors, the threshold is where the two densities are equal. Compare log densities −(x−μ)22s2−12ln⁡s2, dropping the shared 12ln⁡2π.
Where it comes from
2.5, box "Two 1-D Gaussian classes with equal priors, means μ1<μ2 and different variances …", lines 1 to 6
Working
  1. Equal sample counts mean equal priors, P(C1)=P(C2)=12. So the threshold is where p(x|C1)=p(x|C2).
  2. Write the log densities. Class 1 has variance σ2 and class 2 has variance 2σ2. ln⁡p1=−(x−20)22σ2−12ln⁡σ2, ln⁡p2=−(x−30)24σ2−12ln⁡(2σ2). The threshold is where ln⁡p1=ln⁡p2.
  3. Collect the log terms on the right. −12ln⁡(2σ2)+12ln⁡σ2=−12ln⁡2.
  4. Move the squared terms to the left and multiply both sides by 4σ2. −2(x−20)2+(x−30)2=−2σ2ln⁡2.
  5. Expand the left side. −2(x2−40x+400)+(x2−60x+900)=−x2+20x+100.
  6. So x2−20x−100−2σ2ln⁡2=0. The quadratic formula gives x=10±200+2σ2ln⁡2.
  7. Take the root between the means. x∗=10+200+2σ2ln⁡2. The other root, below 0, is a second crossing far in the left tail, where the wide class 2 overtakes class 1 again.
  8. As σ→0, x∗→10+200=24.14. For σ=1,2,3,4, x∗=24.19,24.34,24.58,24.91. All are below 25, so closer to 20. Answer: (a).
  9. Check the direction with intuition. Class 2 is wider, so its density falls off more slowly and is still large well away from 30. It wins over a longer stretch, which pushes the meeting point toward 20.
Where the key needs a correction

The key's formula and its table are right. Its comment on large σ is not quite right, and the answer really depends on σ.

  • x∗=25 when 200+2σ2ln⁡2=15, that is 2σ2ln⁡2=25, so σ=25/(2ln⁡2)≈4.25. For σ>4.25 the threshold is closer to 30, so (a) is only true for σ<4.25.
  • The key says that beyond 4.25 the means are "separated by less than one standard deviation". At σ=4.25 the means are 10 apart, which is 10/4.25≈2.4 standard deviations of class 1. So that remark is wrong. The real reason the threshold moves right is the −12ln⁡2 term. For large σ the narrow class has the taller peak by a factor of 2, and that matters more than the means.
  • The threshold reaches 30 when 200+2σ2ln⁡2=20, which gives σ≈12.0. This matches the key's remark that its option (d) needs σ>12.

In the exam, assume σ is small compared with the gap of 10, choose (a), and if there is room, write one line saying it holds for σ<4.25.

Marks
3 for (a). 1 for "closer to 30" with a reason about the variances. 0 for the other options. No other partial credit.
Mid-sem 2026Q1.10 (3 points). (a) Write the objective function of k-means. (b) Is k-means guaranteed to reach a unique global optimum?›
Formula
J=∑k=1K∑𝐱i∈Ck‖𝐱i−𝝁k‖2 with 𝝁k=1|Ck|∑𝐱i∈Ck𝐱i.
Where it comes from
(a) 2.8, the objective J. (b) 2.8, box "Show that each k-means step can only lower (or keep) J …", lines 2 to 4, and the paragraph on starting means after it
Working
  1. (a) Ck is the set of points in cluster k and 𝝁k is their mean. J adds the squared distance of every point to the mean of its own cluster. It is called the within-cluster sum of squares.
  2. (b) No. The objective is not convex, because which cluster each point belongs to is a discrete choice. Each step of k-means can only lower J, so the algorithm always stops, but it stops at a local minimum that depends on the starting clusters.
  3. Check with an example. Section 2.8 runs the same 2-D data from two starts and gets J=7.875 and J=10.67. Two different stopping points show that the answer is not unique.
Marks
(a) 2 for the double sum with the squared distance and the cluster notation. 1 if the square on the norm is missing. (b) 1 for "no" with "non-convex, local minimum, depends on initialisation". 0.5 for "no" alone. 0 for "yes".

Mid-sem 2026Q2.1 (7.5 points). Bag I has w1 white and b1 black balls. Bag II has w2 white and b2 black balls. A bag is picked at random and a ball drawn from it is white. What is the probability that it came from Bag I?›
Formula
Bayes' theorem, P(I|W)=P(W|I)P(I)P(W), with the total probability P(W)=P(W|I)P(I)+P(W|II)P(II).
Where it comes from
B.8, box "Derive Bayes' theorem", line 3 for the posterior and line 4 for the total probability P(W)
Working
  1. Priors. The bag is picked at random, so P(I)=P(II)=12.
  2. Likelihoods. Bag I has w1+b1 balls of which w1 are white, so P(W|I)=w1w1+b1. In the same way, P(W|II)=w2w2+b2.
  3. Total probability of white. P(W)=12⋅w1w1+b1+12⋅w2w2+b2.
  4. Apply Bayes. P(I|W)=12⋅w1w1+b112⋅w1w1+b1+12⋅w2w2+b2.
  5. Cancel the common 12 from the top and from both terms of the bottom. P(I|W)=w1w1+b1w1w1+b1+w2w2+b2.
  6. Check with numbers. If w1=3, b1=1, w2=1, b2=3, the answer is 3/43/4+1/4=0.75. Bag I is mostly white, so a white ball makes Bag I more likely than the prior 12, as expected. If the bags are identical, the answer is 12, so the ball tells you nothing, which is also right.
Marks
3 for stating every probability (priors, both likelihoods and the total). 2 for applying Bayes' formula correctly. 2.5 for the final simplified expression. Deductions are 1 for a missing or wrong prior and 2 for a wrong likelihood such as w1w1+w2. A final answer with no intermediate probabilities was capped at 4.
Mid-sem 2026Q2.2 (7.5 points). A kernel is κ(𝐩,𝐪)=ϕ(𝐩)𝖳ϕ(𝐪) and the kernel matrix has entries Kij=κ(𝐱i,𝐱j). Show that (a) K is symmetric and (b) K is positive semi-definite.›
Formula
Symmetric means Kij=Kji. Positive semi-definite (PSD) means 𝐜𝖳K𝐜≥0 for every 𝐜∈ℝN.
Where it comes from
(a) 2.12, box "Show that a kernel matrix Kij=ϕ(xi)𝖳ϕ(xj) is symmetric and positive semi-definite", line 1. (b) the same box, lines 2 to 5
Working
Write this in the exam

(a) K is symmetric

  1. Kij=ϕ(𝐱i)𝖳ϕ(𝐱j), a dot product of two real vectors.
  2. The dot product of real vectors does not depend on the order, so ϕ(𝐱i)𝖳ϕ(𝐱j)=ϕ(𝐱j)𝖳ϕ(𝐱i)=Kji.
  3. So Kij=Kji for all i,j, which means K=K𝖳.

(b) K is PSD

  1. Take any 𝐜=[c1,…,cN]𝖳. Then 𝐜𝖳K𝐜=∑i∑jcicjKij.
  2. Substitute Kij. 𝐜𝖳K𝐜=∑i∑jcicjϕ(𝐱i)𝖳ϕ(𝐱j).
  3. Let 𝐯=∑iciϕ(𝐱i). The double sum is 𝐯𝖳𝐯=‖𝐯‖2≥0.
    Why (4 steps)
    1. Expand 𝐯𝖳𝐯 with 𝐯=∑iciϕ(𝐱i). It is (∑iciϕ(𝐱i))𝖳(∑jcjϕ(𝐱j)). The second sum uses a new letter j so the two sums do not get mixed up.
    2. Multiplying two sums gives every pair of terms. So 𝐯𝖳𝐯=∑i∑jcicjϕ(𝐱i)𝖳ϕ(𝐱j), which is line 2.
    3. 𝐯𝖳𝐯 is the sum of the squares of the entries of 𝐯, which is its squared length ‖𝐯‖2.
    4. A sum of squares of real numbers is never negative. So 𝐜𝖳K𝐜≥0.
  4. This holds for every 𝐜, so K is PSD.

A shorter version of (b) puts the vectors ϕ(𝐱i)𝖳 as the rows of a matrix Φ. Then K=ΦΦ𝖳 and 𝐜𝖳K𝐜=‖Φ𝖳𝐜‖2≥0. The rubric gave only 2 of 4.5 for writing K=ΦΦ𝖳 without the 𝐜𝖳K𝐜 step, so write the last part out.

Check with numbers. Take ϕ(𝐱)=𝐱 and the points 1 and 2 in 1-D. Then K=[1224], which is symmetric. For 𝐜=[2,−1]𝖳, 𝐜𝖳K𝐜=4−8+4=0≥0, and 𝐯=2⋅1−1⋅2=0 agrees.

Marks
(a) is worth 3. Full marks need the reason (the dot product commutes). Stating Kij=Kji without the reason earned 2, and "K is symmetric" alone earned 0.5. (b) is worth 4.5 for the full argument ending in ‖⋅‖2≥0. Stopping after the expansion earned 2.5. Only saying "the eigenvalues are non-negative" earned 1.
Mid-sem 2026Q2.3 (7.5 points). f(w)=w2+2w+1, starting at w0=5 with learning rate η=0.1. (a) Find w1 and w2. (b) Suggest a better η. (c) Find the η that reaches the minimum in one step.›
Formula
wk+1=wk−ηf′(wk) with f′(w)=2w+2.
Where it comes from
(a) 1.7, box "Derive the gradient descent update", line 3, in one dimension. (b) and (c) 1.7, Reading the formula for the update (what a small or large η does).
Working
  1. Note that f(w)=(w+1)2, so the minimum is at w∗=−1, where f=0.
  2. (a) f′(5)=12. So w1=5−0.1×12=5−1.2= 3.8.
  3. f′(3.8)=9.6. So w2=3.8−0.1×9.6=3.8−0.96= 2.84.
  4. Find the pattern. Let e=w+1 be the distance from the minimum. One step gives w−η(2w+2)+1=(1−2η)(w+1). So each step multiplies the distance by (1−2η).
  5. Check (a) with the pattern. With η=0.1 the factor is 0.8. The distance goes 6→4.8→3.84, and −1+3.84=2.84.
  6. (b) Gradient descent gets closer faster when |1−2η| is smaller than 0.8, that is when 0.1<η<0.9. For example, η=0.3 gives factor 0.4. Then w1=5−0.3×12=1.4 and w2=1.4−0.3×4.8=−0.04, already close to −1.
  7. (c) For one step to land exactly on −1, the factor must be 0. So 1−2η=0, which gives η= 0.5.
  8. Check (c). w1=5−0.5×12=−1. This equals 1/f′′(w)=1/2, the Newton step for a quadratic.
A slip in the key

The key says any η in (0.1,1) converges faster. That is too wide. For 0.9≤η<1 the factor 1−2η lies between −1 and −0.8, so the steps overshoot back and forth and close in more slowly than with η=0.1. With η=0.95, for example, the distance goes 6→−5.4→4.86. The faster range is 0.1<η<0.9. Also, η=1 gives factor −1, so w jumps between 5 and −7 forever without diverging. It is only η>1 that diverges.

Marks
(a) 3 for both values with working, 2 if w2 has an arithmetic slip. (b) 1.5 for any valid η>0.1 with a reason or a numerical check, 1 without a reason, 0 for η≥1. (c) 3 for 0.5 with a derivation, 2 without working, 1.5 for the right set-up 5−12η=−1 with an arithmetic slip.

Working
  1. (i) Each y is one more than its x, since 2=1+1, 3=2+1, and so on. So y=x+1, and w1=1, w2=1, 𝐰=[1,1]𝖳.
  2. Check (i) with the closed form. With rows [xi,1], X~𝖳X~=[3010104] and X~𝖳𝐲=[40,14]𝖳.
  3. The determinant is 30⋅4−10⋅10=20. So (X~𝖳X~)−1=120[4−10−1030].

  5. (ii) The program, for X of shape (N,d) and 𝐲 of shape (N,).
import numpy as np
N, d = X.shape
X_aug = np.hstack([X, np.ones((N, 1))])   # add a column of ones, shape (N, d+1)
theta_aug = np.linalg.inv(X_aug.T @ X_aug) @ X_aug.T @ y   # (d+1, d+1) @ (d+1, N) @ (N,) gives (d+1,)
theta, b = theta_aug[:d], theta_aug[d]    # the first d entries are the weights, the last is the bias
  1. np.hstack puts the column of ones to the right of X. Each row becomes [𝐱i𝖳,1], so the last weight multiplies 1 and acts as the bias b.
  2. X_aug.T @ X_aug is (d+1)×(d+1). Its inverse times X_aug.T @ y is the normal-equation solution from Section 1.6.
  3. np.linalg.lstsq(X_aug, y) or np.linalg.solve(X_aug.T @ X_aug, X_aug.T @ y) gives the same answer and is more stable than forming the inverse. The rubric accepted lstsq.
  4. A gradient descent version was also accepted. It starts from w = np.zeros(d + 1) and repeats grad = -(2.0 / N) * (X_aug.T @ (y - X_aug @ w)) then w = w - eta * grad, which is the update of Q1.5.

Formula

Mid-sem 2026Q3.2 (15 points). You have 200 face images of size 100×100. The first 100 are person "A" and the next 100 are person "B". Write Python (numpy only) that uses PCA (eigenfaces) to reduce the dimension and then classifies a test image Q as "A" or "B" with k-NN, K=5. Cover (i) the mean and covariance, (ii) the eigenvectors and how many to keep, (iii) the projection, (iv) the k-NN step.›
Formula
𝝁=1N∑i𝐱i, X‾=X−𝝁. Eigenvectors of 1NX‾X‾𝖳 (200×200) give those of 1NX‾𝖳X‾ through 𝐮=X‾𝖳𝐯. Project with 𝐳=W𝖳(𝐱−𝝁).
Where it comes from
(i) 1.12, box "Run PCA by hand on a few 2-D points", lines 1 and 2. (ii) 1.13, box "Get the eigenvectors of 𝐗𝐗𝖳 from those of 𝐗𝖳𝐗", lines 1 to 4, and the 95% rule in 1.12. (iii) 1.12, box "Run PCA by hand on a few 2-D points", line 5. (iv) 1.2, box "Classify a test point with k-NN and find the accuracy", lines 1 to 4.
Working

Each image is flattened to a vector of 100×100=10000 pixels. Stack them as rows of X, which is 200×10000.

(i) Mean and covariance

import numpy as np
N = X.shape[0]                       # X: (200, 10000), one flattened image per row
mu = np.mean(X, axis=0)              # mean face, (10000,)
Xc = X - mu                          # centred data, (200, 10000)
C_small = (1.0 / N) * Xc @ Xc.T      # (200, 200) instead of the (10000, 10000) covariance
  1. The real covariance is C=1NX‾𝖳X‾, of size 10000×10000, which has 108 entries and is slow to decompose.
  2. X‾X‾𝖳 is only 200×200. If X‾X‾𝖳𝐯=λ𝐯, multiply both sides on the left by X‾𝖳. This gives X‾𝖳X‾(X‾𝖳𝐯)=λ(X‾𝖳𝐯). So 𝐮=X‾𝖳𝐯 is an eigenvector of the big matrix with the same eigenvalue.

(ii) Eigenvectors and how many to keep

vals, V = np.linalg.eigh(C_small)            # eigh is for symmetric matrices, eigenvalues come out ascending
order = np.argsort(vals)[::-1]               # indices from largest to smallest
vals, V = vals[order], V[:, order]
U = Xc.T @ V                                 # (10000, 200), each column is an eigenvector of the big matrix
U = U / np.linalg.norm(U, axis=0, keepdims=True)   # scale each column to length 1
ratio = np.cumsum(vals) / np.sum(vals)       # fraction of variance kept by the top k
d = np.searchsorted(ratio, 0.95) + 1         # smallest k with ratio at least 0.95
W = U[:, :d]                                 # (10000, d)
  1. The eigenvectors X‾𝖳𝐯 do not have length 1, so they are normalised. PCA needs unit-length directions.
  2. np.cumsum(vals) / np.sum(vals) is the fraction of the total variance kept by the top 1, 2, 3, … components. searchsorted finds the first position where it reaches 0.95, and adding 1 turns the position into a count.
  3. Centred data of 200 images has rank at most 199, so the smallest eigenvalue is 0 up to rounding. Its 𝐮 is a zero vector, and normalising it divides by almost 0. It is never among the top d, so it does no harm, but numpy may print a warning.

(iii) Projection

Z = Xc @ W                                   # (200, 10000) @ (10000, d) gives (200, d)

Row i of Z is 𝐳i𝖳=(𝐱i−𝝁)𝖳W, which is the transpose of 𝐳i=W𝖳(𝐱i−𝝁). Each face is now described by d numbers instead of 10000.

(iv) k-NN for the test image

q = Q.flatten()                              # (10000,)
zq = (q - mu) @ W                            # centre with the TRAINING mean, then project: (d,)
dist = np.linalg.norm(Z - zq, axis=1)        # distance to each of the 200 training faces, (200,)
nearest = np.argsort(dist)[:5]               # indices of the 5 closest
labels = np.array([0] * 100 + [1] * 100)     # 0 means "A", 1 means "B"
votes_A = np.sum(labels[nearest] == 0)
prediction = 'A' if votes_A > 2 else 'B'     # majority of 5
  1. The test image is centred with the training mean, the same 𝝁 as the training faces. Computing a new mean from Q alone would give Q minus itself, which is zero.
  2. With K=5 and two classes a tie is impossible, so "more than 2 votes for A" is a full majority rule.
  3. Check the shapes. Z - zq broadcasts (200,d) minus (d,) to (200,d), and the norm along axis=1 gives one distance per training face.
Marks
(i) 3: 1.5 for the mean and centring, 1.5 for the covariance or the small-matrix trick. (ii) 4: 1.5 for the eigen decomposition, 1 for converting the small eigenvectors, 1 for a rule to choose d, 0.5 for normalising. (iii) 3: 1.5 for 𝐳i=W𝖳𝐱‾i, 1.5 for code with the right shapes. (iv) 5: 1.5 for flattening, centring and projecting Q, 1 for distances in the reduced space, 1.5 for K=5 with a majority vote, 1 for clear code. Deductions are 1.5 for centring Q with anything other than the training mean, 2 for running k-NN in the original 10000-dimensional space, 1 for W𝐱 in place of W𝖳𝐱, 1 for pseudocode, 0.5 for not flattening, and 1 per shape error.

M.4What the marking shows

Across the 16 rubrics, partial credit followed a few rules. Knowing them is worth a few marks on the day.

  • Marks are split into named pieces (each probability in Q2.1, each step of the PCA pipeline). Writing each piece on its own line makes it easy for the grader to give you every piece you got right.
  • Reasons are marked separately from answers. "K is symmetric" with no reason earned 0.5 of 3, and "no" without "non-convex, local minimum" earned 0.5 of 1.
  • Sign errors cost more than missing constants. A wrong sign in gradient descent earned 1 of 3, and a missing factor of 2 earned 2.5.
  • Code is read for shapes. Most code deductions were for wrong dimensions, missing the bias column, or centring with the wrong mean. Comment the shape of each array, as in the answers above.
  • Multiple-choice and true-or-false questions gave little or no partial credit. Spend the saved time checking them.
  • When a question has a trap (an empty cluster, a conflicting request), noticing it and saying what you did about it earned the marks. Stopping did not.
