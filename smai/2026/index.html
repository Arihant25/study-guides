SMAI 2026 prep · Start here

Get ahead on SMAI before the semester starts

This guide covers last year's run of Statistical Methods in AI, all 26 lectures from January to April 2026. Read this page first (about 15 minutes). It explains how the course works, what to revise and in what order to read the other pages.

Read this first

SMAI is the first machine learning course at IIIT Hyderabad. In Spring 2026 it was taught by C. V. Jawahar, and the slides call it "a fundamental course on machine learning, not an advanced course". It has two halves. The first teaches classical machine learning through linear algebra and probability, with nearest neighbours, regression, PCA, Bayesian decisions and SVMs. The second teaches neural networks, from a single perceptron up to transformers and self-supervised learning.

What this guide is based on. Everything here comes from the 2026 Moodle page. That means the 26 lecture slide decks, the Quiz 1 paper with its official solutions and marking rubric, the tutorial on probability and GMMs, the course's list of short videos, and the announcements. The TAs' answer keys and marking rubrics for the mid-sem, Quiz 2 and the end-sem were not on Moodle but were shared by a student who took the course, so all four 2026 exams are solved here. Questions written for this guide are labelled "Practice".

What may change. The 2027 course may move topics around, change the weights or skip a topic. A new guide built from the 2027 slides will replace this one once they are uploaded. The ideas themselves (regression, PCA, Bayes, backpropagation, attention) will not change, so reading ahead now still pays off.

The guide has five teaching pages, two solved exam papers and a formula sheet. Read the teaching pages in order, because each one assumes only the pages before it. Keep the exam papers for after the pages they test, and do them timed.

How to use each page

Every topic follows the same pattern.

  1. A plain explanation of the idea and why the course cares about it, with the slides that matter shown next to the text. The line under each heading lists the lecture it comes from and the course's short video on it, if there is one.
  2. Each important formula, followed by a box headed "Reading the formula". It says what every symbol means and works a small example with real numbers.
  3. A box headed "Write this in the exam" for each derivation. Its numbered lines are what you would write on paper. Under a line, the small "Why" link opens the reasoning one step at a time. Read the lines first to see the shape of the proof, then open the explanations you need.
  4. A "Remember" box with the results to keep.
  5. Questions. Try each one on paper before opening the answer. Every worked answer has three rows. They give the formula to use, where it comes from on the page, and the working with a check at the end.

Questions are labelled by where they come from. "Quiz 1, 2026", "Quiz 2, 2026", "Mid-sem 2026" and "End-sem 2026" are the real papers. Their answers add a fourth row with the TAs' marking scheme. "L05 slide" is a problem the instructor put on that lecture's slides, which is the style of the in-class problem solving. "Practice" means the question was written for this guide.

Two things stand out. First, 20% of the grade is problem solving done in class, so you have to keep up week by week. That is the main reason to read ahead. The exams

Quiz 1

Covers L01 to L07, roughly. It had 45 points across 12 questions (ten worth 3 points and two worth 7.5), in four sets with different numbers. The questions were short. They asked you to compute some norms, pick which eigenvector solves an optimisation, mark the true statements about eigenfaces, compute precision and recall, run 1-NN by hand, fill in blanks about the SVD, mean-centre some points, write a loss function, do two steps of gradient descent, and fit a line with the closed-form formula. The class average was 29.67 out of 45. The whole paper is solved on Page 1.

Mid-sem

Taken in early March, after L14 (the exact date is not on Moodle). It had 90 points in three parts of 30 minutes each. Part 1 had ten short answers worth 3 points each, Part 2 had four precise answers worth 7.5, and Part 3 had two detailed answers worth 15. It covered L01 to L13, with Python code expected in some answers. The class average was 44.98, and the announcement did not give the scale. The paper is solved on the Mid-sem 2026 page.

Quiz 2

The L18 slides list its scope as an overview of neural networks, neuron models, the single layer perceptron, the multi-layer perceptron, gradient descent, backpropagation and second order methods. That is exactly Page 3. The paper had 45 points in three questions, in four variants. It is solved at the end of Page 3.

End-sem

The 2026 end-sem had 180 marks in three hours and covered the whole course. Part I had ten objective questions worth 6 marks each. Parts II and III each asked for three out of four questions worth 20 marks each. The format is close to the 2025 one shown in L26. The paper is solved on the End-sem 2026 page.

"One, none or more correct"

Many slide questions and the Quiz 1 statement questions say that any number of options can be correct, including none. Quiz 1 gave 0 marks if any wrong option was ticked, half marks for at least half of the right ones, and full marks for all of them. So check each option on its own as true or false, and do not tick an option you are unsure of.

What to take from this
  • Start each assignment the day it comes out. They are long (Assignment 2 listed 28 hours), and the deadline fell when many people were ill.
  • You may have to explain your assignment code out loud, so write it yourself and understand every line.
  • The course is front-loaded with linear algebra. Quiz 1 comes four weeks in, so the maths basics and Page 1 are the best use of time before the semester starts.

What you need before you start

From L01, "Getting ready".

The slides ask for typical engineering maths and nothing advanced, meaning linear algebra, probability and differential calculus. They also ask that you be comfortable with Python and numpy, because programming is not taught. The assignments are in Python with PyTorch later on, and no heavy computation is planned, so a laptop is enough.

Check yourself on these. If any of them is shaky, the maths basics page teaches it from the start.

  • Multiply two matrices, transpose a product, and find the inverse of a 2×2 matrix.
  • Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 matrix by hand.
  • Say what the rank of a matrix is and find it for a small one.
  • Use Bayes' theorem on a medical-test problem.
  • Write the density of a normal distribution and say what each symbol means.
  • Differentiate 11+e−z and use the chain rule through three functions.
  • In numpy, compute a mean, a covariance matrix, a matrix inverse and an eigen decomposition.

The course recommends one textbook, Mathematics for Machine Learning by Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong (free PDF). Chapters 2 (linear algebra) and 4 (matrix decompositions) are named in L05. Chapter 6 covers probability.

A five-week plan before the semester

The semester starts in early January, so there are about five weeks from now. Two hours a day on weekdays fits the whole guide. If you have less time, stop after week 2.
