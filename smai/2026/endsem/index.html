SMAI 2026 prep · Past paper

End-sem 2026, solved

The final exam of the Spring 2026 course, rebuilt from the TAs' answer key and grading rubric. It has 18 questions over three parts and is worth 180 marks. Sit it as a three-hour timed paper first, then spend about three hours on the answers. Budget roughly 6 hours in total.

E.1Read this first

The end-sem was held in late April 2026, a few days after the last lecture (L26, 23 April). It lasted three hours and was worth 180 marks, which the final grading scheme scaled to 21% of the course. It examined the whole course, from k-NN and regression to residual networks and attention.

PartWhat it asksMarksTime
ITen objective questions of 6 marks each. True or false, fill in the blank, short numbers, and multiple-correct questions.6060 min
IIAnswer three of four numerical questions, 20 marks each.6060 min
IIIAnswer three of four analytical questions, 20 marks each.6060 min

This is the same shape as the 2025 final shown on the L26 slide, which also had three 60-mark parts of 60 minutes each. The difference is that in 2026 Parts II and III let you drop one question each. See section 4.16 for how to prepare for each kind of part.

What this page is based on. A friend shared the TAs' answer key with its grading rubric. The key restates each question in a line or two and then gives the answer, so the questions below are rebuilt from it with every number kept. For multiple-correct questions the key lists only the correct options and one reason per option, so the wording of each option here is rebuilt from those reasons. The real paper may have phrased them differently.

A muddle in the key. The key's own summary sheet at the end lists the questions in a different order from the body, and its notes column has slipped by a row or two. For example, it labels Part II Q4 as "Backprop on single neuron" and Part III Q1 as "Eigen decomposition + SVD". The body of the key is complete and consistent, so this page follows the body. Every question that the summary mentions (multi-head attention, 1-D k-means, the SLP and MLP parameter count) does appear in the body.

A second muddle. Part II Q1 is headed 20 marks but its parts add up to 3 + 10 + 8 = 21. Treat it as 20.

How multiple-correct questions were marked

The key states one strict rule for every multiple-correct question. You get full marks only if you tick all the correct options and nothing else. You get half marks if you tick at least half of the correct options (rounded up) and nothing wrong. Ticking even one wrong option gives zero. So for three correct options you need at least two of them, with no wrong one, to score anything.

True-or-false parts are marked separately and are not covered by this rule. A correct T or F gets full marks even without a reason, unless a reason is asked for.

E.2How to use this paper

  1. Print or copy out the questions only. Set a timer for 60 minutes per part and do not open any answer.
  2. In Parts II and III, spend the first two minutes choosing your three questions. Pick the ones where you can write the setup at once. In Part II, Q2 (Bayes) and Q4 (k-means) are pure arithmetic and the safest. In Part III, Q2 (kernel matrix) and Q3 (residual block) are short proofs that the course practised, so most people would drop Q1 or Q4.
  3. Mark your work with the "Marks" row under each answer. It says where the TAs gave and took away marks.
  4. For every question you lost marks on, follow the "Where it comes from" link and redo the questions in that section.

E.3Part I · Objective questions (60 marks)

Ten questions of 6 marks. Aim for six minutes each.

Q1 · IPL wicket prediction (2 + 2 + 2)

A classifier predicts whether the next ball in an IPL match will take a wicket. On a test set of 1000 balls there were 15 actual wickets. The model flagged 20 balls as "wicket-likely", and 12 of those really were wickets.

  1. Write the confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN).
  2. Find the precision and the recall.
  3. Which of these are valid criticisms of judging this model by accuracy alone? (i) Wickets are rare, so a model that never predicts a wicket already scores 98.5% accuracy. (ii) Accuracy hides how many wickets are missed, which recall shows. (iii) Accuracy hides how many flagged balls are false alarms, which precision shows. (iv) A confusion matrix is not useful when the classes are imbalanced.
End-sem 2026Part I Q1 · confusion matrix, precision and recall for the wicket predictor›
Formula
precision=TPTP+FP, recall=TPTP+FN.
Where it comes from
1.5 Performance metrics
Working
  1. A true positive is a flagged ball that was a wicket. TP=12.
  2. A false positive is a flagged ball that was not a wicket. FP=20−12=8.
  3. A false negative is a wicket the model did not flag. FN=15−12=3.
  4. Everything else is a true negative. TN=1000−12−8−3=977.
  5. Precision is 1212+8=1220=0.6.
  6. Recall is 1212+3=1215=0.8.
  7. For (c), option (i) is true. Predicting "no wicket" every time gets 985/1000=98.5% right. Options (ii) and (iii) are true for the reasons they give. Option (iv) is false, because the confusion matrix is exactly the tool that shows what accuracy hides. The answer is (i), (ii), (iii).
  8. Check. 12+8+3+977=1000, so every ball is counted once.
Marks
(a) half a mark for each cell, full marks if any three are right because the fourth follows. Swapping FP and FN still earned 1 mark. (b) 1 mark each, with carry-forward if (a) was wrong. (c) 2 marks for all three correct options, 1 mark for exactly two of them, 0 if (iv) is ticked.

Q2 · Linear regression for strike rate (2 + 2 + 2)

A linear model Xw≈y predicts a batter's strike rate from features in the rows of X.

  1. True or false? The closed form w=(X𝖳X)−1X𝖳y can always be used.
  2. Write the gradient of J(w)=1N‖Xw−y‖2.
  3. Which are true? (i) Ridge regression has the closed form w=(X𝖳X+λI)−1X𝖳y. (ii) Ridge regression sets irrelevant weights exactly to zero. (iii) Adding λI makes the matrix better conditioned and always invertible for λ>0. (iv) Ridge gives sparser weights than lasso.
End-sem 2026Part I Q2 · when the closed form fails, the MSE gradient, ridge versus lasso›
Formula
∇J=2NX𝖳(Xw−y). Ridge gives w=(X𝖳X+λI)−1X𝖳y.
Where it comes from
1.6 Closed-form regression for (a) and (b). 1.8 Ridge and lasso for (c).
Working
  1. (a) False. The formula needs X𝖳X to be invertible. It is singular when two features are copies or combinations of each other, or when there are fewer samples than features. Then there are many least-squares solutions, and you would use the pseudo-inverse or ridge.
  2. (b) Expand J=1N(w𝖳X𝖳Xw−2w𝖳X𝖳y+y𝖳y).
  3. Use ∇(w𝖳Aw)=2Aw for symmetric A, and ∇(w𝖳a)=a. This gives ∇J=1N(2X𝖳Xw−2X𝖳y).
  4. Take out 2X𝖳. So ∇J=2NX𝖳(Xw−y).
  5. (c) (i) is true, it is the ridge formula. (ii) is false, because it is lasso (the L1 penalty) that sets weights exactly to zero. (iii) is true, because X𝖳X+λI has eigenvalues λi+λ>0. (iv) is false, lasso is the sparser one. The answer is (i), (iii).
  6. Check (b) in one dimension. With one feature, J=1N∑(wxi−yi)2, and dJdw=2N∑xi(wxi−yi), which matches.
Marks
(a) 2 for False. Answering True but explaining the pseudo-inverse earned 1. (b) any of 2NX𝖳(Xw−y), X𝖳(Xw−y), 1NX𝖳(Xw−y) or −2NX𝖳(y−Xw) got full marks. Right shape with a wrong constant got 1, only the right sign got half. (c) 2 for both (i) and (iii) with nothing wrong, 1 for one of them.

Q3 · PCA and eigenfaces (2 + 2 + 2)

There are N=400 face images of size 100×100, flattened into the rows of a mean-subtracted data matrix X.

  1. What is the size of the covariance matrix Σ=1NX𝖳X, and what is the largest its rank can be?
  2. True or false? The non-zero eigenvalues of X𝖳X and XX𝖳 are the same, so you can find the eigenfaces from the smaller matrix.
  3. Which are true? (i) PCA is unsupervised and linear. (ii) Maximising the variance kept is the same as minimising the reconstruction error. (iii) PCA uses the class labels, like LDA. (iv) The covariance matrix is symmetric and positive semi-definite.
End-sem 2026Part I Q3 · covariance size and rank, the small-matrix trick, facts about PCA›
Formula
rank⁡(X𝖳X)=rank⁡(X). If XX𝖳u=λu then X𝖳X(X𝖳u)=λ(X𝖳u).
Where it comes from
1.12 PCA and 1.13 Eigenfaces and the small-matrix trick. For (c)(ii) see 2.1 PCA as reconstruction.
Working
  1. Each image has 100⋅100=10000 pixels, so X is 400×10000.
  2. Σ=1NX𝖳X is (10000×400)(400×10000), which is 10000×10000.
  3. Its rank equals the rank of X, which is at most min⁡(400,10000)=400.
  4. Subtracting the mean makes the 400 rows add up to the zero vector. So one row is minus the sum of the others, and the rank is at most 400−1=399.
  5. (b) True. Multiply XX𝖳u=λu on the left by X𝖳. That gives X𝖳X(X𝖳u)=λ(X𝖳u), so X𝖳u is an eigenvector of the big matrix with the same λ. The 400×400 problem is far cheaper.
  6. (c) (i) true. (ii) true, this is the reconstruction view of PCA. (iii) false, PCA never sees labels. (iv) true, z𝖳Σz=1N‖Xz‖2≥0. The answer is (i), (ii), (iv).
Marks
(a) 1 for the size, 1 for the rank. Both 399 and 400 were accepted. (b) full marks for True, even with no reason. (c) 2 for all of (i), (ii), (iv), 1 for exactly two, 0 if (iii) is ticked.

Q4 · Bayesian decision in a classroom (4 + 2)

Two classes have priors P(ω1)=0.7 and P(ω2)=0.3. For an observed x the likelihoods are p(x|ω1)=0.4 and p(x|ω2)=0.6.

  1. Find P(ω1|x).
  2. True or false? Choosing the class with the larger posterior gives the smallest possible probability of error.
End-sem 2026Part I Q4 · one posterior with Bayes' rule›
Formula
P(ω1|x)=p(x|ω1)P(ω1)p(x|ω1)P(ω1)+p(x|ω2)P(ω2)
Where it comes from
B.8 Bayes' theorem and 2.5 Bayesian decision
Working
  1. The top is 0.4×0.7=0.28.
  2. The second term of the bottom is 0.6×0.3=0.18.
  3. The bottom is 0.28+0.18=0.46.
  4. So P(ω1|x)=0.280.46=1423≈0.609.
  5. (b) True. This is why the Bayes classifier is called optimal.
  6. Check. P(ω2|x)=0.18/0.46≈0.391, and the two add up to 1. Class 1 still wins even though its likelihood is smaller, because its prior is much larger.
Marks
(a) 2 for the formula, 1 for the set-up, 1 for the number. Anything from 0.60 to 0.61 was accepted. Right formula with wrong arithmetic got 3. (b) 2 for True.

Q5 · Naive Bayes and logistic regression (2 + 2 + 2)

  1. True or false? Naive Bayes assumes the features are conditionally independent given the class.
  2. Write p(y=1|x) for logistic regression and name the loss usually used to train it.
  3. Which are true? (i) Training logistic regression with the squared error (σ(w𝖳x)−y)2 gives a non-convex loss. (ii) With the squared error, a saturated sigmoid makes the gradient vanish. (iii) Cross-entropy is the maximum likelihood loss for a Bernoulli label. (iv) Logistic regression always violates Bayes optimality.
End-sem 2026Part I Q5 · the naive assumption, the logistic model and its loss›
Formula
p(y=1|x)=σ(w𝖳x)=11+e−w𝖳x. L=−1N∑i[yiln⁡σ(w𝖳xi)+(1−yi)ln⁡(1−σ(w𝖳xi))].
Where it comes from
2.9 Naive Bayes and 2.10 Logistic regression
Working
  1. (a) True. That one assumption is what makes the method "naive".
  2. (b) The model is the sigmoid of a linear score, as in the formula row. The loss is the binary cross-entropy, also called the log-loss or the negative log-likelihood.
  3. (c) (i) true, the L11 slides ask you to check this. (ii) true, the squared-error gradient carries a factor σ′(z), which is near 0 when |z| is large. (iii) true, page 2 derives it. (iv) false, "always" is too strong. If the true posterior really is a sigmoid of a linear score, logistic regression is Bayes optimal. The answer is (i), (ii), (iii).
Marks
(a) 2 for True. (b) 1 for the sigmoid, 1 for any correct name of the loss. (c) 2 for all three correct options, 1 for exactly two, 0 if (iv) is ticked.

Q6 · SVMs and kernels (3 + 3)

  1. Which are true? (i) In the dual solution only the support vectors have αi>0. (ii) The margin is 2‖w‖. (iii) The dual uses the data only through dot products xi𝖳xj, which is what allows kernels. (iv) Replacing the constraints by a hinge loss alone, with no C and no slack variables, gives exactly the soft-margin SVM.
  2. True or false? A larger C gives a narrower margin.
End-sem 2026Part I Q6 · support vectors, margin, kernels, the effect of C›
Formula
The soft-margin SVM solves min⁡12‖w‖2+C∑iξi such that yi(w𝖳xi+b)≥1−ξi, ξi≥0.
Where it comes from
2.11 SVMs and 2.12 Kernels
Working
  1. (i) True. A point strictly outside the margin has αi=0, so it does not affect w=∑iαiyixi.
  2. (ii) True. The two planes w𝖳x+b=±1 are 2‖w‖ apart.
  3. (iii) True. Replace each xi𝖳xj by K(xi,xj) and nothing else changes.
  4. (iv) The key marks this false. The soft-margin primal is 12‖w‖2+C∑imax⁡(0,1−yi(w𝖳xi+b)), so the hinge loss needs the C and the ‖w‖2 term with it. The key admits that some books treat the two as the same.
  5. (b) True. C is the price of each violation. A larger price forces fewer violations, which means a narrower margin, closer to the hard-margin SVM.
Marks
(a) 3 for (i), (ii), (iii), 1.5 for exactly two of them. Because (iv) is genuinely ambiguous, ticking all four also got 3. This was the only exception to the strict rule in the paper. (b) 3 for True, at most 1 off for a wrong explanation.

Q7 · From SLP to MLP (3 + 3)

  1. Which are true? (i) The perceptron rule finds a separating line in finitely many steps if the data are linearly separable. (ii) A single layer perceptron can learn XOR. (iii) The delta rule converges, with a small enough learning rate, even when the data are noisy and not separable. (iv) A single layer perceptron is a universal approximator.
  2. An MLP has 3 inputs, one hidden layer of 2 sigmoid neurons and 1 linear output. Every neuron has a bias. How many learnable parameters does it have?
End-sem 2026Part I Q7 · perceptron facts and a parameter count›
Formula
A dense layer has (inputs + 1) × outputs parameters.
Where it comes from
3.2 The perceptron, 3.3 The delta rule and 3.4 MLPs. This is the L15 slide problem.
Working
  1. (i) true, this is the perceptron convergence theorem. (ii) false, XOR is not linearly separable. (iii) true, the delta rule is gradient descent on a convex squared error, so it settles at the least-squares weights. (iv) false, it is an MLP with a hidden layer that is a universal approximator. The answer is (i), (iii).
  2. (b) Input to hidden. Each of the 2 hidden neurons has 3 weights and 1 bias, so 2×(3+1)=8.
  3. Hidden to output. The output neuron has 2 weights and 1 bias, so 1×(2+1)=3.
  4. The total is 8+3=11.
Marks
(a) 3 for both (i) and (iii), 1.5 for one of them, 0 if (ii) or (iv) is ticked. (b) 3 for 11. Answers of 8 or 10 (a bias forgotten) got 2, and 6 (all biases forgotten) got 1.

Q8 · Optimisers, BatchNorm and dropout (2 + 2 + 2)

  1. True or false? Adam combines momentum with a separate adaptive learning rate for each parameter.
  2. Which help against vanishing gradients? (i) ReLU activations. (ii) Skip connections. (iii) Batch normalisation. (iv) Initialising all weights to zero.
  3. What did the original BatchNorm paper say it was for? (i) Reducing internal covariate shift. (ii) Reducing the number of parameters. (iii) Replacing dropout. (iv) Making the loss convex.
End-sem 2026Part I Q8 · Adam, fixes for vanishing gradients, why BatchNorm›
Formula
Adam keeps mt=β1mt−1+(1−β1)gt and vt=β2vt−1+(1−β2)gt2, corrects them by 1−βt, and steps by ηmˆt/(vˆt+ϵ).
Where it comes from
3.8 Optimisers, 3.7 Training in practice, 3.5 Activations and 4.6 ResNet
Working
  1. (a) True. mt is the momentum part and vt scales the step for each weight separately.
  2. (b) (i) helps, because ReLU's slope is 1 wherever it is active. (ii) helps, because the skip adds a path with derivative 1. (iii) helps, because it keeps pre-activations in the range where sigmoid and tanh are not flat. (iv) does not, zero weights make every hidden unit get the same gradient so they never become different. The answer is (i), (ii), (iii).
  3. (c) Only (i). Ioffe and Szegedy (2015) introduced BatchNorm to reduce internal covariate shift, meaning the change in each layer's input distribution while earlier layers train.
Marks
(a) 2 for True. (b) 2 for all three, 1 for exactly two, 0 if (iv) is ticked. (c) single correct option, so 2 or 0.

Q9 · 2-D convolution arithmetic (2 + 2 + 2)

The input is 224×224×1. A convolution layer has 32 filters of size 7×7, stride 2 and padding 3.

  1. What is the output size?
  2. How many learnable parameters does the layer have?
  3. A 2×2 max-pool with stride 2 follows. What is its output size?
End-sem 2026Part I Q9 · output size, parameters and pooling for a 7×7 conv layer›
Formula
Output side ⌊(n+2p−k)/s⌋+1. Parameters (k⋅k⋅D+1)⋅K.
Where it comes from
4.3 Output size and 4.4 Counting parameters
Working
  1. Put in n=224, p=3, k=7, s=2. The top is 224+6−7=223.
  2. 223/2=111.5, and the floor is 111.
  3. Add 1 to get 112. Each filter gives one channel, so the output is 112×112×32.
  4. (b) Each filter has 7⋅7⋅1=49 weights and 1 bias, which is 50.
  5. For 32 filters that is 32×50=1600.
  6. (c) Pooling halves each side, 112/2=56, and keeps the channels. The output is 56×56×32.
  7. Check (a). Padding 3 with a 7×7 filter is "same" padding, (7−1)/2=3. With stride 1 the output would be 224, and stride 2 halves it to 112.
Marks
(a) 1.5 for 112×112, 0.5 for 32 channels. Forgetting the padding gives 109, which earned half a mark for method. (b) 2 for 1600, 1.5 for 1568 (biases forgotten). (c) 1 for 56×56, 1 for keeping 32 channels.

Q10 · Multi-head self-attention (2 + 2 + 2)

A multi-head self-attention layer has 8 heads. The input and output dimension is d=100, there are N=1000 tokens, and each head's queries, keys and values have dimension 50.

  1. How many weight matrices does the layer have?
  2. What is the size of each?
  3. How many learnable parameters are there, ignoring biases?
End-sem 2026Part I Q10 · counting the matrices and parameters of multi-head attention›
Formula
Each head i has WQ(i),WK(i),WV(i) of size d×dk. The output projection WO is (h⋅dk)×d.
Where it comes from
4.11 Self-attention
Working
  1. (a) Each head has 3 matrices, so the heads have 8×3=24. The output projection adds 1. The total is 25.
  2. (b) Each query, key and value matrix maps 100 numbers to 50, so it is 100×50.
  3. Concatenating the 8 heads gives 8×50=400 numbers, and WO maps them back to 100. So WO is 400×100.
  4. (c) The 24 head matrices hold 24×100×50=120000 weights.
  5. WO holds 400×100=40000.
  6. The total is 120000+40000=160000.
  7. Note that N=1000 never appears. The number of tokens changes the cost of running the layer but not the number of weights, because the same matrices are applied to every token.
Marks
(a) 2 for 25, 1 for 24 (WO forgotten). Writing 4 because you stacked each of Q, K and V across heads into one matrix was also accepted. (b) 1 for the head matrices, 1 for WO, transposed shapes accepted. (c) 2 for 160 000, 1 for 120 000.

E.4Part II · Three out of four (60 marks)

Numerical questions of 20 marks. Choose three. Aim for 20 minutes each.

Q1 · Gradient descent on a single neuron (3 + 10 + 8)

One neuron computes o=σ(w1x1+w2x2+b) with σ(z)=11+e−z, and the loss is L=12(o−y)2. Start from w1=1, w2=−1, b=0. The sample is x=[1,1] with y=1, and the learning rate is η=1.

  1. Show that σ′(z)=σ(z)(1−σ(z)).
  2. Do the forward pass and find ∂L∂w1, ∂L∂w2 and ∂L∂b.
  3. Do one gradient descent step.
Write this in the exam

(a) Show that σ′(z)=σ(z)(1−σ(z)).

  1. σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1, so σ′(z)=e−z(1+e−z)2.
    Why (3 steps)
    1. Write σ as a power, σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1.
    2. The chain rule gives −1⋅(1+e−z)−2 times the derivative of the inside.
    3. The derivative of 1+e−z is −e−z. The two minus signs cancel, leaving e−z(1+e−z)2.
  2. σ′(z)=11+e−z⋅e−z1+e−z.
  3. e−z1+e−z=1−11+e−z=1−σ(z), so σ′(z)=σ(z)(1−σ(z)).
    Why (covers lines 2 and 3, 3 steps)
    1. Split the square in the bottom into two copies of 1+e−z and give one to each factor.
    2. The first factor is σ(z) by definition.
    3. Write e−z=(1+e−z)−1. Dividing by 1+e−z gives 1−11+e−z, which is 1−σ(z).
End-sem 2026Part II Q1 (b) and (c) · forward pass, three gradients and one update›
Formula
∂L∂wi=(o−y)σ′(z)xi and ∂L∂b=(o−y)σ′(z). Update w←w−η∇L.
Where it comes from
3.6 Backpropagation and B.12 The chain rule
Working
  1. z=1⋅1+(−1)⋅1+0=0.
  2. o=σ(0)=11+1=0.5.
  3. σ′(0)=0.5×(1−0.5)=0.25.
  4. ∂L∂o=o−y=0.5−1=−0.5.
  5. The chain rule goes L→o→z→w1. So ∂L∂w1=(−0.5)(0.25)(1)=−0.125.
  6. x2=1 too, so ∂L∂w2=−0.125.
  7. The bias acts like an input of 1, so ∂L∂b=−0.125.
  8. (c) w1←1−1×(−0.125)=1.125.
  9. w2←−1−1×(−0.125)=−0.875.
  10. b←0−1×(−0.125)=0.125.
  11. Check. The new z=1.125−0.875+0.125=0.375, so o=σ(0.375)≈0.593. That is closer to y=1 than 0.5 was, so the loss went down from 0.125 to about 0.083.
Marks
(a) 1.5 for a correct step, 1.5 for the final form. The result alone got 2. (b) 1 each for z, o and σ′(0), 2 for the chain-rule expression, 1 for the bias version, and the three numbers together were worth about 4. Writing y−o throughout was fine if you were consistent. (c) 2 for the update rule and 2 for each new value, capped at 6, with only 1 off for a carried sign error.

Q2 · Bayesian classification in one dimension (5 + 10 + 5)

Class A is 𝒩(100,100) and class B is 𝒩(200,100), so both have σ=10. The priors are P(A)=0.8 and P(B)=0.2.

  1. Write both likelihoods and evaluate them at x=150. Should they add up to 1?
  2. State Bayes' theorem and classify x=149,150,151. Should the pairs of values you compare add up to 1?
  3. Find the posteriors P(A|x) and P(B|x) at those points. Should they add up to 1?
End-sem 2026Part II Q2 · likelihoods, posteriors and decisions near the midpoint›
Formula
p(x|A)=1102πe−(x−100)2/200. Choose A if p(x|A)P(A)>p(x|B)P(B).
Where it comes from
B.9 The Gaussian and 2.5 Bayesian decision
Working
  1. (a) With σ2=100, the exponent of A is −(x−100)2200 and of B is −(x−200)2200.
  2. At x=150 both squares are 502=2500, so both exponents are −12.5.
  3. The constant is 1102π≈0.03989, and e−12.5≈3.727×10−6.
  4. So p(150|A)=p(150|B), and both are about 1.487×10−7.
  5. They do not add up to 1. Each is a density over x. It integrates to 1 over all x, but at one fixed x the two classes' densities have no reason to add up to 1.
  6. (b) Bayes' theorem is P(ω|x)=p(x|ω)P(ω)p(x). The bottom is the same for both classes, so compare p(x|ω)P(ω).
  7. Work with the ratio, because the constants cancel. p(x|A)P(A)p(x|B)P(B)=4e[(x−200)2−(x−100)2]/200.
  8. At 149 the squares are 512=2601 and 492=2401. The exponent is (2601−2401)/200=1, and the ratio is 4e≈10.87. Choose A.
  9. At 150 the exponent is 0, and the ratio is 4. Choose A.
  10. At 151 the squares swap, the exponent is −1, and the ratio is 4/e≈1.47. Still above 1, so choose A.
  11. The pairs p(x|ω)P(ω) do not add up to 1. Only after dividing by their sum do they become posteriors that do.
  12. (c) At 150, P(A|150)=0.80.8+0.2=0.8 and P(B|150)=0.2, because the equal likelihoods cancel.
  13. At 149, P(A|149)=10.8711.87≈0.916 and P(B|149)≈0.084.
  14. At 151, P(A|151)=1.472.47≈0.595 and P(B|151)≈0.405.
  15. Each pair adds up to 1, as posteriors over all the classes must.
  16. Check, and see why 151 still goes to A. With equal variances the boundary is where the ratio is 1, which is x∗=150+σ2ln⁡(P(A)/P(B))μB−μA=150+100ln⁡4100≈151.39. The larger prior of A pushes the boundary past 151.

(a) 2 for the densities, 2 for the value (within 10%, or left as 1102πe−12.5), 1 for "No" with a reason. (b) 2 for Bayes' theorem, 2 for each of the three decisions, 2 for the remark about the pairs. Classifying all three as A without the ratios still earned at least 5. (c) 3 for at least one correct pair of posteriors, 2 for saying they add up to 1.

Q4 · k-means in one dimension (4 + 6 + 10)

  1. Write the k-means objective.
  2. How does the minimum of the objective change as K grows? How would you choose K?
  3. Run k-means on {1,2,3,8,9,10} with K=2, starting from μ1=1 and μ2=2. Give the objective after each iteration.
End-sem 2026Part II Q4 · objective, the elbow method and a full run›
Formula
J=∑k=1K∑xi∈Ck‖xi−μk‖2
Where it comes from
2.8 k-means
Working
  1. (a) The objective is the formula above. It is minimised alternately over the clusters Ck and the means μk.
  2. (b) The best J never goes up as K grows, because with one more cluster you can always do at least as well. At K=N every point is its own cluster and J=0.
  3. So you cannot pick K by minimising J. Plot J against K and look for the elbow, the K after which J falls only slowly.
  4. (c) Iteration 1, assign. Every point except 1 is closer to 2, so C1={1} and C2={2,3,8,9,10}.
  5. Update. μ1=1 and μ2=32/5=6.4.
  6. J=0+4.42+3.42+1.62+2.62+3.62. That is 19.36+11.56+2.56+6.76+12.96, which is 53.2.
  7. Iteration 2, assign. 2 and 3 are now closer to 1 than to 6.4 (distances 1 and 2 against 4.4 and 3.4). So C1={1,2,3} and C2={8,9,10}.
  8. Update. μ1=2 and μ2=9. J=1+0+1+1+0+1=4.
  9. Iteration 3. With means 2 and 9 every point stays where it is, so the algorithm has converged.
  10. The answer is C1={1,2,3} with μ1=2, and C2={8,9,10} with μ2=9, and J=4.
  11. Check. J went 53.2, then 4, then 4. It never rose, as k-means guarantees.
Marks
(a) 2 for the double sum, 2 for the squared distance. (b) 2 for "non-increasing", 2 for the sketch, 2 for the elbow (silhouette or gap statistic also accepted). (c) 1 each for iteration 1's clusters, means and J, 2 for iteration 2's clusters and means, 2 for its J, 2 for showing iteration 3 changes nothing, 1 for the final answer. Skipping J cost only 2 in total.

Q1 · A wide network with four parallel branches (6 + 7 + 7)

The input is x∈ℝd. There are four branches k=1,2,3,4. Branch k has a weight vector wk∈ℝd and an output vector uk∈ℝp. It computes zk=wk𝖳x and αk=σ(zk), and it adds αkuk to the output.

  1. Write the output y (as a formula or in NumPy).
  2. When d=1, find ∂y∂x.
  3. For general d and p, find ∂y∂z2.
Reading the setup

This is a one-hidden-layer network drawn sideways. The four numbers z1,…,z4 are the hidden pre-activations, the αk are the hidden outputs, and the uk are the columns of the output weight matrix. Stacking the wk𝖳 as the rows of a 4×d matrix W and the uk as the columns of a p×4 matrix U gives y=Uσ(Wx).

Example. With d=1, x=1, all wk=0 and uk=[k] (so p=1), every αk=σ(0)=0.5, and y=0.5(1+2+3+4)=5.

End-sem 2026Part III Q1 · forward expression and two derivatives of a branch network›
Formula
y=∑k=14σ(wk𝖳x)uk∈ℝp, and σ′=σ(1−σ).
Where it comes from
3.4 MLPs and 3.6 Backpropagation
Working
  1. (a) Add up the four contributions. y=∑kαkuk=∑kσ(wk𝖳x)uk, a vector in ℝp. In NumPy, with W of shape (4, d) and U of shape (4, p), it is y = U.T @ sigmoid(W @ x).
  2. (b) With d=1, wk and x are plain numbers. Each term is σ(wkx)uk, and only σ(wkx) depends on x.
  3. The chain rule gives ddxσ(wkx)=σ′(wkx)⋅wk.
  4. So ∂y∂x=∑kσ(wkx)(1−σ(wkx))wkuk, a vector in ℝp.
  5. (c) Only branch 2 contains z2. The other three terms do not change when z2 changes, so their derivatives are 0.
  6. ∂y∂z2=∂α2∂z2u2=σ(z2)(1−σ(z2))u2, a vector in ℝp.
  7. Check with the example in the box above. There σ′(0)=0.25, so (b) gives ∑k0.25⋅0⋅uk=0, which makes sense because with all wk=0 the output does not depend on x.
Marks
(a) 2 for αk=σ(wk𝖳x), 2 for the sum, 2 for saying y∈ℝp. Correct code got full marks. (b) 2 for noticing the scalars, 2 for the chain rule, 2 for the σ′(wkx)wk factor, 1 for "vector in ℝp". (c) 2 for "only branch 2", 2 for σ′(z2), 2 for the u2, 1 for the dimension.

A kernel is κ(p,q)=ϕ(p)𝖳ϕ(q), and the kernel matrix of N samples is Kij=κ(xi,xj).

  1. Show that K is symmetric.
  2. Show that K is positive semi-definite.
  3. For κ(p,q)=(p𝖳q)3 with p,q∈ℝ2, find ϕ explicitly.
Write this in the exam

(b) Show that v𝖳Kv≥0 for every v∈ℝN.

  1. v𝖳Kv=∑i∑jvivjKij=∑i∑jvivjϕ(xi)𝖳ϕ(xj).
  2. =(∑iviϕ(xi))𝖳(∑jvjϕ(xj)).
    Why (2 steps)
    1. The numbers vi and vj can move inside the dot product, so each term is (viϕ(xi))𝖳(vjϕ(xj)).
    2. A dot product distributes over sums. Summing over i and j separately gives the dot product of the two sums.

Q3 · Backpropagation through a residual block (12 + 4 + 4)

A residual block computes y=g4(g3(g2(g1(x)))+x), where each gi is a function of one number with derivative gi′.

  1. Find ∂y∂x.
  2. In one line, how do residual connections help with vanishing gradients?
  3. How does ReLU help with vanishing gradients?
Write this in the exam

(a) Find ∂y∂x for y=g4(g3(g2(g1(x)))+x).

  1. Name the pieces. h1=g1(x), h2=g2(h1), h3=g3(h2), s=h3+x, y=g4(s).
  2. ∂y∂x=g4′(s)∂s∂x.
  3. ∂s∂x=∂h3∂x+1, and ∂h3∂x=g3′(h2)g2′(h1)g1′(x).
    Why (3 steps)
    1. s is a sum of two things that both depend on x, so its derivative is the sum of their derivatives.
    2. The skip path is x itself, whose derivative is 1.
    3. The deep path is three functions applied in turn, so the chain rule multiplies their three derivatives.
  4. So ∂y∂x=g4′(s)(g3′(h2)g2′(h1)g1′(x)+1).
End-sem 2026Part III Q3 · the +1 in a residual gradient, and why ReLU helps›
Formula
∂y∂x=g4′(s)(g3′g2′g1′+1)
Where it comes from
4.6 ResNet and skip connections and 3.5 Activations
Working
  1. (a) See the box above.
  2. (b) The skip connection adds a "+1" inside the bracket. So even if the product g3′g2′g1′ of the deep path shrinks towards 0, the gradient through the block is still about g4′(s).
  3. (c) ReLU⁡(z)=max⁡(0,z) has derivative 1 for z>0 and 0 for z<0.
  4. Backpropagation multiplies one derivative per layer. A sigmoid's derivative is at most 0.25, so ten sigmoid layers can shrink a gradient by 0.2510≈10−6. Along active ReLU units the factors are exactly 1, so nothing shrinks.
  5. The cost is that a unit stuck at z<0 passes no gradient at all (a "dead" ReLU).
  6. Check (a) with every gi(t)=t. Then y=2x, so the derivative is 2. The formula gives 1⋅(1⋅1⋅1+1)=2.
Marks
(a) 3 each for the addition node, the chain rule through g4, the "+1" and the product g3′g2′g1′. Forgetting the "+1" cost 4 or 5 marks, because it is the whole point of a residual block. (b) 4 for any line that mentions the added "+1", 3 for "a shortcut" without it. (c) 2 for "slope 1 when positive", 2 for linking it to sigmoid and tanh saturating, at least 3 if either is clear.

Q4 · A triangular mixture model (6 + 4 + 6 + 4)

Instead of Gaussians, model univariate data as a mixture of triangles. Each triangle is symmetric about its mean, has a fixed ratio of base to height, and gives probability zero outside its base.

  1. Write the density of one triangle with mean μ.
  2. Write out the EM algorithm for a GMM.
  3. Adapt it to the triangular mixture model (TMM).
  4. Compare the TMM with the GMM.
Reading the setup

The paper was ambiguous. Its figure showed height 1 and base 2, while its text said base to height is 1 to 2. The key accepted either, as long as the area is 1. A density has to have area 1, and a triangle's area is 12×base×height. Height 1 and base 2 gives 12⋅2⋅1=1. Base 1 and height 2 also gives 1. So both readings are valid densities.

Use the figure's version. The triangle peaks at μ with height 1 and falls in a straight line to 0 at μ±1. Distance |x−μ| from the peak lowers the height by the same amount.

Example. With μ=3, the density at 3 is 1, at 3.5 is 0.5, at 2.2 is 0.2, and at 4.5 is 0.

End-sem 2026Part III Q4 · a triangle density and EM for a triangle mixture›
Formula
T(x|μ)=max⁡(0, 1−|x−μ|). E-step γik=πkT(xi|μk)∑jπjT(xi|μj).
Where it comes from
2.7 GMMs and EM. The triangle itself is new, and is taught in the box above.
Working
  1. (a) T(x|μ)=1−|x−μ| when |x−μ|≤1, and 0 otherwise. The support is [μ−1,μ+1]. (Under the text's reading it is max⁡(0,2−4|x−μ|) on [μ−0.5,μ+0.5].)
  2. (b) GMM. Start with guesses for πk, μk and σk2.
  3. E-step. γik=πk𝒩(xi|μk,σk2)∑jπj𝒩(xi|μj,σj2), the responsibility of component k for point i.
  4. M-step. Nk=∑iγik, πk=Nk/N, μk=1Nk∑iγikxi, σk2=1Nk∑iγik(xi−μk)2.
  5. Repeat until the log-likelihood stops changing by more than a small tolerance.
  6. (c) TMM. Start with guesses for πk and μk. There is no width to learn, because the triangle's shape is fixed.
  7. E-step. Use the triangle in place of the Gaussian, γik=πkT(xi|μk)∑jπjT(xi|μj).
  8. If a point lies outside every triangle, the bottom is 0. Handle it by adding a small constant to T or by moving a mean towards that point.
  9. M-step. Nk=∑iγik, πk=Nk/N, μk=1Nk∑iγikxi. The weighted mean is the natural copy of the GMM step. The key notes that the exact maximum-likelihood update for a triangle is not a simple formula, and the weighted mean works well in practice.
  10. Repeat until the means and the log-likelihood stop changing.
  11. (d) For the TMM, it is cheaper (no exponentials or square roots), a component ignores points outside its base so there are no long tails, and each component has fewer parameters.
  12. Against the TMM, the density has a kink at the peak so the likelihood is not smooth, a point outside every base breaks the E-step, and a fixed width cannot fit clusters of different spreads.
  13. Check (a). The area is ∫μ−1μ+1(1−|x−μ|)dx. Each half is a triangle of base 1 and height 1, area 12, so the total is 1.
Marks
(a) 2 for using |x−μ|, 2 for the straight-line shape with area 1, 2 for the support. Forgetting "zero outside" got 4. (b) 1 for the E-step, 2 for the M-step, 1 for the stopping rule. (c) 2 for the E-step with the triangle, 2 for the M-step, 1 for noting the fixed width, 1 for convergence. Copying GMM with the triangle put in got full marks. (d) 2 for two advantages and 2 for two disadvantages. Any sensible points were accepted.

E.6What the marking rewarded

  • Multiple-correct questions were strict. One wrong tick gave zero. Leaving out an option you are unsure of costs at most half the marks, and a wrong tick costs all of them.
  • True or false needs no reason, unless one is asked for. Do not add a qualification you are not sure of, because a wrong one could cost half a mark.
  • Derivations and numbers were marked generously. The key says to give credit for a correct set-up and chain rule even when the arithmetic slips, and to carry a wrong number forward without punishing it twice. So always write the formula before you put numbers in.
  • Either sign convention was fine (for example o−y or y−o), as long as you used it consistently.
  • Both 1N and 12N versions of the MSE were accepted, and so were transposed matrix shapes.
  • Ambiguous questions were read kindly. The triangle question accepted either reading of the ratio, and the paper told students to make reasonable assumptions. State your assumption in one line and carry on.
  • Units and shapes earn marks. Several questions gave a mark just for saying the answer is a vector in ℝp, or for keeping the 32 channels after pooling.
